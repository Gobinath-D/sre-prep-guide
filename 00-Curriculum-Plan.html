From "I use these tools" to "I understand, build, break, fix, design, and explain the systems underneath."

A first-principles SRE and Platform Engineering textbook plus a build-break-fix laboratory manual, sequenced for someone who already ships on AWS/EKS, Kubernetes, Helm, Argo CD, Istio, and Prometheus, and now wants the internals, the packet-level reasoning, and Senior/Lead/Staff interview command.

This page is the plan only — not the textbook. Review it, tell me what to change, and I write the book one part at a time (starting with Linux), keeping this structure and cross-references intact.

0How this book works

Your learning loop drives every chapter: Understand → Build → Observe → Break → Troubleshoot → Fix → Explain → Design → Interview. Nothing is presented as a definition to memorize. Every concept starts from the problem it solves, then earns its complexity one layer at a time.

Textbook half

Prose that teaches the "why" and the internals — the reasoning a Staff SRE carries in their head. You can read a chapter cold, months from now, and rebuild the mental model.

Lab-manual half

Every major topic ships a five-lab arc. You build the smallest real thing, inspect what actually got created, break it on purpose, and investigate before any root cause is revealed.

Interview half

Each topic closes with a 30-second answer, a 2-minute answer, deep-dive and scenario questions, and the follow-ups an interviewer throws after your first answer.

Active recall is built in, not bolted on. Chapters stop and ask you: what happens next? which layer is failing? what command would you run? what evidence confirms it? — then give progressive hints, not the answer. The point is to grow the reflex, so an unfamiliar incident in an interview or at 3 a.m. is still solvable.

Who this assumes you are

You already deploy to EKS, write Helm charts, run Argo CD syncs, and read Grafana. So chapters skip the "what is a container" tour and go straight for the parts that experienced engineers usually hold as folklore: what a namespace actually is, why TIME_WAIT exists, what kube-proxy writes into iptables, how a pod gets AWS credentials without a secret. Where a fundamental gap would block the internals, the book fills it fast and moves on.

0.2Two frameworks used everywhere

These repeat so often that they get taught once, early, then referenced by name for the rest of the book. Learning them as reflexes is half of what separates a senior SRE from a mid-level one.

The troubleshooting loop

  1. SymptomState it precisely and measurably. "Slow" is not a symptom; "p99 doubled at 14:02 for /checkout" is.
  2. HypothesesList several plausible causes across layers before touching anything.
  3. Evidence & investigationCheapest, most decisive check first. Let data kill hypotheses.
  4. IsolationBisect the request path until the failing component is cornered.
  5. Root cause → fix → validationProve the cause, apply the smallest safe fix, confirm the symptom is gone.
  6. PreventionWhat guardrail, alert, or design change stops the rerun.

The system-design method

Never jump to a diagram. Every design in Part X walks the same 21 steps:

1 Clarify2 Functional3 Non-functional4 Scale est.5 Traffic6 Storage7 API8 High-level9 Data model10 DB choice11 Cache12 Queue13 Network14 Security15 Observability16 Failure17 Scale18 Availability19 DR20 Cost21 Trade-offs

Each component gets a written justification: why it is here, and what breaks without it.

4Lab progression

Local-first, as you chose. Every major topic runs the same five-lab arc, and the tooling you install grows exactly in step with the curriculum — nothing before you need it.

The five-lab arc (every major topic)

  1. BuildThe smallest working example. Not a toy — a real, minimal instance of the thing.
  2. UnderstandInspect what actually got created. Read the state, the process, the packet, the config.
  3. BreakIntroduce one specific failure on purpose.
  4. TroubleshootThe root cause is not revealed. You investigate with hints, then confirm.
  5. Production challengeRealistic constraints, and you design the fix or the guardrail.

Toolchain, introduced in order

Everything runs on your laptop. AWS/EKS appears only where the cloud behavior is the lesson — VPC CNI IP exhaustion, IRSA, ALB target groups, Route 53 failover — and each of those chapters also ships a local stand-in (kind + a mock or a diagram-driven walkthrough) so you're never blocked on an account or a bill.

How labs escalate across the book

Early labs are single-machine and fast: break a process, watch a TCP handshake, exhaust file descriptors. By Part IV they're cluster-scale on kind. By Part VII you're breaking a Helm release and recovering it through Argo CD. The capstone gauntlet (Ch 116) is the final form — multi-component failures where the symptom is three layers away from the cause, exactly like production.

5System-design progression

Architecture is taught as something that grows, never as a finished diagram handed down. Each project starts at the simplest correct design and is forced to evolve as requirements and scale increase — so you learn why each component appears, not just that it does.

Base
Client → Load balancer → App → Database — the four-box design every system starts as.
▼ traffic grows, reads dominate
+ Cache
Add caching — cache-aside, invalidation, stampede. Now failure modes multiply (Ch 85).
▼ writes spike, work gets slow
+ Async
Add queues & workers — decouple, backpressure, idempotency, retries with jitter (Ch 84, 98).
▼ one DB can't hold it
+ Scale data
Replication & sharding — read replicas, partitioning, quorum, consistency trade-offs (Ch 82, 86).
▼ one AZ / region isn't enough
+ Geo & DR
Multi-AZ → multi-region, CDN, DR — active-active vs active-passive, RPO/RTO, cost and latency (Ch 90, 95, 102).

The seven projects (Ch 96–102) are ordered by how much of this ladder they force you to climb: the URL shortener stops at caching and read-scaling; the multi-region SaaS climbs the whole thing. Each ends with a redesign pass where you critique your own architecture at the next order of magnitude.

7Beginner → Staff — the skill ladder

Where each part lands you, framed as what you can do and how you'd interview at that level. You're entering around the Intermediate rung with real gaps below and above it; the book closes both directions.

FoundationalParts I–III
Can: reason about processes, memory, files, and packets on a single machine; explain what a container is from the kernel up. Interview: answers Linux and networking fundamentals without hand-waving.
IntermediateParts IV–VI
Can: operate Kubernetes and EKS with an understanding of the control loop and the packet path; explain how a pod gets an IP and AWS credentials. Interview: handles "walk me through what happens when…" for the cluster and the cloud.
AdvancedParts VII–IX
Can: build and run the platform — GitOps, mesh, IaC, observability — and reason about distributed-systems and data trade-offs. Interview: defends design and reliability choices with evidence.
SeniorParts X–XI
Can: design systems that evolve with scale, and localize any production failure systematically. Interview: drives a system-design round and thinks out loud through an incident with a clear method.
Lead / StaffParts XII–XIII
Can: own an end-to-end platform, run incidents, and make + document architecture decisions; mentor and influence. Interview: passes the behavioral/leadership bar and turns the capstone into credible senior-scope stories.
