Processes, threads, scheduling & signals

One CPU, hundreds of programs, and every one of them thinks it owns the machine. The kernel keeps that illusion running by tracking each program, sharing the CPU fairly, and passing notes between them. When the illusion cracks, you get a zombie pileup, a process wedged in D, or one greedy job starving the box.

Start: Beginner→→→End: Staff-level ~40 min read · Linux lab (Docker)

By the end of this chapter you can

  • Read a process's state letter (R/S/D/Z/T) in ps and say exactly what the kernel is doing to it.
  • Describe how the scheduler shares one CPU fairly, what nice actually changes, and what a context switch costs.
  • Tell a process apart from a thread, and know what they share and what they don't.
  • Send and trap signals, and know which two signals can never be caught.
  • Diagnose the three classics: a zombie pileup, a process stuck in D, and one runaway job starving the rest.

Why this exists · the problemThe problem: one CPU, many programs

A CPU core does exactly one thing at a time. Yet right now your laptop is "running" a browser, a music player, a dozen background daemons, and your shell — hundreds of programs, on maybe eight cores. Nobody is waiting in line for their turn in any way you can feel. How?

The kernel fakes it, very fast. It runs one program for a few milliseconds, freezes it mid-instruction, saves everything about it, loads the next one, and runs that for a few milliseconds. Do this thousands of times a second and every program appears to run continuously. That trick has a name — time-sharing — and it forces the kernel to answer three questions constantly: what am I running, whose turn is it next, and how do these programs signal each other and me?

To answer the first, the kernel keeps a record for every running program — a process. To answer the second, it runs a scheduler that picks who gets the CPU. To answer the third, it delivers signals — tiny, fixed messages like "please stop" or "your child just died." Get these three right in your head and the whole zoo of ps output, load average, kill, and "why is this pod pegged at 100%" stops being folklore.

Key point

A process is not a program. The program is the file on disk (/bin/ls). A process is a running instance of it — with its own memory, its own open files, its own place in the scheduler's queue. Run ls twice at once and you have one program and two processes.

Mental model · analogyThe mental model: a shared workshop

Carry this picture through the chapter.

Real-world analogy

Imagine a workshop with a single workbench — that's your CPU core. Many workers (processes) all need the bench, but only one can use it at a time. A supervisor (the scheduler) rotates them: you get the bench for a short slice, then step aside so the next worker gets a turn. To keep it fair, the supervisor keeps a ledger of how much bench time each worker has already used, and always calls up whoever has used the least.

A new worker doesn't appear from nowhere. An existing worker clones themselves (that's fork), and the clone usually immediately swaps their toolbox and instructions for a completely different job (exec). Some workers step away from the bench to wait for a delivery — a phone call, a part from the store — and doze in the break room until it arrives (sleeping). One kind of wait is nastier: a worker signs for a delivery and is frozen at the loading dock until the truck shows, unable to even hear the fire alarm (that's the dreaded D state).

The workshop has a PA system for short, fixed announcements: "worker 42, phone call," "please pack up and leave" (SIGTERM), or the one nobody can ignore — the evacuation order (SIGKILL). And when a worker finishes, they don't just vanish: they leave a completed timecard that their manager (the parent process) must collect. A finished worker whose timecard hasn't been collected yet is a zombie — done working, but still on the roster.

ArchitectureThe process, its states, and the run queue

Everything the kernel knows about a process lives in one big struct — the process control block. In Linux it's called task_struct, and there's one per task. It holds the process ID, the parent's ID, the current state, the register snapshot from the last time it was paused, pointers to its memory map and its open-file table, its scheduling weight, and its set of pending signals. When people say "the kernel context-switches," they mean it saves the CPU registers into one task_struct and restores them from another.

A task is always in exactly one state, and ps prints that state as a single letter. These five are the ones you'll read every day:

  • R — running or runnable. Either on the CPU right now, or sitting on the run queue ready to go the instant the scheduler picks it. ps shows R for both.
  • S — interruptible sleep. Waiting for something (a timer, a socket, a keypress) and can be woken early by a signal. Most processes on a healthy box are in S almost all the time.
  • D — uninterruptible sleep. Blocked inside a syscall, usually on I/O, and will not respond to signals — not even SIGKILL. A process wedged in D is the one that ruins your afternoon.
  • Z — zombie (defunct). Finished executing, but its exit status hasn't been collected by the parent yet. It holds no memory or CPU — just a slot in the process table.
  • T — stopped. Suspended by job control or a debugger (SIGSTOP/SIGTSTP), frozen until SIGCONT.

The run queue is where the "whose turn is next" decision happens. Each CPU has its own run queue holding the runnable (R) tasks. Sleeping tasks (S, D) aren't on it — they sit on wait queues attached to whatever they're waiting for, and get moved back onto a run queue when that event fires. So a busy-looking ps with fifty processes might have only two on a run queue and forty-eight asleep. Load average, which people misread constantly, counts the R and D tasks — which is why heavy disk I/O drives load average up even when CPUs are idle.

Why an SRE cares

Every alert you'll ever get about "high load" or "CPU pegged" or "the app is hung" resolves to a question about states and the run queue. Is load high because forty threads are genuinely runnable and fighting for eight cores (a real CPU problem), or because ten threads are stuck in D waiting on a slow EBS volume (a storage problem wearing a CPU costume)? Same load number, completely different fix. This chapter is how you tell them apart in thirty seconds.

Internal workingHow a process is born, run, and reaped

The lifecycle has three machines you should be able to narrate: creation, scheduling, and teardown.

Creation: fork, then exec

Unix creates processes with two separate calls, and the separation is the clever part.

  1. fork() clones the caller. The kernel makes a near-identical copy of the calling process: same memory, same open file descriptors, same code. The original is the parent, the copy is the child. fork() returns twice — the child sees 0, the parent sees the child's PID — which is how each figures out who it is.
  2. The copy is cheap, thanks to copy-on-write. The kernel doesn't duplicate the parent's memory up front. Parent and child share the same physical pages, marked read-only; only when one of them writes a page does the kernel copy that page. So forking a 2 GB process costs kilobytes, not gigabytes — until something is written.
  3. execve() replaces the program. The child usually immediately calls execve("/bin/ls", …), which throws away the copied memory image and loads a completely different program into the same process. The PID stays the same; the contents are new. This is why it's two calls: fork gives you a new process you control, and in the gap before exec you can redirect its file descriptors (that's exactly how a shell wires up ls > out.txt).
  4. The scheduler takes over. The new task is placed on a run queue in state R, and from here it's just another mouth to feed.

Modern glibc actually implements fork on top of a more general call, clone(), which takes flags for exactly what to share. fork shares almost nothing (separate memory); creating a thread uses the same clone() with flags that say "share the address space, the file descriptors, and the signal handlers." That single difference — shared address space or not — is the entire distinction between a thread and a process.

Scheduling: sharing the CPU fairly

For over fifteen years Linux's default scheduler was CFS, the Completely Fair Scheduler. Its idea is simple and worth internalising: give every runnable task a virtual runtime (vruntime) that ticks up as it runs, and always run the task with the lowest vruntime. Tasks that have run a lot fall to the back; tasks that just woke up have low vruntime and get the CPU quickly. CFS keeps the runnable tasks in a red-black tree ordered by vruntime and picks the leftmost node — an O(log n) decision it makes thousands of times a second.

nice tilts this. A task's vruntime advances in proportion to real time divided by its weight, and weight comes from the nice value. Nice ranges from -20 (greediest) to +19 (most generous), and each step is roughly a 1.25× change in CPU share. So a nice -5 task next to a nice 0 task gets a bigger slice — its vruntime rises more slowly, so the scheduler keeps picking it. Nice doesn't set a hard percentage; it sets a ratio against whoever else is competing.

CFS, EEVDF, and not getting caught out

As of kernel 6.6 (late 2023) the default fair scheduler is EEVDF (Earliest Eligible Virtual Deadline First), which replaced CFS. The mental model barely changes: it still tracks per-task virtual time and fairness, but it adds a notion of a virtual deadline so latency-sensitive tasks get served promptly. The concepts here — fairness, vruntime, nice as a weight — carry over, and most production hosts you touch today still run kernels with CFS. If an interviewer says "CFS," answer in CFS terms and mention EEVDF is the current successor. Neither is a real-time scheduler; for hard timing you use the separate SCHED_FIFO/SCHED_RR classes.

A context switch is what happens between two tasks: the kernel saves the outgoing task's registers into its task_struct, restores the incoming task's registers, and — if the incoming task is a different process — switches the page tables to its address space. That last step can flush the TLB (the cache of virtual-to-physical mappings), which is why switching between two threads of one process is cheaper than switching between two processes: same address space, no TLB flush. A switch is on the order of a microsecond, but a box doing hundreds of thousands per second is spending real CPU just shuffling tasks — a pattern you'll spot in the labs.

Teardown: exit, zombie, reap

When a process calls exit() (or returns from main, or is killed), the kernel tears down its memory and closes its files — but it can't fully remove the process yet, because the exit status (did it succeed? was it killed, and by what signal?) has to survive until the parent asks for it. So the kernel keeps a husk: the zombie. It's a nearly empty task_struct holding just the exit status and a PID.

The parent collects it by calling wait() or waitpid() — "reaping." Reaping frees the last of the process and the PID goes back into circulation. If the parent never reaps, the zombie lingers forever, holding a PID. Pile up enough of them and you exhaust the PID space (default max is in /proc/sys/kernel/pid_max, often 4 million on modern kernels but historically 32768) and the box can't fork anything new — including your shell to log in and fix it.

What if the parent dies first? The child becomes an orphan, and the kernel reparents it to PID 1 (init, or systemd). PID 1's job includes reaping orphans, so on a normal host orphans get cleaned up automatically. This is exactly why zombies famously accumulate in containers: the container's PID 1 is often the application itself, which never expects to be a reaper and ignores SIGCHLD — so adopted children pile up as zombies. The fix is a tiny init like tini (that's what docker run --init gives you), which we'll meet again in Chapter 26.

Think first: why does fork() return two different values in what looks like one function call?

Because after fork() there are two processes, and each returns from the call independently. The kernel arranges the child to see 0 and the parent to see the child's PID. There's no magic — both processes resume at the instruction after fork(), each with its own copy of everything, each with a different value sitting in the return register. The idiom if (fork()==0) { …child… } else { …parent… } is just each process reading its own return value and taking the branch meant for it.

ComponentsThe pieces, and what each owns

  • task_struct — the process control block. One per task. Owns the PID/PPID, state, register snapshot, memory map pointer, open-file table, scheduling weight and vruntime, and the signal state. Everything else here is a field or a list hanging off this.
  • The scheduler and run queues. One run queue per CPU, holding the runnable (R) tasks. The scheduler picks the next task and performs context switches. Sleeping tasks live on wait queues, not the run queue.
  • Threads vs processes. A process is one or more threads sharing an address space. Each thread is a schedulable task with its own TID (thread ID); the shared identifier is the TGID, which is what getpid() returns and what you see as "the PID." Threads share memory, file descriptors, and the working directory; they do not share the CPU register set or the stack. ps -eLf shows one line per thread (the LWP column).
  • Signals. A fixed set of numbered messages (kill -l lists them). Each has a default action — terminate, terminate-and-dump-core, ignore, stop, or continue — and a process can install a handler to run its own code instead, or block a signal to defer it. Two are special: SIGKILL (9) and SIGSTOP (19) can never be caught, blocked, or ignored, because they're the kernel's guaranteed lever over any process.
  • PID 1 (init / systemd). The ancestor of everything, started by the kernel at boot. Adopts orphans and reaps them. In a container, whatever you set as the entrypoint is PID 1, with all of PID 1's reaping responsibilities whether it knows it or not.
  • /proc/<pid>/. The live window into a task's task_struct: status (state, PPid, thread count, context-switch counts), stat, wchan (what it's sleeping on), fd/ (open descriptors), and task/ (its threads).

Data flowFollowing one command from prompt to reap

Type ls at a bash prompt and press Enter. Here's the whole life of that command:

  1. Bash parses the line and calls fork(). Now there are two bashes — the parent (your shell) and a child clone.
  2. The child (still bash) sets up any redirections, then calls execve("/bin/ls", …). Its memory image is replaced by ls. Same PID, new program.
  3. The kernel puts the new task on a run queue in state R. The scheduler gives it the CPU when its vruntime comes up.
  4. ls runs — mostly it's blocked in S waiting on getdents/write syscalls, occasionally R when it has work. It prints the directory and calls exit().
  5. ls becomes a zombie (Z) holding its exit status. Meanwhile the parent bash was blocked in wait() the whole time (state S) — that's why your prompt didn't come back yet.
  6. The kernel delivers SIGCHLD to bash; bash's wait() returns the child's exit status; the zombie is reaped and its PID freed. Bash prints the next prompt.

That six-step dance happens every single time you run a command. When you background a job with &, the only change is that bash doesn't block in wait() — it prints the prompt immediately and reaps the child later when SIGCHLD arrives.

Lab 1Build — see processes, threads, and statesBuild

Start a couple of processes and read the process table. ps with an explicit format is worth memorising — the defaults hide the columns you actually need.

bash
sleep 300 &                    # a process that just sleeps (state S)
ps -eo pid,ppid,stat,ni,wchan:16,comm
Output (trimmed) PID PPID STAT NI WCHAN COMMAND 1 0 Ss 0 do_wait bash 14 1 S 0 hrtimer_nanosle sleep 15 1 R+ 0 - ps

Read the STAT column. bash is Ss — sleeping (S) and a session leader (s). sleep is S, parked in hrtimer_nanosleep — the WCHAN tells you the exact kernel function it's blocked in. ps itself is R+ — running, in the foreground (+). Now look at threads. A single process can hold many; ps -eLf shows one row per thread:

bash
# A multi-threaded stand-in: 4 CPU workers in ONE process.
stress-ng --cpu 4 --timeout 60s &
ps -o pid,tid,nlwp,comm -L -p $(pgrep -n stress-ng)
Output PID TID NLWP COMMAND 30 30 5 stress-ng-cpu 30 31 5 stress-ng-cpu 30 32 5 stress-ng-cpu 30 33 5 stress-ng-cpu 30 34 5 stress-ng-cpu

One PID (30), five TIDs (30–34): the main thread plus four workers. They share the PID and the address space; each is scheduled independently. That's the whole thread story on one screen. Look at its live state in /proc:

bash
grep -E 'State|Threads|VmRSS' /proc/$(pgrep -n stress-ng)/status
OutputState: R (running) Threads: 5 VmRSS: 4210 kB
Lab 2Understand — signals, handlers, and the two you can't catchUnderstand

Signals are how you (and the kernel) talk to a running process. First, list them, then trap one in bash and prove that SIGKILL ignores your handler.

bash
kill -l | tr ' ' '\n' | head       # the signal names/numbers

# A script that catches SIGTERM(15) and SIGINT(2) but not SIGKILL(9):
cat > /tmp/catcher.sh <<'EOF'
trap 'echo "caught SIGTERM — cleaning up, ignoring the request"' TERM
trap 'echo "caught SIGINT (Ctrl-C)"' INT
echo "pid $$ running; try to kill me"
while true; do sleep 1; done
EOF
bash /tmp/catcher.sh &
PID=$!

Now send it signals and watch what it does:

bash
kill -TERM $PID     # delivers SIGTERM -> handler runs, process SURVIVES
kill -TERM $PID     # again — still alive, still trapped
kill -KILL $PID     # SIGKILL(9) -> no handler possible, process dies now
Outputcaught SIGTERM — cleaning up, ignoring the request caught SIGTERM — cleaning up, ignoring the request [1]+ Killed bash /tmp/catcher.sh

This is the exact reason kill defaults to SIGTERM, not SIGKILL: SIGTERM is a polite "please shut down" that a well-behaved program traps to flush buffers and close connections cleanly. SIGKILL is the kernel yanking the plug — no cleanup, no handler. This is also why Kubernetes sends SIGTERM first and only escalates to SIGKILL after the grace period; your app should catch SIGTERM and drain. Try stopping and continuing a process, too:

bash
sleep 300 & P=$!
kill -STOP $P ; ps -o pid,stat,comm -p $P   # STAT shows T (stopped)
kill -CONT $P ; ps -o pid,stat,comm -p $P   # back to S
Think first: your app "ignores" SIGTERM from kill, so you reach for kill -9. What might you be breaking, and what should you check first?

kill -9 (SIGKILL) gives the process zero chance to flush writes, commit a transaction, or deregister from a load balancer — you can corrupt state or drop in-flight requests. Before escalating, check whether the process is actually ignoring the signal or is simply stuck in D state, where no signal (not even KILL) is delivered until the I/O completes. Run ps -o pid,stat,wchan,comm -p <pid>. If STAT is D, kill -9 won't work either — the problem is downstream I/O, and you have to fix that. If STAT is S/R and it truly ignores SIGTERM, that's an application bug: it either installed a no-op handler or is wedged in its own signal-handling code. Reaching for -9 reflexively hides both of those.

Lab 3Break — a zombie pileupBreak

Zombies happen when a parent forks children that exit but never calls wait(). Manufacture 200 of them, then investigate as if you found them in production.

bash
# Parent forks 200 children that exit immediately, then sleeps
# WITHOUT ever reaping them. Each dead child becomes a zombie.
python3 -c '
import os, time
for _ in range(200):
    if os.fork() == 0:
        os._exit(0)      # child exits right away
time.sleep(600)          # parent never calls os.wait()
' &
sleep 1

Now look. Don't assume — count and classify what you see:

bash
ps -eo pid,ppid,stat,comm | awk '$3 ~ /Z/'   | head
ps -eo stat | grep -c '^Z'                    # how many zombies?
Output 47 31 Z python3 <defunct> 48 31 Z python3 <defunct> 49 31 Z python3 <defunct> ... 200

Two hundred processes marked Z and <defunct>, all with the same PPID (31). That shared parent is the whole story. Before reading the cause, answer for yourself: they show up in ps — are they using CPU or RAM? And how do you clear them?

Reveal the cause and the fix

Zombies use no CPU and no memory — they're finished. Each holds exactly one thing: a PID slot. So the damage isn't resource starvation, it's PID exhaustion. Enough of them and the kernel can't fork anything new (you'll see "resource temporarily unavailable" / EAGAIN), and you can't even start a shell to fix it.

You cannot kill a zombie — it's already dead; kill -9 on a zombie does nothing. The only cure is to make the parent reap them, or kill the parent so the zombies are reparented to PID 1, which reaps immediately:

bash
PARENT=$(ps -eo ppid,stat | awk '$2 ~ /Z/ {print $1; exit}')
kill "$PARENT"          # parent dies; zombies reparent to PID 1 and vanish
ps -eo stat | grep -c '^Z'

In the real world the parent is your app, and the fix is in code: reap children (call wait(), or handle SIGCHLD), or run under a proper init. In containers this is the classic "why do I have thousands of zombies" — the app is PID 1 and doesn't reap. docker run --init (or a real init in the image) fixes it; see Chapter 26.

Lab 4Troubleshoot — a process stuck in DTroubleshoot

The D state is uninterruptible sleep: a task blocked inside the kernel on I/O that won't return signals until the I/O finishes. Force some heavy synchronous disk I/O and catch a worker in D. In a container disk can be fast, so sample quickly in a loop.

bash
# stress-ng --hdd hammers the disk with synchronous writes.
stress-ng --hdd 4 --hdd-bytes 512m --timeout 40s &

# Sample fast and print only tasks currently in D:
for i in $(seq 1 60); do
  ps -eo pid,stat,wchan:20,comm | awk '$2 ~ /D/'
  sleep 0.3
done | sort -u
Output (varies) 92 D folio_wait_bit_common stress-ng-hdd 93 D jbd2_log_wait_commit stress-ng-hdd 94 D balance_dirty_pages stress-ng-hdd

Those WCHAN values are the kernel functions the tasks are blocked in — waiting on page writeback and the filesystem journal. Now the senior move: given a task in D, what is it waiting on, and can you kill it? Investigate before revealing.

bash
PID=<a D-state pid from above>
cat /proc/$PID/wchan; echo          # the blocking kernel function
sudo cat /proc/$PID/stack 2>/dev/null || cat /proc/$PID/stack   # full kernel stack
kill -9 $PID; ps -o pid,stat,comm -p $PID   # does SIGKILL even work?
Reveal what's happening and how to handle it

A task in D is blocked in the kernel and does not receive signals — so kill -9 is queued but not delivered until the I/O completes. If the underlying storage answers (as our local disk does), the task leaves D in milliseconds and everything's fine. The nightmare version is when the storage never answers — a hung NFS mount, a detached EBS volume, a failing disk. Then the task is stuck in D indefinitely, unkillable, and it counts toward load average, so you see load climb to 20 with the CPUs idle.

Your job when you find a task wedged in D: don't try to kill it (you can't) and don't blame the app. Read /proc/<pid>/stack and wchan to see the I/O path, then find and fix the storage — remount the NFS share, reattach the volume, replace the disk. Sometimes the only way out is a reboot, because the task can't be reaped while the I/O is outstanding. This is why "load average is 30 but CPU is 2%" is almost always a storage incident, not a compute one.

Lab 5Production challenge — one job starving the restSenior challenge

A batch job shares a box with your latency-sensitive service, and users start seeing slow responses. The batch job isn't "broken" — it's just greedy, and the scheduler is being fair to a fault. Reproduce the shape: saturate every core, then watch a would-be interactive task struggle.

bash
NPROC=$(nproc)
stress-ng --cpu "$NPROC" --timeout 120s &    # the greedy "batch job"
HOG=$!

# A stand-in for your latency task: time how long a fixed unit of work takes.
time (for i in $(seq 1 3); do sha256sum /usr/bin/* >/dev/null 2>&1; done)

With every core pegged, that fixed work takes far longer than on an idle box — your "service" just got slow because the scheduler is splitting the CPU evenly between it and a job you care about less. The wrong fix is "add CPUs." The right question: how do you make the batch job yield to the important one, without killing it? Work it out, then reveal.

Reveal the investigation and the durable fix

First confirm it's a CPU-contention problem and find the hog:

bash
uptime                              # load avg >> nproc, and it's all R (not D)
ps -eo pid,ni,pri,pcpu,stat,comm --sort=-pcpu | head

Everything runnable, no D — so it's genuine CPU contention, not I/O. The lightweight lever is nice: make the batch job more generous so the scheduler weights it lower.

bash
renice +19 -p "$HOG"      # batch job now yields; re-run the timed work

Re-run the timed loop and it finishes much closer to idle-box speed, because a nice +19 task gets a small fraction of the CPU whenever anything else wants it. But nice is advisory and global; the durable, enforceable fix on a shared box or in Kubernetes is a cgroup v2 CPU weight or quota — cpu.weight for proportional share, cpu.max for a hard cap. That's exactly what a Kubernetes CPU request/limit compiles down to. We build cgroups from scratch in Chapter 23; the point here is that the scheduler's fairness is per-task, and the only way to say "this whole job matters less than that one" is nice or, better, cgroups.

Failure & troubleshootingHow this shows up in real incidents

Three incident shapes come straight out of this chapter. Learn them as reflexes.

Incident pattern · zombie accumulation → can't fork

Symptom: new processes fail to start (fork: retry: Resource temporarily unavailable / EAGAIN); sometimes you can't even SSH in because login can't fork a shell. First cut: ps -eo stat | grep -c '^Z' and check cat /proc/sys/kernel/pid_max against total process count. Confirm: the zombies share one PPID — ps -eo ppid,stat | awk '$2~/Z/' — that parent is the non-reaper. Usual cause: an app that forks children (or an entrypoint that's PID 1 in a container) and never reaps them. Fix: make the parent reap (wait/handle SIGCHLD) or run a real init (--init/tini). Killing zombies directly does nothing; kill or fix the parent.

Incident pattern · D-state from stuck I/O

Symptom: load average climbs to 15–30 while CPU utilisation sits near idle; processes are unkillable, even with -9. First cut: ps -eo pid,stat,wchan,comm | grep ' D' — count the D tasks and read their wchan. Confirm: cat /proc/<pid>/stack shows a filesystem/network-storage path (NFS, EBS, a specific block device). Usual cause: storage that stopped answering — a hung NFS server, a detached or throttled EBS volume, a dying disk. Fix: repair the storage, not the process. Remember load average counts D, so this looks like a CPU emergency and isn't one.

Incident pattern · a runaway process starving others

Symptom: everything on the box is slow, load is well above core count, and one process sits near 100% (or N×100% if multithreaded). First cut: top — is it high %us (its own code) and are tasks R, not D? ps --sort=-pcpu to name the hog. Confirm: nothing in D, load ≈ number of busy runnable tasks. Usual cause: an infinite loop, a runaway query, a missing backoff, or just a legitimate batch job co-scheduled with a latency service. Fix: renice to yield immediately; enforce durably with cgroup cpu.weight/cpu.max (K8s CPU limits). Only add capacity once you've ruled out a bug.

Production · security · performanceWhat matters in production

Signals are your shutdown contract. Orchestrators (systemd, Kubernetes, Docker) all follow the same drill: send SIGTERM, wait a grace period, then SIGKILL. If your service doesn't trap SIGTERM to drain connections and flush state, every deploy and every autoscale event drops in-flight requests. Kubernetes' default grace period is 30 seconds — a number worth knowing, because a slow drain that exceeds it gets the hard kill anyway. Write the handler.

Reaping is not optional. Any process that spawns children — a supervisor, a language runtime doing multiprocessing, a shell script with background jobs — must reap them or delegate to an init that does. This bites hardest in containers, where a naïve entrypoint becomes PID 1. Use --init, or an image with a real init, or handle SIGCHLD yourself.

Fairness is per-task, and that cuts both ways. The scheduler will happily give a runaway thread its fair slice while your users wait. On multi-tenant boxes and in Kubernetes, express priority as cgroup weights and quotas, not hope. And watch context-switch rate: a box doing 500k+ switches/second (see vmstat 1's cs column, or /proc/<pid>/status voluntary vs nonvoluntary counts) is thrashing — too many runnable threads for the cores, or lock contention forcing constant voluntary yields. More threads is not more throughput past the core count.

Common mistakes: reaching for kill -9 when the process is actually in D (it won't work, and it hides a storage fault); reading load average as a CPU metric when it also counts D; assuming threads scale past core count; and running a container without an init and then wondering where the zombies came from.

System-design connectionWhere this reaches into architecture

The cost of context switches sets a real ceiling: past a few thousand threads on a box, you spend more CPU switching than working, which is precisely why high-concurrency servers (nginx, Envoy, Go's runtime) use a small pool of threads driving an event loop or lightweight goroutines instead of one OS thread per connection. When you design a service for 50k concurrent connections, you're really designing around this chapter.

"Send SIGTERM, drain, then SIGKILL" is the seed of graceful shutdown and connection draining behind every rolling deploy and blue/green cutover (Chapter 73). The "blocked, not dead" reframe from D-state and sleeping processes is the origin of timeouts, health checks, and circuit breakers — in a distributed system most of your processes are asleep in a syscall waiting on someone else, so reliability engineering (Chapter 88) is largely the art of not waiting forever. And scheduler fairness plus cgroup weights is the machinery under Kubernetes requests and limits and node-level noisy-neighbour isolation.

InterviewInterview questions & answers

Answer each in your head before opening it.

30-secWhat's the difference between a process and a thread?

A process is a running program with its own private address space, its own open-file table, and its own PID. A thread is a unit of execution inside a process; threads of the same process share the address space, the file descriptors, and the working directory, but each has its own stack and registers. In Linux both are tasks created by clone() — the only real difference is which resources they share. Threads share memory (so communication is cheap but you need locks); processes are isolated (safer, but they must pass data through pipes, sockets, or shared memory).

2-minWalk me through what happens from fork() to a reaped child.

A process calls fork(), and the kernel makes a copy of it — same memory (via copy-on-write, so it's cheap), same open files. fork returns twice: the child sees 0, the parent sees the child's PID. The child usually calls execve() immediately, which replaces its memory image with a new program while keeping the same PID; the gap between fork and exec is where a shell sets up redirections. The kernel puts the new task on a run queue in state R, and the scheduler runs it when its vruntime comes up. It runs, blocking in S while it waits on I/O, until it calls exit(). At that point it becomes a zombie (Z) holding just its exit status, and the kernel sends SIGCHLD to the parent. The parent calls wait()/waitpid(), collects the status, and the zombie is reaped — its PID freed. If the parent died first, the child was already reparented to PID 1, which reaps it. The headline: two calls for creation because you want a controllable process before you load the new program, and a mandatory reap so the exit status survives until someone reads it.

DeepHow does CFS share one CPU fairly, and what does nice actually change?

CFS gives each runnable task a virtual runtime, vruntime, that advances as the task runs, and it always schedules the task with the smallest vruntime — kept in a red-black tree so picking the leftmost node is O(log n). A task that has run a lot has high vruntime and drifts to the back; a task that just woke has low vruntime and gets the CPU quickly, which is what makes interactive work feel responsive. nice changes the task's weight: vruntime advances as real-time-run divided by weight, so a low-nice (high-priority) task's vruntime rises slowly and the scheduler keeps picking it. Each nice step is about a 1.25× change in CPU share, and it's a ratio against whatever else is competing, not a fixed percentage. So nice doesn't reserve CPU — it changes how the pie is split when there's contention. As of kernel 6.6 the default is EEVDF, which keeps this fairness model but adds a virtual deadline so latency-sensitive tasks are served sooner; the vocabulary an interviewer expects is still CFS/vruntime/weight. For hard real-time guarantees you'd leave the fair class entirely for SCHED_FIFO/RR.

ScenarioLoad average is 25 on an 8-core box, but CPU utilisation is 5%. What's going on and how do you confirm?

Load average counts both runnable (R) and uninterruptible (D) tasks, so a high load with idle CPUs almost always means tasks piled up in D — blocked on I/O that isn't completing. I'd run ps -eo pid,stat,wchan,comm | grep ' D' to count the D tasks and read what they're blocked in, then cat /proc/<pid>/stack to see the exact I/O path. Nine times out of ten the wchan/stack points at network storage — a hung NFS mount or a stalled/detached EBS volume — or a failing local disk. The fix is on the storage side, not the processes; those tasks are unkillable while the I/O is outstanding, so kill -9 is pointless. I'd verify by checking dmesg for I/O errors and iostat/mount for the culprit device. The trap I'd avoid is treating this as a CPU emergency and adding compute — it's a storage incident.

Follow-upWhy do containers accumulate zombies, and how do you stop it?

On a normal host, orphaned children are reparented to PID 1 (init/systemd), whose job includes reaping, so zombies get cleaned up. In a container, PID 1 is whatever you set as the entrypoint — usually the application, which never expected to be an init and doesn't reap SIGCHLD. So any grandchild processes it spawns that outlive their immediate parent get reparented to the app-as-PID-1 and sit there as zombies, slowly eating PID slots. The fix is to give the container a real init as PID 1: docker run --init (which injects tini), a tini/dumb-init entrypoint in the image, or an app that installs a SIGCHLD handler and reaps. It's the same underlying rule as on a host — someone has to call wait() — just easy to forget when your app is unexpectedly PID 1.

RetainQuick revision

A process is a running instance of a program, tracked by the kernel in a task_struct (the PCB) and always in one state: R (runnable/running), S (interruptible sleep), D (uninterruptible sleep — deaf to signals), Z (zombie — exited, awaiting reap), or T (stopped). Processes are born by fork() (a copy-on-write clone) then execve() (replace the program); threads are the same clone() mechanism sharing the address space. The scheduler shares one CPU by running the task with the lowest vruntime, and nice tilts that share by changing weight — CFS historically, EEVDF since kernel 6.6. Signals are fixed messages: catch SIGTERM to shut down cleanly; SIGKILL and SIGSTOP can never be caught. Load average counts R and D, so high load with idle CPUs is a storage problem. And every dead child must be reaped, or you get a zombie pileup — the container classic.

The 10 things to remember

  1. A process is a running instance with its own memory and files; a program is the file on disk.
  2. Creation is two calls: fork() clones (copy-on-write, cheap), execve() replaces the program in place.
  3. A thread is clone() sharing the address space and file descriptors; same PID/TGID, different TID.
  4. States: R runnable, S interruptible sleep, D uninterruptible sleep, Z zombie, T stopped — read them in ps's STAT.
  5. The scheduler runs the lowest-vruntime task; nice (−20…+19) changes weight, i.e. the CPU ratio under contention.
  6. A context switch saves/restores registers and switches page tables; thread-to-thread is cheaper (no TLB flush).
  7. Signals are fixed messages; catch SIGTERM to drain and clean up — SIGKILL(9) and SIGSTOP(19) can't be caught.
  8. A task in D ignores every signal, including -9; fix the underlying I/O, don't try to kill it.
  9. Load average counts R and D — high load with idle CPUs means blocked I/O, not a CPU shortage.
  10. Every child must be reaped via wait(); unreaped children become zombies — run a real init as PID 1 in containers.
