Part II · Networking from First Principles · Chapter 11

L2: Ethernet, MAC, ARP, switching

Chapter 10 left a packet holding a destination IP and a question: which box on this wire do I physically hand it to? Layer 2 is the answer — the hardware handoff on one segment. Get it, and stale-ARP blackholes and duplicate-IP mysteries stop being magic.

Start: Beginner→→→End: Staff-level ~45 min read · Linux lab (Docker)

By the end of this chapter you can

  • Explain what a MAC address is, how it differs from an IP, and why one is hop-local and the other end-to-end.
  • Trace an ARP request and reply, and read the Linux neighbour table and its states.
  • Describe how a switch learns MAC-to-port mappings and when it floods versus forwards.
  • Diagnose a stale-ARP blackhole and a duplicate-IP conflict from the symptoms alone.
  • Explain why big flat L2 networks don't scale, and how gratuitous ARP moves a VIP during failover.

Why this exists · the problemThe problem: the last-metre handoff

Layer 3 gives you a plan — "send this packet toward 10.0.0.2" — but a plan isn't delivery. At some point the packet has to become electrical signals on an actual wire, handed to an actual network card. And here's the catch: the machines on a local network segment don't know each other by IP address at all. IP is a software idea, assigned and reassigned by administrators. The network card only knows its own burned-in hardware address and how to send a frame to another hardware address on the same wire. So there's a gap: you have an IP, you need the hardware address that currently owns it, and nobody handed you a phone book.

On top of that, a wire is shared. Twenty machines on one segment can't all shout at once and expect anyone to hear. Early networks (hubs) really did just copy every signal to every port — a shared party line where everyone heard everything, which wasted bandwidth and leaked traffic to machines it wasn't meant for. Something had to learn who sits where and deliver each frame only to the port that needs it.

Layer 2 solves both problems. ARP is the phone book: given an IP on your segment, it finds the hardware address that answers to it, by asking everyone at once and letting the owner reply. The switch is the smart delivery: it watches which hardware address lives behind which port and forwards each frame only where it belongs. Together they turn "I have an IP and a shared wire" into "the right box got the frame." Every packet you'll ever send crosses this layer on its first metre and its last — and when it goes wrong, it goes wrong in ways that look, deceptively, like higher-layer problems.

Key point

IP routing decides the next hop; L2 delivers the frame to that next hop. Every hop in a route is really "resolve the next box's hardware address, then hand it a frame." ARP does the resolving; the switch does the handing. A fault in either drops traffic that looks perfectly correct at L3.

Mental model · analogyThe mental model: name badges in a big room

Picture a large open-plan office full of people, all in one room.

Real-world analogy

Everyone wears a permanent name badge printed at the factory — that's the MAC address, burned into the network card and globally unique. Separately, everyone has a desk number that gets reassigned as people move teams — that's the IP address, assigned by admins. You've been told to deliver a note to whoever is at desk 10.0.0.2, but you only know the desk, not the face.

So you stand up and shout to the whole room: "Who is at desk 10.0.0.2? Tell me your name badge!" Everyone hears it (it's a broadcast), but only the person at that desk answers, quietly and directly to you: "That's me — badge bb:bb:bb." That call-and-answer is ARP, and you write the desk→badge mapping on a sticky note so you don't have to shout next time — your ARP cache. Now the note itself goes to a mail clerk in the middle of the room — the switch — who has quietly learned, by watching who sends mail, which door each badge sits behind. The clerk hands your note straight to bb:bb:bb's desk and nobody else's. If the clerk hasn't seen a badge yet, it hedges by slipping a copy under every door (flooding) until it learns. And "the whole room that can hear your shout" is the broadcast domain — make the room too big and the constant shouting drowns out real work.

ArchitectureARP resolution, then frame delivery

Here's the whole L2 story on one LAN: Host A wants to reach the IP 10.0.0.2, discovers it belongs to Host B via ARP, and the switch delivers the frame to B's port and no one else's. Watch the three steps and the switch's table filling up.

Notice the asymmetry that defines L2. The request is a broadcast — one-to-everyone — because A has no idea who owns that IP yet, so it has to ask the whole room. The reply is a unicast — B knows exactly who asked. And the switch turns the initially-dumb medium into targeted delivery by learning: every frame's source MAC teaches it "this address lives behind this port," so over time it forwards precisely instead of flooding. Broadcasts and unknown addresses are the only things it still floods — which is exactly why a network with too many machines, or too much broadcast traffic, bogs down.

Internal workingARP, the cache, and switch learning

Break the mechanism into its moving parts.

  1. A cache miss triggers ARP. When the kernel needs to send a frame to a next-hop IP and has no MAC for it, it queues the packet and emits an ARP request. ARP rides directly on Ethernet (EtherType 0x0806, not inside IP), with opcode 1 and the destination MAC set to the broadcast address ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff so every card on the segment receives it. The payload says, in effect, "who has 10.0.0.2? tell 10.0.0.1 at aa:aa:…."
  2. Only the owner replies. Every host inspects the request, but only the one configured with 10.0.0.2 answers, with an ARP reply (opcode 2) sent as a unicast straight back to A's MAC: "10.0.0.2 is-at bb:bb:…." As a bonus, B also learns A's mapping from the request — ARP populates both ends.
  3. Both sides cache the mapping. A stores 10.0.0.2 → bb:bb:… in its neighbour table so it won't have to broadcast again for a while. On Linux this table has states you can watch: INCOMPLETE (request sent, no reply yet), REACHABLE (confirmed recently), STALE (old but usable, will be re-verified on next use), DELAY/PROBE (re-checking), and FAILED. Entries expire, which is what lets the network heal after a NIC or IP moves — but also what causes a window of staleness when something changes.
  4. The switch learns from source MACs. Independently, the switch reads the source MAC of every frame that arrives and records "MAC X is behind port N" in its MAC/CAM table, with an aging timer (commonly ~5 minutes). It never needs to be told; it learns by observing traffic. This is the one line that separates a switch from a dumb hub.
  5. Forward or flood. To send a frame, the switch looks up the destination MAC. Known → send out that one port. Unknown (an unknown unicast) or a broadcast/multicast → flood to every port except the one it came in on, and keep flooding until it learns the location from a reply. Flooding is the safety net; learning is what makes it rare.
  6. The frame itself. An Ethernet frame is destination MAC (6 bytes), source MAC (6), an optional 4-byte 802.1Q VLAN tag, an EtherType/length field (2), the payload (46–1500 bytes), and a 4-byte FCS checksum. The VLAN tag matters: it lets one physical switch carry several isolated broadcast domains, so a "VLAN" is really "a separate room that happens to share the building's wiring."
ARP is only for the local segment

You never ARP for a machine on a different network — you can't, because the broadcast doesn't leave the segment. When the destination IP is remote, the kernel ARPs for the gateway's MAC instead, wraps the frame for the gateway, and lets the router take it from there. So the destination IP in the packet is the far-away server, but the destination MAC is your local router. This is the concrete mechanism behind Chapter 10's "IP is end-to-end, MAC is hop-by-hop": at each hop, the sender ARPs for the next hop and rewrites the frame. Misreading this — expecting to see the server's MAC on your local capture — confuses a lot of first-time packet readers.

Think first: you ping a server three subnets away and capture on your host. Whose MAC address is in the destination field of the frame that leaves your NIC?

Your default gateway's MAC — not the server's. Because the server is on a different subnet, your kernel knows it can't deliver directly; it looks up the route, finds the next hop is the gateway, and ARPs for the gateway's MAC. The frame's destination MAC is the gateway; the packet's destination IP is still the far server. The gateway strips your frame, and forwards the packet on with a new frame addressed to its next hop. You'll only ever see a remote server's MAC if it's on your own segment. This is why "MAC changes every hop, IP doesn't" is not a slogan — it's literally what ARP does at each router.

ComponentsThe pieces, named

  • The MAC address. 48 bits, six hex bytes. The first three are the OUI, an IEEE-assigned vendor prefix; the last three identify the device. Two flag bits in the first byte matter: the low bit of the first octet marks broadcast/multicast (all-ones ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff is broadcast), and the next bit marks a locally-administered address — which is why virtual NICs (Docker, VMs) start with 02: instead of a real vendor prefix. Meant to be globally unique and permanent, though software can override it.
  • The Ethernet frame. The L2 envelope: dst MAC, src MAC, optional VLAN tag, EtherType, payload, FCS. Minimum 64 bytes on the wire, maximum payload 1500 (the standard MTU). The FCS lets the receiver drop corrupted frames silently.
  • ARP. The IP-to-MAC resolver: broadcast request, unicast reply, cached result. Includes gratuitous ARP — announcing your own mapping unsolicited — used to update everyone's caches after a change and to detect duplicates.
  • The neighbour cache. Linux's ARP table, viewed and edited with ip neigh. Holds the learned mappings and their states, with garbage-collection thresholds (gc_thresh1/2/3) that cap its size — a limit that bites on dense container hosts.
  • The switch and its MAC/CAM table. Learns MAC→port from source addresses, ages entries out, forwards known unicast to one port and floods the unknown. A pure L2 device: it reads MACs, never IPs.
  • The broadcast domain. The set of devices a broadcast reaches — one L2 segment. It's bounded by routers (L3) and split by VLANs. Its size is the thing that makes or breaks L2 scalability, because broadcasts touch everyone in it.
  • VLANs (802.1Q). A tag in the frame that partitions one physical switch into several independent broadcast domains, so tenants or tiers stay isolated at L2 without separate hardware.
Think first: a switch receives a frame for a destination MAC it has never seen. What does it do, and what does that cost?

It floods the frame — sends a copy out every port except the one it arrived on — because it has no idea which port that MAC lives behind. This is "unknown-unicast flooding," and it's the same behaviour as a broadcast. The cost is twofold: bandwidth wasted on every port that didn't need the frame, and the frame reaching machines it wasn't meant for (a small privacy/security leak). Normally it's momentary — the reply teaches the switch the port and future frames are delivered precisely. But it stops being momentary if the destination never answers (a silent or dead host), or if the domain is huge, or if MAC entries keep aging out and re-flooding. That's another reason large flat L2 domains hurt: more hosts and more churn means more flooding, and flooding scales with the size of the broadcast domain, not with the traffic that actually needed to be delivered.

Data flowFirst contact on a LAN

Follow the very first packet between two hosts that have never spoken. Host A runs ping 10.0.0.2. The kernel builds an ICMP echo inside an IP packet with destination 10.0.0.2, checks the routing table, and sees the destination is on its own subnet — so the next hop is the destination itself. It needs B's MAC, checks the neighbour cache, and misses. The packet is parked and an ARP request goes out as a broadcast: "who has 10.0.0.2?" The switch, not knowing where the broadcast should go, floods it to B and C. C ignores it; B recognises its own IP and unicasts a reply, "is-at bb:bb:…," which the switch — having just learned B's port from that reply's source MAC — delivers straight to A. A writes 10.0.0.2 → bb:bb:… into its neighbour cache as REACHABLE, un-parks the waiting ICMP packet, wraps it in a frame addressed to B's MAC, and sends it. The switch forwards it to B's port only. B replies, the echo comes back the same way, and every future frame between them skips ARP entirely until the cache entry ages out. One ping, and you've exercised the entire layer: cache miss, broadcast, reply, learning, cached delivery. You're about to watch each of those events in tcpdump.

Hands-onLabs — build, understand, break, troubleshoot

Two containers on one Docker bridge give you a real L2 segment with real MACs and ARP. Build it:

bash
docker network create labnet
docker run -d --name web  --network labnet nginx:alpine
docker run --rm -it --network labnet --name box --cap-add=NET_ADMIN ubuntu:24.04 bash
# inside box:
apt-get update && apt-get install -y iproute2 iputils-ping iputils-arping tcpdump
Lab 1Build — see MACs and an empty cache fill upBuild

Look at your own hardware address, then watch the neighbour cache go from empty to populated.

bash
ip link show eth0 | grep link/ether     # your MAC — note the 02: (locally administered)
ip neigh                                # neighbour cache — likely empty at first
ping -c1 web                            # forces resolution of web's IP
ip neigh                                # now there's an entry
Outputlink/ether 02:42:ac:12:00:03 brd ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff ... 172.18.0.2 dev eth0 lladdr 02:42:ac:12:00:02 REACHABLE

Two things to register. Your MAC starts 02: — the locally-administered bit, because Docker made it up rather than using a vendor OUI. And the ping turned an empty cache into a REACHABLE mapping of web's IP to its MAC. That entry is the "sticky note" — the next frame to web won't need ARP at all.

Lab 2Understand — watch the ARP exchangeUnderstand

See the request and reply on the wire. Flush the cache first so resolution happens fresh, and sniff ARP while you ping.

bash
ip neigh flush all                       # forget everything
tcpdump -ni eth0 -e arp -c 2 &           # capture the next 2 ARP frames
sleep 1; ping -c1 web >/dev/null; wait
Output02:42:ac:12:00:03 > ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff, ARP, Request who-has 172.18.0.2 tell 172.18.0.3, length 28 02:42:ac:12:00:02 > 02:42:ac:12:00:03, ARP, Reply 172.18.0.2 is-at 02:42:ac:12:00:02, length 28

There's the whole protocol in two lines. The request goes to ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff — the broadcast MAC, so everyone hears "who-has 172.18.0.2 tell 172.18.0.3." The reply is a unicast from web straight back to you: "is-at 02:42:ac:12:00:02." Now peek at the cache states as an entry ages — use it, wait, and watch it drift from REACHABLE toward STALE:

bash
ping -c1 web >/dev/null; ip neigh        # REACHABLE right after use
sleep 40; ip neigh                       # later: STALE — will re-verify on next send

STALE isn't broken — it's "probably still right, I'll confirm when I next use it." That distinction is what makes the cache both fast and self-healing.

Lab 3Break — the stale/wrong ARP entryBreak

Poison your own cache with a wrong MAC and watch traffic vanish — even though the IP, the route, and the host are all fine. This is a stale-ARP blackhole, reproduced on purpose.

bash
ping -c1 web >/dev/null                   # confirm it works
# Pin a bogus MAC for web's IP (a MAC nobody owns):
ip neigh replace 172.18.0.2 lladdr de:ad:de:ad:de:ad dev eth0 nud permanent
ip route get 172.18.0.2                   # routing still says: go direct, it's on-link
ping -c2 -W2 web ; echo "exit=$?"         # ...and yet
Output172.18.0.2 dev eth0 src 172.18.0.3 uid 0 PING web (172.18.0.2) ... 2 packets transmitted, 0 received, 100% packet loss exit=1

Routing is perfect, the host is up, the IP is right — and every packet disappears. Why, and how would you find this if you hadn't caused it? Reason it through before revealing.

Reveal — the frame is being sent to a MAC that doesn't exist

Your cache maps 172.18.0.2 to de:ad:de:ad:de:ad, a MAC no card owns. So the kernel dutifully builds frames addressed to that MAC and hands them to the switch, which floods them looking for a port that never answers. The packets are physically leaving your NIC and going nowhere — a blackhole. Nothing at L3 is wrong, which is exactly why this fools people: ping shows loss, ip route looks fine, the remote host is healthy. The tell is at L2: ip neigh shows the mapping, and a tcpdump shows your frames going out to a MAC that never replies. The fix is to drop the bad entry and let ARP re-resolve:

bash
ip neigh show 172.18.0.2                  # the smoking gun: de:ad:de:ad:de:ad PERMANENT
ip neigh del 172.18.0.2 dev eth0          # or: ip neigh flush all
ping -c1 web                              # re-ARPs, learns the real MAC, works again

In the real world this happens without your help: a server's NIC is swapped, or a VIP moves to a different machine, and hosts keep sending to the old MAC until their cache entry ages out or a gratuitous ARP corrects it. That aging window is the outage.

Lab 4Troubleshoot — the duplicate IPTroubleshoot

Two machines claiming one IP is one of the most maddening intermittent faults in networking. Simulate the detection tooling and reason about the symptom. First, use ARP-based duplicate detection to check whether an address is already in use before you claim it:

bash
# -D = duplicate-address-detection: probe and see if anyone already answers.
arping -D -c2 -I eth0 172.18.0.2 ; echo "in-use? exit=$?"   # web owns it → conflict
arping -D -c2 -I eth0 172.18.0.99 ; echo "free? exit=$?"    # nobody owns it → free
OutputARPING 172.18.0.2 ... Unicast reply from 172.18.0.2 [02:42:AC:12:00:02] in-use? exit=1 ARPING 172.18.0.99 ... (no reply) free? exit=0

Now the scenario: users report a service is up sometimes and dead other times, seemingly at random, and someone notices the server's MAC in ip neigh keeps changing between two values. What's happening, and which command confirms it? Think first.

Reveal — two hosts are answering for one IP

Two machines are configured with the same IP, so both answer every ARP request for it. Whichever reply arrives last wins the cache, and since that races differently each time, clients flip between the two hosts' MACs — hence connectivity that works or fails depending on which box happened to win, and a neighbour entry that keeps changing MAC. The confirmations: arping -D reports the address in use (as above), the kernel logs a duplicate (dmesg | grep -i 'duplicate address'), and repeated ip neigh show <ip> shows the lladdr flipping. The fix is to find and re-address one of the two hosts — this is why address management (DHCP leases, IPAM, static assignment discipline) exists. On a well-run L2, RFC 5227 address-conflict detection is what stops a host from claiming an address that's already answering. The signature to remember: intermittent by-chance connectivity plus a MAC that changes underneath a stable IP equals duplicate IP.

Lab 5Production challenge — gratuitous ARP and VIP failoverSenior challenge

High availability at L2 rests on one trick: when a virtual IP (VIP) moves from a dead node to a live one, the new node shouts a gratuitous ARP so every host and switch updates its cache to the new MAC immediately, instead of waiting minutes for entries to age out. Watch a gratuitous ARP and reason about the failure it prevents. Add a VIP to your box and announce it:

bash
ip addr add 172.18.0.50/16 dev eth0       # claim the VIP on this node
# -U = unsolicited/gratuitous: tell the whole segment ".50 is now at MY mac"
tcpdump -ni eth0 -e arp -c1 &
sleep 1; arping -U -c1 -I eth0 172.18.0.50; wait
Output02:42:ac:12:00:03 > ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff, ARP, Reply 172.18.0.50 is-at 02:42:ac:12:00:03, length 28

That's a gratuitous ARP: an unsolicited reply, broadcast to everyone, mapping the VIP to this node's MAC — nobody asked, but everyone updates. Now the senior question: a VIP fails over, the new node sends its gratuitous ARP, and yet some clients keep hitting the dead node for a while. Give at least two reasons this happens and how you'd reduce the failover window. Work it fully.

Reveal — why failover isn't instant, and how to tighten it

Several things stretch the window. A lost gratuitous ARP: it's a single broadcast with no acknowledgement — if it's dropped, caches don't update until they age out on their own (which is why HA tools send several GARPs, not one). Stale caches with long timers: a host that has the VIP as REACHABLE may not re-verify for its full timeout; some devices honour a gratuitous update, some don't. The switch's MAC table: it also has to relearn that the VIP's MAC now lives behind a different port, which happens when the new node sends traffic, but a silent new node leaves the switch pointing at the old port. Long-lived TCP connections: existing flows to the old node don't magically move; they reset and must reconnect. To tighten it: send multiple gratuitous ARPs on failover (keepalived/VRRP do this), keep neighbour and MAC-table timers sane, ensure the new node emits traffic promptly so the switch relearns, and design clients to retry on connection reset rather than hang. And the deeper lesson driving modern design: this L2 failover dance is fragile and doesn't cross subnets, which is a big reason clouds and Kubernetes prefer L3 mechanisms — moving a route or reprogramming a load balancer — over ARP-based VIP moves. You'll see the L3 version of this all through Parts V and VI.

Failure & troubleshootingHow this shows up in real incidents

Incident pattern · the stale-ARP blackhole after a move

Symptom: a host, VIP, or NIC was replaced, and some clients can reach it while others silently can't — with no L3 error. First cut: compare ip neigh show <ip> on a broken client against the target's actual MAC. Confirm: a tcpdump shows frames leaving to a MAC that never answers. Cause is usually: a stale cache entry pointing at the old MAC, not yet aged out and not corrected by a gratuitous ARP. Flush the neighbour entry, and on the server side send gratuitous ARPs on any address move.

Incident pattern · duplicate IP

Symptom: connectivity that works intermittently and seemingly at random, sometimes tied to which client you test from. First cut: watch ip neigh show <ip> for a lladdr that flips between two MACs. Confirm: arping -D reports the address in use; dmesg logs "duplicate address detected." Cause is usually: two hosts configured with the same IP — a static-assignment mistake or a DHCP/IPAM gap. Re-address one; add conflict detection to prevent a repeat.

Incident pattern · neighbour table overflow on dense hosts

Symptom: a busy container host starts dropping traffic and dmesg fills with "neighbour table overflow." First cut: ip neigh | wc -l against the kernel's gc_thresh3. Confirm: the count is pinned near the threshold. Cause is usually: far more neighbours (many pods, many peers) than the default cap of ~1024. Raise net.ipv4.neigh.default.gc_thresh1/2/3. A very real Kubernetes-node problem at pod density — L2 limits leaking into an L3-heavy world.

Production · security · performanceWhat matters in production

Keep broadcast domains small. Every broadcast — every ARP, every unknown-unicast flood — touches every device in the domain, so a big flat L2 network spends more and more of its capacity on noise and takes longer to converge when something changes. Broadcast storms (often a cabling loop without spanning-tree protection) can melt a whole segment. The industry answer is to bound L2 domains tightly with VLANs and routers, and, at scale, to route at L3 all the way to the host so the L2 domains are tiny. This is precisely why modern datacenter and cloud fabrics are L3-routed rather than one giant switched network, and it's the backdrop for how Kubernetes and cloud VPCs are built.

L2 has no security, so don't trust it. ARP has no authentication whatsoever: any machine on the segment can send a forged reply claiming to be the gateway and quietly become a man-in-the-middle (ARP spoofing/poisoning). Switch defences exist — Dynamic ARP Inspection, port security, DHCP snooping — and help on managed networks. But the durable answer, the one that assumes the network is hostile, is to encrypt and authenticate above L2: TLS end to end, mTLS between services, a service mesh. "The network is a trust boundary" is a mistake; treat the LAN as untrusted and the ARP-spoofing threat mostly evaporates.

Failover and address moves need a plan. When a VIP or an IP moves, caches across the segment must be corrected fast, or you get a stale-ARP outage lasting until entries age out. Gratuitous ARP is the mechanism, and HA tooling (keepalived/VRRP) sends several on failover for exactly the reliability reasons in Lab 5. But because ARP-based failover is fragile and confined to one subnet, prefer L3 mechanisms — moving a route, reprogramming a load balancer, updating DNS with a short TTL — wherever the design allows.

Watch the neighbour-table limits on dense hosts. The Linux ARP cache has hard size thresholds, and container hosts with hundreds of pods and peers blow past the ~1024 default, dropping traffic with "neighbour table overflow." Tuning gc_thresh is a standard part of preparing a node for high pod density — an L2 detail that becomes an L4/L7 outage if ignored.

Common mistakes to retire: assuming a correct IP and route means delivery will work (a stale MAC blackholes it); treating the LAN as a trust boundary; expecting a single gratuitous ARP to fail over instantly; and building large flat L2 domains because "it's simpler," then meeting broadcast storms and slow convergence in production.

System-design connectionWhere this reaches into architecture

The limits of L2 shape how large systems are built. Because broadcast domains don't scale and ARP-based failover is fragile and subnet-bound, serious fabrics push routing (L3) as close to the host as possible and keep each L2 segment small — the design principle behind cloud VPCs and behind the way Kubernetes networks pods. Inside a single node, pod-to-pod traffic still leans on L2 and a virtual switch (a Linux bridge) with the same ARP and learning you just watched; across nodes, the cluster deliberately switches to L3 routing or an overlay to avoid one enormous broadcast domain spanning the whole cluster (Part V). High availability inherits the same tension: the classic on-prem VIP-plus-gratuitous-ARP pattern gives way, in the cloud, to moving an elastic IP or reprogramming a load balancer's targets — the L3 answer to the same "the active node died, send traffic elsewhere" problem. Even the neighbour-table limits reach up into design: they cap how many peers a node can hold, which is a real constraint on pod density. Every one of those choices is someone having internalised what this chapter shows — that L2 is fast and simple in the small and fragile and noisy in the large — and designing to stay in the small.

InterviewInterview questions & answers

Answer each in your head first.

30-secWhat's the difference between a MAC address and an IP address?

A MAC address is a 48-bit hardware address burned into the network card, unique and permanent, and it only has meaning on the local segment — it's how frames are delivered hop by hop. An IP address is assigned in software, can change, and is meaningful end-to-end across the whole internet — it's how packets are routed. The key operational consequence: the destination IP in a packet stays constant from source to final host, while the destination MAC is rewritten at every hop to name just the next device. ARP is what bridges the two, resolving a local IP to the MAC that currently owns it.

2-minWalk me through ARP resolving an address, and what the switch does.

When a host needs to send a frame to a next-hop IP on its own segment and has no MAC for it, it broadcasts an ARP request — an Ethernet frame with EtherType 0x0806, destination MAC all-ones, asking "who has this IP? tell me." Every host on the segment receives it because it's a broadcast, but only the one owning that IP replies, with a unicast ARP reply sent straight back: "that IP is at this MAC." The asker caches the mapping in its neighbour table so it won't have to broadcast again, and the responder learns the asker's mapping from the request too. Meanwhile the switch is doing its own thing independently: it reads the source MAC of every frame and records which port each MAC lives behind, with an aging timer. To forward a frame it looks up the destination MAC — if it knows the port, it sends only there; if it's an unknown unicast or a broadcast, it floods to every port but the ingress. So ARP answers "what's the MAC for this IP," and the switch answers "which port is that MAC behind" — and if the destination IP is on a different subnet, the host ARPs for the gateway's MAC instead, not the far host's.

DeepA host has the right IP, right route, and the target is healthy, but every packet is lost. What's your L2 theory?

A wrong or stale ARP entry — the frames are being addressed to a MAC that no longer owns that IP, or to no real card at all, so they're blackholed. This happens when a NIC is replaced or a VIP moves and clients keep the old mapping until it ages out. I'd confirm at L2, not L3: ip neigh show <ip> to see the cached MAC and compare it to the target's real one, and a tcpdump to watch frames leaving to a MAC that never answers. The fix is to drop the neighbour entry (ip neigh del or flush) so ARP re-resolves, and on the server side to send gratuitous ARPs whenever an address moves so caches update immediately rather than after the aging timeout. The reason this fools people is that everything at L3 looks perfect — the whole failure lives one layer down, which is exactly the layer-localisation habit from Chapter 10 paying off.

ScenarioA service is intermittently reachable, and its MAC in the neighbour cache keeps changing. Diagnosis?

A duplicate IP — two hosts configured with the same address, both answering ARP requests for it. Because whichever reply arrives last wins the cache, and that races differently each time, clients flip between the two hosts' MACs, so connectivity works or fails depending on which one they land on. The changing lladdr under a stable IP is the signature. I'd confirm with arping -D against the address (it'll report the address in use, with replies coming from more than one MAC), check dmesg for a "duplicate address detected" log, and watch ip neigh show <ip> flip. The fix is to find the second host and re-address it, and the prevention is disciplined address management — DHCP or IPAM with conflict detection — so two machines can't claim one IP. It's a pure L2 conflict that presents as a flaky application, which is why you have to reason down the layers to catch it.

Follow-upWhy do large flat L2 networks not scale, and what do we do instead?

Because L2 relies on broadcasts — ARP, and flooding of unknown unicast — and every broadcast reaches every device in the domain. As the domain grows, an increasing share of capacity goes to noise, convergence after a change slows down, and a loop can trigger a broadcast storm that saturates the whole segment. Failover is fragile too: it depends on gratuitous ARP updating everyone's caches, which is unacknowledged and confined to a single subnet. So at scale we bound L2 domains hard — VLANs to segment, routers to cap broadcast — and push L3 routing as close to the host as possible, so each L2 segment stays tiny and the heavy lifting is done by routing, which scales hierarchically. That's why cloud VPCs and Kubernetes network the way they do: small L2 domains, L3 or overlays between nodes, and load-balancer or route changes rather than ARP tricks for failover. Keep L2 in the small, where it's fast and simple, and let L3 handle the large.

RetainQuick revision

Layer 2 is the hardware handoff on one segment. A MAC address is a 48-bit, burned-in, segment-local hardware address; an IP is a software address that's meaningful end-to-end. ARP bridges them: to send to a next-hop IP on your segment, you broadcast "who has this IP?" to ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff, the owner unicasts "is-at this MAC," and both sides cache the mapping — with states like REACHABLE and STALE that make the cache fast and self-healing. If the destination is on another subnet, you ARP for the gateway's MAC, not the far host's, which is why the MAC changes every hop while the IP doesn't. A switch independently learns MAC→port from source addresses and forwards known unicast to one port while flooding the unknown and the broadcast; the set of devices a broadcast reaches is the broadcast domain, bounded by routers and split by VLANs. The failures that matter: a stale or wrong ARP entry blackholes traffic that's perfect at L3; a duplicate IP makes connectivity flaky with a MAC that flips; and dense hosts overflow the neighbour table. L2 is unauthenticated (ARP spoofing is trivial), so trust encryption above it, not the wire. And because broadcasts and ARP-based failover don't scale, real fabrics keep L2 domains small and route at L3 — gratuitous ARP moves a VIP in the small, route and load-balancer changes do it in the large.

The 10 things to remember

  1. MAC = 48-bit hardware address, segment-local and hop-by-hop; IP = software address, end-to-end. ARP maps one to the other.
  2. ARP request is a broadcast to ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff; the reply is a unicast; both ends cache the result.
  3. You ARP for the destination only if it's on your subnet — otherwise you ARP for the gateway's MAC.
  4. The neighbour cache has states (REACHABLE/STALE/FAILED); STALE means "probably fine, verify on next use."
  5. A switch learns MAC→port from source MACs, forwards known unicast to one port, and floods unknown/broadcast frames.
  6. The broadcast domain is everyone a broadcast reaches — one L2 segment/VLAN; routers bound it, VLANs split it.
  7. A stale or wrong ARP entry blackholes traffic that looks perfect at L3 — check ip neigh, not just ip route.
  8. Duplicate IP = intermittent connectivity plus a MAC that flips under a stable IP; detect with arping -D and dmesg.
  9. Gratuitous ARP announces your own mapping to update caches on failover — sent several times because it's unacknowledged.
  10. L2 is unauthenticated and doesn't scale big/flat: keep broadcast domains small, route at L3, and encrypt above the wire.
