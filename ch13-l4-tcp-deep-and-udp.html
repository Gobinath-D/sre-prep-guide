Part II · Networking from First Principles · Chapter 13

L4 — TCP deep, and UDP

IP will lose your packets, reorder them, and duplicate them without apology. TCP turns that chaos into a reliable, ordered byte stream — and it does it with a state machine you can watch live, retransmission timers you can tune, and a TIME_WAIT rule that looks like a bug and is actually protecting you. This is the protocol behind almost every incident you'll ever debug.

Why this exists · the problemThe problem TCP solves

IP makes exactly one promise, and it's a weak one: it will make a best effort to move your packet one hop closer to its destination. That's all. Packets can be dropped when a queue fills, delivered out of order because two of them took different paths, duplicated by an over-eager retransmit somewhere upstream, or delayed for seconds and then arrive after you'd given up. From an application's point of view, raw IP is a firehose of unreliable, unordered fragments.

But almost everything you write wants the opposite. When you send an HTTP request or a SQL query, you want the bytes to arrive all of them, in order, exactly once, and you want to be told if that becomes impossible. Someone has to bridge that gap — to take the unreliable packet service underneath and present a reliable, ordered byte stream on top. That someone is TCP (Transmission Control Protocol), and the way it does it is the substance of this chapter.

TCP achieves reliability with four mechanisms working together: sequence numbers so the receiver can reorder and detect gaps, acknowledgements so the sender knows what arrived, retransmission so lost data is resent, and windows so neither the receiver nor the network is overwhelmed. Layer all four on top of best-effort IP and you get the illusion of a perfect pipe. The illusion is good enough that most engineers forget it's an illusion — until a retransmit storm or a stuck TIME_WAIT reminds them.

Key point

TCP is a stateful protocol running over a stateless one. Both ends keep a control block tracking sequence numbers, windows, timers, and a connection state. Every TCP problem you'll debug is really a question about that state: which state is each end in, what's it waiting for, and why is it stuck there? UDP, by contrast, keeps almost no state — and that's its entire trade-off.

Mental model · analogyThe mental model: a tracked phone call vs a postcard

Real-world analogy

TCP is a phone call with a meticulous note-taker. First you establish the call — "can you hear me?" / "yes, can you hear me?" / "yes" — a three-part exchange so both sides know the line is open before either says anything important. Then you talk, and crucially, you number every sentence and the other person confirms which numbers they've heard: "got up to sentence 40." If they say "I'm still on 37," you know 38 and 39 were lost and you repeat them. When you're done, you don't just drop the phone — you say goodbye and wait for them to say goodbye back, so neither of you hangs up mid-sentence.

UDP is mailing postcards. You write an address, drop it in the box, and hope. No call setup, no numbering, no confirmation, no goodbye. Most arrive; some don't; a few might arrive out of order or twice. You find out about losses only if you build your own scheme on top. In exchange, there's zero setup cost and zero waiting — you can blast a thousand postcards without waiting for a single reply.

That "wait a moment after saying goodbye before you reuse the phone number" habit is TIME_WAIT. It feels wasteful — the call's over, why keep the line reserved? — but it's what stops a stray late sentence from the old call being mistaken for the start of the next one on the same number.

ArchitectureThe handshake, the states, and the close

A TCP connection has a life: it's born in a three-segment handshake, lives in ESTABLISHED while data flows, and dies in a four-segment close. Both ends walk a state machine in lock-step, and every state has a reason to exist. The figure is the single most important diagram in this chapter — the handshake, the data phase, and the close, with the state each side occupies written beside its lifeline.

Why an SRE cares

Ninety percent of the network incidents you'll be paged for are a connection stuck in the wrong state or churning through states too fast. A pile of CLOSE_WAIT means an app forgot to close sockets. A pile of TIME_WAIT on a busy client means port pressure. SYN-SENT that never advances means the SYN isn't getting through — firewall, wrong port, or nothing listening. Learn to read the states with ss and you're reading the connection's mind.

Internal workingThe state machine and why TIME_WAIT protects you

Walk the machine the way the kernel does. Each transition is triggered by a segment arriving or the application calling a syscall:

  1. The server does a passive open. It calls listen() and sits in LISTEN, waiting. It sends nothing.
  2. The client does an active open. connect() sends a SYN carrying a random initial sequence number (the ISN — random for security, so off-path attackers can't guess it). The client moves to SYN-SENT.
  3. The server replies SYN,ACK — acknowledging the client's ISN and offering its own — and moves to SYN-RECEIVED. This half-open connection lives in the SYN queue.
  4. The client sends the final ACK and reaches ESTABLISHED. When that ACK arrives, the server also reaches ESTABLISHED and the connection moves from the SYN queue to the accept queue, where it waits for the app to call accept(). Three segments, and both ends now agree the pipe is open and know each other's starting sequence numbers.
  5. Data flows. Each byte has a sequence number; the receiver ACKs the highest contiguous byte it has (a cumulative ACK). SYN and FIN each consume one sequence number even though they carry no data — that's why the ACK is seq+1.
  6. Closing takes four segments because close is one-directional. The active closer sends FIN (→ FIN-WAIT-1). The peer ACKs it (→ CLOSE-WAIT on the peer, FIN-WAIT-2 on the closer) — but the peer can keep sending data, because only its direction is still open. When the peer's app also closes, it sends its own FIN (→ LAST-ACK). The closer ACKs it and enters TIME-WAIT.
  7. TIME_WAIT waits, then closes. The active closer sits in TIME-WAIT for 2 × the maximum segment lifetime (on Linux this is a fixed 60 seconds, TCP_TIMEWAIT_LEN) before finally reaching CLOSED and releasing the 4-tuple.

Why TIME_WAIT exists is the question that separates people who memorised the diagram from people who understand it. It does two jobs. First, it guarantees the final ACK can be re-sent: if that ACK is lost, the peer (stuck in LAST-ACK) will retransmit its FIN, and the closer must still be around to ACK it again — if it had vanished, the peer would hang. Second, and more subtly, it lets any old, delayed duplicate segments from this connection drain out of the network before the same 4-tuple can be reused. Without it, a straggler from the dead connection could arrive during a brand-new connection on the identical (src IP, src port, dst IP, dst port) and be accepted as valid data — silent corruption. TIME_WAIT trades a held port for correctness. It is a feature.

Think first: your server has 40,000 sockets in TIME_WAIT and you're running low on resources. Whose fault is it, and what changed?

The active closer holds TIME_WAIT, so if the server is accumulating it, the server is closing connections first. Normally clients close first (browser finishes, disconnects), leaving TIME_WAIT on the client. A server drowning in TIME_WAIT usually means it's initiating the close — for example, an HTTP server with keepalive_timeout set aggressively low, or a proxy that closes upstream connections after each request. The right fixes are to let the client close (raise keep-alive), enable net.ipv4.tcp_tw_reuse for outbound connections, or reduce how often you open brand-new connections at all (pooling). What you should not do is slash TCP_TIMEWAIT_LEN to near zero — that re-opens the exact reuse hazard TIME_WAIT exists to prevent.

ComponentsRetransmission, flow control, congestion control, Nagle

Four subsystems keep the byte stream reliable and polite. You tune all four in production, so name them precisely.

Retransmission and the RTO

TCP resends data it believes was lost. It decides "lost" two ways. The retransmission timeout (RTO): the sender starts a timer per segment; if no ACK arrives before it fires, resend and double the timer (exponential backoff). The RTO isn't a constant — it's computed from a smoothed round-trip-time estimate plus its variance (RFC 6298: RTO = SRTT + 4×RTTVAR), so a connection to a nearby host has a tight timeout and one to the far side of the planet a loose one. The faster path is fast retransmit: three duplicate ACKs for the same sequence number mean "I keep getting later data but I'm still missing this one," so the sender resends immediately without waiting for the timer (RFC 5681). Selective ACK (SACK) lets the receiver say exactly which ranges it has, so the sender resends only the holes.

Flow control — protect the receiver

Every ACK carries a receive window (rwnd): "I have this many bytes of buffer free." The sender must never have more unacknowledged data in flight than the receiver's advertised window. If the receiver's application stops reading, its buffer fills, it advertises a zero window, and the sender stops and sends periodic window probes until space opens up. Because the window field is 16 bits (max 64 KB), high-bandwidth long-distance links use the window-scaling option (RFC 7323) to multiply it — without scaling, a fat transcontinental link is capped absurdly low.

Congestion control — protect the network

Flow control protects the receiver; congestion control protects the network in between, which has no window to advertise. The sender keeps a second, hidden window — the congestion window (cwnd) — and the amount it may send is min(rwnd, cwnd). It starts small and ramps up: slow start doubles cwnd each round trip (exponential) until it hits a threshold or sees loss; then congestion avoidance grows it roughly linearly. Loss is the signal — a drop means "the path is congested," so cwnd is cut (the AIMD rule: additive increase, multiplicative decrease). Linux's default algorithm is CUBIC; BBR is an alternative that models bandwidth and latency instead of treating loss as the only signal, and it can help on lossy or bufferbloated paths.

Nagle and delayed ACK — the classic latency trap

Two optimisations, each sensible alone, that combine into a notorious stall. Nagle's algorithm (RFC 896) says: if you have unacknowledged data outstanding, don't send a new small segment — buffer it until the outstanding data is ACKed, so you coalesce tiny writes into fewer packets. Delayed ACK (RFC 1122) says: don't ACK immediately, wait up to ~40 ms (Linux) hoping to piggyback the ACK on a reply. Now picture a request/response protocol where the client sends a small request in two writes: Nagle holds the second write waiting for an ACK of the first, while the server's delayed-ACK holds that ACK waiting for data to piggyback on — deadlock, broken only when the ~40 ms timer fires. The symptom is a protocol that should take microseconds taking a suspiciously round 40 ms per exchange. The fix is TCP_NODELAY (disable Nagle) on latency-sensitive sockets — which is why nearly every RPC library and database driver sets it.

Think first: a client sees exactly ~40 ms of latency on every small request to a service on the same rack, where round-trip should be under 1 ms. What's happening?

That oddly precise, oddly constant 40 ms is the delayed-ACK timer firing — the fingerprint of a Nagle + delayed-ACK interaction. The client (or server) is holding a small segment under Nagle because prior data is unacknowledged, and the other side is holding the ACK under delayed-ACK hoping to piggyback. Neither moves until the timer expires. The fix is setsockopt(TCP_NODELAY) on the sending side; if you can't touch the code, sometimes TCP_QUICKACK on the receiver helps. The giveaway is always that the latency is a fixed, network-independent constant (~40 ms) rather than scaling with distance or load.

Data flowFollowing one connection, SYN to FIN

A client runs curl http://server:8080. The kernel picks an ephemeral source port and sends a SYN with a random ISN; the client socket is SYN-SENT. The server, in LISTEN, receives it, creates a half-open entry in its SYN queue, and returns SYN,ACK (SYN-RECEIVED). The client ACKs and both reach ESTABLISHED; the server's connection moves to the accept queue, and the server app's accept() pops it. Now data: curl writes the HTTP request; the server ACKs bytes as they arrive, growing cwnd from slow start; the response streams back, bounded by min(rwnd, cwnd). When curl has the full response it calls close(), sending a FIN (FIN-WAIT-1). The server ACKs (client → FIN-WAIT-2, server → CLOSE-WAIT), finishes writing anything pending, then sends its own FIN (LAST-ACK). The client ACKs and enters TIME-WAIT, holding its 4-tuple for 60 seconds so no straggler can pollute a reused connection. The server reaches CLOSED immediately. Every step you just read is visible with ss and tcpdump — which is the whole point of the labs.

Lab 3Break — manufacture a pile of TIME_WAITBreak

Open and close many short connections fast, then count the states. Because curl is the active closer, TIME_WAIT lands on the client side.

bash
for i in $(seq 1 200); do curl -s localhost:8080 >/dev/null; done
ss -tan state time-wait | wc -l
ss -tan state time-wait | head -3
Output201 TIME-WAIT 0 0 127.0.0.1:53990 127.0.0.1:8080 TIME-WAIT 0 0 127.0.0.1:54004 127.0.0.1:8080

Two hundred connections, two hundred TIME_WAIT sockets, each holding its ephemeral port for 60 seconds. On loopback that's harmless. Now the question a senior asks: at what request rate does this become a problem, and why?

Reveal the math

You have roughly 28,000 ephemeral ports (net.ipv4.ip_local_port_range is about 32768–60999 by default). Each TIME_WAIT holds one port for 60 seconds. So the ceiling for brand-new connections to a single destination IP:port is about 28000 / 60 ≈ 460 connections per second before you start reusing ports faster than they free up and connect() begins to fail with EADDRNOTAVAIL. A client hammering one backend with fresh short-lived connections hits this wall surprisingly early. The real fix is connection reuse (keep-alive / pooling) so you're not opening a new connection per request; net.ipv4.tcp_tw_reuse=1 lets the kernel safely reuse TIME_WAIT sockets for new outbound connections and buys headroom. This is the port-pressure incident in miniature, and you'll meet it for real in Chapter 14.

Lab 4Troubleshoot — SYN-backlog / accept-queue overflowTroubleshoot

A listener has two queues: the SYN queue (half-open handshakes) and the accept queue (completed handshakes waiting for accept()). If the app is slow to accept and connections arrive faster, the accept queue fills and the kernel starts dropping. Reproduce it with a server that listens but never accepts.

bash
# A socket with a tiny backlog that never calls accept():
python3 - <<'PY' &
import socket, time
s = socket.socket(); s.setsockopt(socket.SOL_SOCKET, socket.SO_REUSEADDR, 1)
s.bind(("0.0.0.0", 9090)); s.listen(2)   # backlog of 2, no accept()
time.sleep(600)
PY
# Fire more connections than the backlog can hold:
for i in $(seq 1 20); do (curl -s --max-time 2 localhost:9090 &) ; done
sleep 1
ss -tlnp 'sport = :9090'
nstat -az | grep -iE 'ListenOverflow|ListenDrop'
OutputState Recv-Q Send-Q Local Address:Port LISTEN 3 2 0.0.0.0:9090 # Recv-Q(3) > Send-Q(2): queue is over its limit TcpExtListenOverflows 17 TcpExtListenDrops 17

For a listening socket, Recv-Q greater than Send-Q means completed connections are piling up past the accept-queue limit, and the ListenOverflows counter climbing confirms the kernel is dropping them. In production this looks like intermittent connection timeouts under load while the server's CPU looks fine — the app just isn't accepting fast enough, or its listen() backlog (and net.core.somaxconn) is too small. The fixes: raise the backlog and somaxconn, add worker threads/processes to accept faster, and for SYN floods specifically enable net.ipv4.tcp_syncookies so a full SYN queue can't wedge new legitimate handshakes.

Lab 5Production challenge — read a connection resetSenior challenge

"Connection reset by peer" is one of the most-misread errors in the industry. Force one and see exactly what it is on the wire. The cleanest way: connect to a port with nothing listening — the kernel answers with an immediate RST.

bash
# Shell A — capture:
tcpdump -i lo -n 'tcp port 9999' -c 4
# Shell B — connect to a dead port:
curl -s --max-time 2 localhost:9999 ; echo "curl exit: $?"
OutputIP 127.0.0.1.55220 > 127.0.0.1.9999: Flags [S], seq ... # SYN IP 127.0.0.1.9999 > 127.0.0.1.55220: Flags [R.], seq ... # RST — no one home curl exit: 7 # "Failed to connect" — ECONNREFUSED

Now the senior distinction that saves you on call: a fast RST is not a timeout. Which of these are you seeing, and what does each tell you? Reason it out before opening the answer.

Reveal how to read resets vs timeouts

A RST on the SYN (like above) is ECONNREFUSED: something is reachable and actively said "no port here" — the host is up, the service is down or on a different port. It's fast. A SYN with no reply at all that eventually gives up is ETIMEDOUT: a firewall is silently dropping the packet, or the host is gone — nothing is refusing, nothing is answering. It's slow (the SYN retransmits with exponential backoff for seconds). A RST mid-connection (on an established flow) is "connection reset by peer" (ECONNRESET): the other side tore the connection down abnormally — it crashed, it hit an idle/keepalive timeout, a stateful firewall or load balancer expired the flow, or the app called close with unread data. The one-line rule: refused = reachable but no service; timeout = filtered or gone; reset mid-stream = the peer or a middlebox killed an existing connection. Confirming which one you have — with the socket error and a quick tcpdump — is usually 80% of the diagnosis.

Failure & troubleshootingHow this shows up in real incidents

Incident pattern · TIME_WAIT / ephemeral-port pressure

Symptom: a client or proxy starts failing outbound connections under load with EADDRNOTAVAIL / "cannot assign requested address," while the backend is healthy. First cut: ss -tan state time-wait | wc -l and check net.ipv4.ip_local_port_range. Confirm: TIME_WAIT count is near the size of the ephemeral range for one destination. Cause is usually: opening a fresh connection per request to one backend instead of reusing connections. Fix with keep-alive/pooling first; tcp_tw_reuse as headroom. Never set TIME_WAIT to zero.

Incident pattern · SYN-backlog / accept-queue overflow

Symptom: intermittent connection timeouts under traffic spikes; server CPU and memory look fine. First cut: ss -tlnp and look for listener Recv-Q > Send-Q; watch nstat ListenOverflows/ListenDrops. Confirm: those counters climb during the spikes. Cause is usually: the app accepts too slowly or the backlog / somaxconn is too small. Raise both and speed up accept; enable tcp_syncookies if it's a SYN flood.

Incident pattern · mystery connection resets

Symptom: apps log "connection reset by peer" at seemingly random times. First cut: tcpdump both ends and find who sends the [R]. Confirm: resets cluster at a fixed idle interval. Cause is usually: a stateful middlebox (NAT gateway, cloud load balancer, firewall) silently dropping the flow from its table after an idle timeout, so the next packet gets a RST — the classic "idle connections die after N minutes." Fix with TCP keep-alive tuned below the middlebox timeout, or shorter application-level heartbeats.

Incident pattern · retransmit storm / throughput collapse

Symptom: throughput between two hosts is a fraction of the link and jittery. First cut: ss -ti shows retrans, rtt, and cwnd per socket; nstat shows TcpRetransSegs. Confirm: high retransmits and a cwnd that keeps collapsing. Cause is usually: packet loss on the path (a bad NIC, an overloaded switch, or bufferbloat) making congestion control repeatedly back off. Investigate the path; consider BBR on lossy links, but fix the loss first.

Production · security · performanceWhat matters in production

Reuse connections. The single highest-value TCP lesson for throughput and stability is: don't open a new connection per request. The handshake costs a round trip, slow start means the first exchanges are slow, and short-lived connections generate TIME_WAIT and port pressure. Keep-alive and connection pools amortise all of that. This is why HTTP/1.1 defaults to persistent connections and why HTTP/2 multiplexes — topics for Chapter 16.

Tune with intent, not superstition. The knobs that actually earn their keep: somaxconn and the app backlog for accept-heavy services; tcp_tw_reuse for outbound-heavy clients; window scaling (on by default) for long-fat links; TCP_NODELAY for latency-sensitive RPC; TCP keep-alive below any middlebox idle timeout. The knobs people cargo-cult and shouldn't: gutting TCP_TIMEWAIT_LEN, and the old tcp_tw_recycle (removed from Linux precisely because it broke connections behind NAT).

Security touches L4 directly. Random ISNs defend against off-path injection; SYN cookies defend against SYN-flood denial of service by letting the server not allocate state until the handshake completes. RSTs can be forged by on-path attackers to tear down connections, which is one reason TLS matters (Chapter 17) — it authenticates the stream so a spoofed RST can drop the connection but can't inject data.

Common mistakes: treating "connection reset" and "connection timeout" as the same thing (they point at opposite causes); blaming the app for TIME_WAIT that a load balancer's close policy created; opening a connection per request and then being surprised by port exhaustion; and reaching for kernel tunables before checking whether the real fix is connection reuse.

The alternativeUDP and its trade-offs

UDP is what's left when you strip TCP down to nothing. Its header is 8 bytes — source port, destination port, length, checksum — and that's the whole protocol. No handshake, no sequence numbers, no ACKs, no retransmission, no ordering, no flow control, no congestion control, no connection state. You send a datagram; it probably arrives; you're not told if it doesn't. That sounds useless until you notice what it buys: zero setup latency, no head-of-line blocking (a lost datagram doesn't stall the next one), and no per-connection state on the server, so one socket can serve enormous fan-out.

That's exactly right for some workloads. DNS uses UDP because a query and its answer each fit in one datagram and retrying is cheaper than a handshake (Chapter 15). Real-time media and gaming use it because a late packet is worthless — you'd rather skip it than wait for a retransmit that arrives after the moment has passed. Metrics like statsd fire-and-forget over UDP. And QUIC — the transport under HTTP/3 — is built on UDP precisely so it can implement better reliability and congestion control in user space without being trapped by TCP's in-kernel behaviour and middlebox ossification (Chapter 16). The catch is honest: if you use UDP and you need reliability or ordering or congestion control, you are now writing it, and getting congestion control right is genuinely hard. UDP isn't "TCP but faster" — it's "TCP's guarantees are now your problem."

System-design connectionWhere this reaches into architecture

TCP's mechanics set hard limits you design around. The handshake round trip plus slow start is why latency-sensitive systems keep connections warm and pool them, and why putting a service physically closer to its clients (edge, regional replicas) pays off — fewer, shorter round trips. TIME_WAIT and the ephemeral-port ceiling cap how many fresh connections one client can make to one backend per second, which pushes you toward connection reuse and toward spreading load across many backends. Congestion control is why a single TCP flow rarely fills a fat pipe and why parallel connections (or protocols like QUIC) move bulk data faster. And the "reset vs timeout vs refused" distinction is the seed of good health checks and circuit breakers (Chapter 88): a fast refuse should fail fast, a silent timeout needs a deadline, and a mid-stream reset should trigger a retry on a fresh connection — not the same dead one.

InterviewInterview questions & answers

Answer each in your head first.

30-secWhy does closing a TCP connection take four segments but opening takes three?

Because a close is one direction at a time. In the handshake, the middle SYN and ACK can be combined into one SYN,ACK segment, giving three. In the close, each side has its own direction to shut down: the active closer sends FIN and gets an ACK, but the peer may still have data to send, so its FIN comes later as a separate segment with its own ACK — four in total. If both sides had nothing left to send, the ACK and the second FIN could coalesce, but you can't count on that.

2-minWhat is TIME_WAIT, who holds it, and why shouldn't you just disable it?

TIME_WAIT is the state the active closer — the side that sent the first FIN — sits in after acknowledging the peer's FIN, for twice the maximum segment lifetime (60 seconds on Linux). It does two jobs. First, if its final ACK is lost, the peer will retransmit its FIN, and the closer must still exist to re-ACK it; otherwise the peer hangs in LAST-ACK. Second, it keeps the 4-tuple reserved long enough for any delayed duplicate segments from the old connection to expire, so they can't be misread as data on a new connection reusing the same tuple. Disabling it — slashing the timer to zero — brings back exactly that reuse hazard and risks silent data corruption. The legitimate ways to relieve TIME_WAIT pressure are connection reuse (keep-alive, pooling) so you close far fewer connections, and tcp_tw_reuse for new outbound connections, which is safe because it uses timestamps to reject stale segments. The thing to internalise is that TIME_WAIT is protection, not overhead.

DeepExplain the difference between flow control and congestion control, and how the two windows interact.

Flow control protects the receiver; congestion control protects the network. Flow control is explicit: every ACK carries the receiver's advertised window, rwnd, saying how much buffer it has free, and the sender never keeps more unacknowledged data in flight than that. If the receiver stops reading, it advertises a zero window and the sender pauses, probing until space frees up. Congestion control is inferred, because the routers in between have no window to advertise: the sender maintains a hidden congestion window, cwnd, that it grows on success and shrinks on loss — slow start doubles it per round trip until a threshold, then congestion avoidance grows it linearly, and loss cuts it (AIMD). The amount the sender may actually transmit is min(rwnd, cwnd), so whichever is smaller governs. Practically: if throughput is limited and rwnd is large but cwnd keeps collapsing, you have loss on the path and a congestion problem; if cwnd is healthy but rwnd is small, the receiver's buffers or its read speed are the bottleneck. Linux uses CUBIC by default; BBR models bandwidth and RTT instead of treating loss as the sole congestion signal, which helps on lossy or over-buffered paths.

ScenarioEvery small request to a nearby service takes ~40 ms when it should take under 1 ms. Diagnose.

The suspiciously round, distance-independent 40 ms is the delayed-ACK timer, which means I'm looking at a Nagle + delayed-ACK interaction. One side is holding a small segment under Nagle's algorithm because it has unacknowledged data outstanding, and the other side is holding the ACK under delayed-ACK, hoping to piggyback it on a reply that isn't coming — so nothing moves until the ~40 ms timer fires and forces the ACK. I'd confirm with tcpdump: I'd see a small segment, then a ~40 ms gap, then the ACK, then progress. The fix is to set TCP_NODELAY on the latency-sensitive socket to disable Nagle, which is exactly why RPC frameworks and database drivers set it by default. If I can't change the code, TCP_QUICKACK on the receiver can help. What I would not do is assume the network is slow — the constant, sub-round-trip-independent 40 ms rules that out immediately.

Follow-upWhen would you choose UDP, and what are you taking on by doing so?

I'd choose UDP when the workload doesn't want TCP's guarantees or can't afford its costs: single request/response exchanges that fit one datagram where a handshake is pure overhead (DNS), real-time media and gaming where a late packet is worthless so retransmission actively hurts, high-fan-out fire-and-forget like metrics, and anything that wants to implement its own smarter transport in user space (QUIC does this over UDP to escape TCP's kernel and middlebox ossification). What I'm taking on is everything TCP was doing for me: if I need reliability, ordering, deduplication, flow control, or congestion control, I now have to build it, and congestion control in particular is hard to get right and easy to get antisocially wrong. So the decision is really "do I need these guarantees, and if I do, am I prepared to reimplement the ones I still need?" UDP isn't faster TCP; it's TCP with the guarantees removed and handed back to me.

RetainQuick revision

TCP builds a reliable, ordered byte stream on top of best-effort IP using sequence numbers, acknowledgements, retransmission, and windows. A connection is born in the three-way handshake (SYN / SYN-ACK / ACK), lives in ESTABLISHED, and dies in a four-way close (FIN / ACK / FIN / ACK). The active closer holds TIME_WAIT for 2×MSL (60s on Linux) to protect a reused 4-tuple from stragglers and to be able to re-ACK a lost final FIN — it's protection, not waste. Flow control (rwnd) protects the receiver; congestion control (cwnd, slow start + AIMD, CUBIC/BBR) protects the network; the sender is bounded by their minimum. Nagle plus delayed-ACK produces the classic ~40 ms stall, fixed by TCP_NODELAY. Most incidents are states gone wrong: TIME_WAIT/port pressure, accept-queue overflow, or resets from a middlebox timing out an idle flow. Read it all with ss and tcpdump. UDP is TCP with every guarantee removed — cheap, stateless, and yours to rebuild if you need any of them back.

The 10 things to remember

  1. TCP is stateful over stateless IP: every problem is "which state, waiting for what, why stuck."
  2. Handshake is three segments (SYN / SYN-ACK / ACK); close is four (FIN / ACK / FIN / ACK) because each direction closes separately.
  3. The active closer — whoever sends the first FIN — holds TIME_WAIT for ~60s on Linux.
  4. TIME_WAIT protects a reused 4-tuple from delayed duplicates and lets the final ACK be re-sent. Don't zero it.
  5. Flow control (rwnd) protects the receiver; congestion control (cwnd) protects the network; sender sends min of the two.
  6. Slow start ramps cwnd exponentially, congestion avoidance linearly, loss cuts it (AIMD). CUBIC default, BBR alternative.
  7. Nagle + delayed-ACK = the ~40 ms stall on small request/response; fix with TCP_NODELAY.
  8. Refused (fast RST) = reachable but no service; timeout (silent) = filtered/gone; mid-stream RST = peer or middlebox killed it.
  9. ss -tno shows states and timers; a listener with Recv-Q > Send-Q is overflowing its accept queue.
  10. UDP strips all guarantees for zero setup and no head-of-line blocking — good for DNS, media, QUIC; you rebuild what you need.
