Part II · Networking from First Principles · Chapter 15

DNS, end to end

"It's always DNS" is a joke because it's usually true. DNS is the indirection layer under every hostname you type, a distributed database walked one delegation at a time, cached at every hop, and wired into your OS through a config file most engineers have never read. Learn the resolution path and the caching rules and you'll debug in minutes what teams lose afternoons to.

Start: Beginner→→→End: Staff-level ~48 min read · Linux lab (Docker)

By the end of this chapter you can

  • Read /etc/resolv.conf — nameservers, search domains, and ndots — and predict what a lookup will do.
  • Explain caching, TTL, and negative caching, and why a "fixed" record can take minutes to appear.
  • Diagnose the ndots latency trap that plagues Kubernetes and the musl surprises that plague Alpine.

Why this exists · the problemThe problem DNS solves

IP addresses are how machines talk, but they're hostile to humans and hostile to change. Nobody remembers 93.184.216.34; everybody remembers example.com. Worse, addresses move — you re-deploy behind a new load balancer, fail over to another region, scale from one server to fifty — and if every client had hard-coded the old address, every one of them would break. You need a layer of indirection: a stable name that maps to whatever address is current, and a way to change that mapping without touching a single client.

That layer is DNS, the Domain Name System. At its simplest it answers one question — "what address is behind this name?" — but the way it answers is the interesting part. There is no single server holding all the names in the world; that would never scale and no one would agree to run it. Instead the namespace is a tree, and authority over each branch is delegated down: the root delegates .com to the registry that runs it, which delegates example.com to whoever owns that domain, who decides what www.example.com points to. To resolve a name you walk down that tree, asking each level "who handles the next part?" — and because the answers are cached aggressively at every step, the whole planet's lookups don't melt the root.

The indirection buys you everything: load balancing (one name, many A records), failover (change the record, clients follow), blue-green and canary routing, geo-routing (answer differently based on where the query came from), and clean service discovery inside a cluster. It also buys you a whole category of outages, because now a name that won't resolve looks exactly like a service that's down — and the two need completely different fixes.

Key point

DNS is a distributed, delegated, cached database. No server knows everything; each knows its own zone and who to delegate the rest to. Resolution walks the tree top-down, and caching with TTLs is what makes it fast and what makes changes take time to propagate. Almost every DNS incident is really a question about one of those three words: which server is authoritative, what got delegated where, or what's stale in a cache.

Mental model · analogyThe mental model: a chain of librarians

Real-world analogy

You walk up to the front-desk librarian (your recursive resolver) and ask, "where do I find www.example.com?" This librarian doesn't know — but that's fine, because their job isn't to know, it's to find out and remember. They keep a notepad of recent answers (the cache); if they looked this up two minutes ago and it hasn't expired, they just read it back to you instantly.

If it's not on the notepad, they start walking the directory, most general part first. They ask the root desk, "who handles .com?" The root doesn't know the address either — it only knows delegations — so it replies "go ask the .com desk, here's where it is." At the .com desk: "who handles example.com?" — "go ask example.com's own desk, here." Finally at example.com's desk (the authoritative server, the one that actually owns the answer): "www is at 93.184.216.34." The front-desk librarian writes that on the notepad with a use-by time (the TTL) and hands it to you. You never talked to the root or the TLD — your librarian did all the walking.

Two subtleties matter. If example.com's desk says "there's no such name," a good librarian writes that down too, for a while (negative caching), so they don't re-walk the whole tree every time you ask for something that doesn't exist. And if you ask for just "printer" with no domain, the librarian tries appending the office's known suffixes ("printer.eng.corp", "printer.corp") before giving up — that's the search list, and how eagerly they do it is ndots.

ArchitectureThe resolution path

The figure traces one cold lookup — nothing cached — for www.example.com. Notice that your machine sends exactly one query (a recursive request to its resolver) and gets one answer. All the tree-walking (the iterative queries) is done by the recursive resolver on your behalf. That division of labour is the whole design.

Why an SRE cares

Inside Kubernetes this exact diagram runs constantly, with CoreDNS as the recursive resolver and a twist: pods are configured with ndots:5 and a stack of search domains, so an innocent-looking external lookup can fan out into several failed queries first (Chapter 45). Knowing the path and the caching rules is what lets you tell "the service is down" from "the name won't resolve" from "the resolver is slow" — three incidents that look identical from the app's error log and have nothing in common underneath.

Internal workingRecursion, iteration, caching, and negative caching

Walk the cold lookup step by step, then see what caching changes.

  1. The stub resolver forms the query. Your app calls getaddrinfo("www.example.com"); the OS resolver reads /etc/resolv.conf, applies the search/ndots rules to decide the exact name(s) to ask, and sends a UDP packet to port 53 of the first configured nameserver, with the "recursion desired" (RD) bit set — "please do the whole job for me."
  2. The recursive resolver checks its cache. If it has a fresh answer (TTL not expired), it returns it immediately and we're done. On a cold cache, it starts iterating.
  3. It asks a root server "where is .com?" The root doesn't resolve names — it only knows delegations — so it replies with a referral: the NS records for .com (and glue A records so you can reach them). The resolver knew where the roots are from its built-in root hints.
  4. It asks a .com server "where is example.com?" Another referral: the NS records for example.com's authoritative servers.
  5. It asks an authoritative server "what is www.example.com?" This server owns the zone, so it answers authoritatively with the A record and a TTL.
  6. It caches every step and returns the answer. The resolver caches the A record, and often the intermediate NS records too, each for its own TTL. It sends one response back to your stub. Total: a handful of round trips on a cold cache, one hop on a warm one.

Recursive vs iterative is the distinction people muddle. Your stub asks for recursion — "give me the final answer." The recursive resolver satisfies that by making iterative queries up the tree, where each server it asks returns either the answer (if authoritative) or a referral to a more specific server. The root and TLD servers do not do recursion for you; they only ever refer. That's deliberate: it keeps the top of the tree stateless and fast, doing nothing but handing out delegations.

TTL and caching are where operational reality lives. Each record carries a TTL in seconds; a resolver may serve it from cache until that expires. Low TTLs (30–60s) mean changes propagate fast but generate far more queries; high TTLs (hours) mean cheap and fast lookups but slow, painful changes. This is why you lower a record's TTL well before a planned migration — so that when you flip it, clients pick up the change quickly instead of clinging to a cached old address.

Negative caching (RFC 2308) is the one people forget and get burned by. When a name doesn't exist (NXDOMAIN) or has no record of the requested type (NODATA), that failure is cached too, for a duration set by the zone's SOA record. The consequence: if you query a name a moment before it's created, the "doesn't exist" answer gets cached, and even after you create the record, lookups keep failing until the negative TTL expires. "I added the record but it's still not resolving" is, nine times out of ten, negative caching — not a mistake in the record.

Think first: you create a new DNS record, but clients still get "no such host" for several minutes even though a fresh dig against the authoritative server shows it. Why?

Negative caching. Something queried that name before it existed, so a resolver cached the NXDOMAIN/NODATA answer for the duration set by the zone's SOA negative TTL (often a few minutes to an hour). Your direct dig @authoritative bypasses that cache and sees the new record instantly, but clients going through the caching resolver keep getting the stale "doesn't exist" until the negative TTL expires. The fixes are patience (wait out the negative TTL), flushing the specific resolver's cache if you control it, or — for next time — keeping the SOA negative TTL modest so mistakes and new records don't get stuck for long. This is also why you should never query a name you're about to create.

/etc/resolv.conf — three lines that decide everything

The stub resolver's entire behaviour comes from this file. Three directives matter:

  • nameserver <ip> — up to three, tried in order. glibc moves to the next only when the first times out, so a dead first nameserver adds a full timeout to every lookup.
  • search a.corp b.corp — suffixes to append to unqualified names. Ask for web and the resolver may try web.a.corp, then web.b.corp.
  • options ndots:N — the threshold that decides whether to try search domains first or treat the name as absolute first. If the queried name has fewer than N dots, the search list is applied before trying the name as-is. Default is ndots:1.

The resolver library: glibc vs musl

The code that reads resolv.conf and does all this is part of your libc, and the two common libcs behave differently — the source of a whole genre of "works on Ubuntu, breaks on Alpine" bugs. glibc (Debian, Ubuntu, most base images) reads /etc/nsswitch.conf to decide the lookup order (usually files dns — check /etc/hosts first, then DNS), queries nameservers sequentially, and fully implements search domains and ndots. musl (Alpine) has no /etc/nsswitch.conf, queries all configured nameservers in parallel and takes the first reply, and — famously — versions before 1.2.4 (2023) did not fall back to TCP for large responses, so a DNS answer over 512 bytes could simply fail on Alpine while working everywhere else. musl also historically handled search/ndots more conservatively. The upshot: Alpine's tiny image size comes with resolver behaviour you must test, not assume.

Think first: a service works perfectly on Ubuntu but intermittently fails DNS on Alpine, and the failures correlate with responses that have many records. What's the likely cause?

musl's lack of TCP fallback (in versions before 1.2.4). DNS starts over UDP, and a response too big for the UDP buffer comes back truncated with the TC bit set, which is the signal to retry over TCP. glibc does that retry automatically; older musl did not, so the truncated answer was effectively a failure. Large responses — a name with many A records, a big TXT/DKIM record, DNSSEC data — are exactly what triggers it. The fixes: upgrade to musl ≥ 1.2.4, or shrink the responses, or (in Kubernetes) make sure EDNS0 buffer sizes and CoreDNS are configured so answers fit. The tell is that the failures track response size and only happen on the Alpine-based images.

Data flowFollowing one lookup, search domains and all

An app on a corporate host with search eng.corp corp and options ndots:2 calls getaddrinfo("api.internal"). The name has one dot, which is fewer than ndots:2, so the resolver applies the search list first: it queries api.internal.eng.corp (NXDOMAIN, cached negatively), then api.internal.corp (NXDOMAIN), then finally the name as absolute, api.internal. — three queries for one lookup. Now the same app calls getaddrinfo("www.example.com"): two dots, which meets ndots:2, so the resolver tries it as absolute first — one query, straight to the recursive resolver, which returns the cached or freshly-walked A record. Same machine, same config, wildly different query counts depending only on how many dots the name has. That single fact — that dots decide whether you pay for the search list — is the seed of the Kubernetes latency problem in the failure section, and you can watch every one of these packets with tcpdump port 53.

Lab 1Build — read resolv.conf and a dig answerBuild

Start where every lookup starts — the config — then read a real answer.

bash
cat /etc/resolv.conf
dig example.com A +noall +answer +stats
Output (example)# resolv.conf nameserver 192.168.65.7 options ndots:0 # dig answer example.com. 240 IN A 93.184.216.34 ;; Query time: 12 msec ;; SERVER: 192.168.65.7#53(192.168.65.7) (UDP)

Read the answer left to right: the name, the TTL (240 seconds — how long this may be cached), the class IN (internet), the type A, and the address. The footer tells you which resolver answered, on port 53, over UDP. Run the same dig twice in a row and watch the TTL count down — that's the recursive resolver serving it from cache, decrementing the remaining lifetime on each hit. When it hits zero, the next query re-fetches.

Lab 2Understand — walk the tree with dig +traceUnderstand

dig +trace does the iterative walk itself, from the root down, printing each referral — so you see the exact path the figure describes, live.

bash
dig +trace www.example.com A
Output (heavily trimmed). NS a.root-servers.net. ... # the roots com. NS a.gtld-servers.net. ... # root referred us to .com example.com. NS a.iana-servers.net. ... # .com referred us to example.com www.example.com. 86400 IN A 93.184.216.34 # authoritative answer

Four tiers, top to bottom: root gives you .com's name servers, .com gives you example.com's, and example.com's server gives you the actual A record. Each upper level handed back a referral (NS records), never the answer — only the authoritative server at the bottom knows the address. This is the single best command for "is DNS itself broken, or just my resolver?": if +trace works but a normal dig doesn't, your configured resolver is the problem, not the domain.

Lab 4Troubleshoot — the ndots latency trapTroubleshoot

Reproduce the Kubernetes-style problem: a high ndots plus search domains turns one external lookup into several failed ones. Rewrite resolv.conf the way a pod's looks, then capture. (If your /etc/resolv.conf is read-only, re-run docker run adding --dns 1.1.1.1 --dns-search dev.svc.cluster.local --dns-search svc.cluster.local --dns-search cluster.local.)

bash
cp /etc/resolv.conf /tmp/resolv.bak

# Shell A:
tcpdump -i any -n 'port 53' -c 8
# Shell B — a 2-dot name, which is < ndots:5, so search list runs first:
getent hosts api.github.com
OutputA? api.github.com.dev.svc.cluster.local. → NXDOMAIN A? api.github.com.svc.cluster.local. → NXDOMAIN A? api.github.com.cluster.local. → NXDOMAIN A? api.github.com. → 1/0/0 (finally!)

Four lookups to resolve one name — three wasted round trips against the search domains before the real one, because api.github.com has two dots and ndots:5 says "fewer than five dots? try the search list first." Why does this matter, and what's the fix?

Reveal the impact and the fixes

Each wasted query is a full round trip to the resolver — inside a cluster that's CoreDNS, and under load those extra queries multiply into real latency and real DNS server load. A service making thousands of external calls pays 3–4× the DNS traffic it should. The fixes, in order of preference: fully-qualify the name with a trailing dot (api.github.com.) so the resolver treats it as absolute and skips the search list entirely; lower ndots for workloads that mostly call external names; or use a dnsConfig/NodeLocal DNS cache to cut the cost. The reason Kubernetes ships ndots:5 at all is so that internal short names like service or service.namespace resolve through the search domains — it helps intra-cluster names and hurts external ones, which is the trade-off you tune per workload (Chapter 45). Restore: cp /tmp/resolv.bak /etc/resolv.conf.

No nsswitch.conf — musl doesn't use one, so tricks that depend on it (custom lookup ordering, some /etc/hosts behaviours, certain NSS modules) simply don't apply on Alpine. Now the senior questions to reason through before opening the answer: what else differs, and how would you prove a suspected musl DNS bug in production?

Reveal the senior view

The behavioural differences that bite: musl queries all nameservers in parallel and takes the first answer (glibc goes sequentially), so a fast-but-wrong resolver can win a race; musl before 1.2.4 had no TCP fallback, so responses over 512 bytes could fail silently; and musl applies search/ndots more conservatively. To prove a musl DNS bug I'd capture with tcpdump port 53 inside the Alpine container and compare against the same lookup on glibc: if the Alpine capture shows a truncated (TC-bit) UDP response and no follow-up TCP query while glibc retries over TCP and succeeds, that's the smoking gun. The pragmatic fixes are to base images on glibc (Debian slim) for DNS-heavy or large-response workloads, upgrade musl to ≥ 1.2.4, or keep responses small. Separately, to see caching: run dig example.com twice quickly and watch the TTL count down between calls — that decrementing number is the resolver's cache clock, and when it reaches zero the next query goes back to the authoritative server. Understanding that counter is how you reason about propagation delay during a migration.

Failure & troubleshootingHow this shows up in real incidents

Incident pattern · intermittent DNS resolution failures

Symptom: occasional "name or service not known" or slow lookups, not on every request. First cut: dig the name repeatedly and time it; check whether resolv.conf lists multiple nameservers. Confirm: failures/slowness align with one nameserver being unhealthy. Cause is usually: a dead or overloaded first nameserver — glibc waits for it to time out (default 5s) before trying the next, so every lookup that hits the bad one stalls. Fix: remove/replace the bad nameserver, tune timeout/attempts, or front pods with a local caching resolver.

Incident pattern · ndots latency in Kubernetes

Symptom: external API calls from pods are slow and DNS query volume to CoreDNS is huge. First cut: tcpdump port 53 in a pod during an external call. Confirm: each external lookup fans out into several NXDOMAINs against the cluster search domains first. Cause is usually: ndots:5 plus a not-fully-qualified name. Fix: trailing-dot FQDNs for external names, per-workload dnsConfig, or a NodeLocal DNS cache. This is a preview of Chapter 45.

Incident pattern · the change that won't propagate

Symptom: you updated or created a record, but clients still see the old value (or "no such host"). First cut: dig @<authoritative> vs dig through the normal resolver. Confirm: the authoritative server is correct but the resolver serves stale/negative data. Cause is usually: TTL not yet expired, or negative caching of a name you queried before creating it. Fix: wait out the TTL/negative TTL; for planned changes, lower the TTL well in advance.

Incident pattern · Alpine/musl DNS surprises

Symptom: a service works on Debian/Ubuntu images but fails DNS on Alpine, often tied to large responses or multiple resolvers. First cut: reproduce on Alpine with tcpdump port 53. Confirm: truncated responses with no TCP retry, or a parallel-query race. Cause is usually: musl's resolver behaviour (no TCP fallback pre-1.2.4, parallel nameserver queries). Fix: glibc base image for DNS-heavy work, upgrade musl, or shrink responses.

Production · security · performanceWhat matters in production

TTL is a planning tool, not an afterthought. Before any migration or failover that relies on DNS, lower the record's TTL days ahead so clients aren't clinging to a cached old address when you flip it — then raise it back afterward to cut query load. Treat the negative TTL with the same respect: a long one turns a brief "record doesn't exist yet" window into a long outage.

Cache close to the workload. The single biggest DNS reliability and latency win at scale is a local caching resolver near the client — systemd-resolved on a host, a NodeLocal DNS cache in Kubernetes, or a sidecar. It absorbs repeat lookups, survives brief upstream blips, and cuts round trips. Centralising all DNS on one distant resolver makes that resolver a latency tax and a single point of failure for everything.

Security touches DNS heavily. Plain DNS is unauthenticated and unencrypted over UDP, so it's spoofable and observable; DNSSEC authenticates records, and DoT (port 853) / DoH (over 443) encrypt the query. DNS is also a favourite exfiltration and command-and-control channel precisely because it's usually allowed out of every network — which is why monitoring DNS query patterns is a real security control. And CAA records let you constrain which CAs may issue certificates for your domain, closing off mis-issuance.

Common mistakes: hard-coding IPs to "avoid DNS" and then breaking on every failover; forgetting negative caching and thinking a new record is misconfigured; leaving a dead nameserver first in resolv.conf so every lookup eats a timeout; shipping DNS-heavy services on Alpine without testing musl's resolver; and setting TTLs so high that emergencies can't be routed around. Each is avoidable once you know the path and the caching rules.

System-design connectionWhere this reaches into architecture

DNS is the internet's primary indirection and load-distribution layer, so it shows up in almost every large design. Global traffic management — geo-routing users to the nearest region, weighted routing for canaries, latency- and health-based failover — is largely done in DNS by answering the same name differently depending on who's asking and what's healthy (Route 53's routing policies, Chapter 53). Service discovery inside clusters is DNS: a Kubernetes Service is a name that resolves to a stable virtual IP, and headless services return the pod addresses directly (Chapter 45). The TTL choice is a genuine design trade-off between agility and load that you make per record. And the "is it down or is it just DNS?" ambiguity is why good architectures separate DNS health from service health in their monitoring and why clients should cache and fail over sensibly rather than treating a resolution failure as a hard, permanent error. Get DNS wrong at design time and you inherit slow failovers and mystery latency; get it right and it's the cheapest global routing you'll ever deploy.

InterviewInterview questions & answers

Answer each in your head first.

30-secWhat's the difference between a recursive resolver and an authoritative server?

An authoritative server owns a zone's data and answers definitively for the names in it — it's the source of truth for, say, example.com. A recursive resolver owns no data; its job is to find the answer on a client's behalf by walking the tree from the root down, following referrals, and caching what it learns. Your device talks only to a recursive resolver; the recursive resolver talks to the authoritative servers. Root and TLD servers are authoritative for their level and only ever hand back referrals, never do recursion for you.

2-minWalk me through resolving www.example.com on a cold cache.

My stub resolver reads /etc/resolv.conf, applies the search and ndots rules to settle on the exact name, and sends one UDP query to port 53 of its configured recursive resolver with the recursion-desired bit set. The resolver checks its cache; cold, so it iterates. It asks a root server "who handles .com?" and gets a referral — the NS records for .com — because the root only delegates. It asks a .com server "who handles example.com?" and gets another referral to example.com's authoritative name servers. It asks one of those "what is www.example.com?" and, because that server is authoritative, gets the actual A record with a TTL. The resolver caches the A record and the intermediate NS records, each for its own TTL, and returns a single answer to my stub. So my machine sent one recursive query and got one answer; the resolver did all the iterative walking. A warm cache collapses the whole thing to one round trip, which is the entire point of caching.

DeepExplain TTL and negative caching, and how they affect a migration.

Every DNS record carries a TTL in seconds, and a resolver may serve it from cache until that expires, decrementing the remaining time on each hit. Negative caching (RFC 2308) applies the same idea to failures: an NXDOMAIN or NODATA answer is cached for a duration set by the zone's SOA record, so resolvers don't re-walk the tree for names that don't exist. For a migration this is central. Because clients hold a cached address for up to the TTL, I lower the record's TTL well before the change — say from an hour to 60 seconds a day ahead — so that when I flip the record, clients pick up the new address within a minute instead of an hour, then I raise it back afterward to reduce query load. Negative caching is the trap people hit: if anything queries a name before I create it, the "doesn't exist" answer gets cached for the negative TTL, and even after I add the record, lookups through that resolver keep failing until it expires — which looks like a broken record but is stale cache. So my rules are: pre-lower TTLs before planned changes, keep the SOA negative TTL modest, and never query a name I'm about to create. A direct dig at the authoritative server bypasses caching and tells me the real current state.

ScenarioPods make external API calls and they're slow; CoreDNS load is high. What's happening and how do you fix it?

This is the ndots trap. Kubernetes configures pods with ndots:5 and a stack of cluster search domains, and the rule is that a name with fewer dots than ndots gets the search list applied first. An external name like api.vendor.com has two dots, under five, so before the resolver ever tries it as an absolute name it appends each cluster search domain — api.vendor.com.namespace.svc.cluster.local, then .svc.cluster.local, then .cluster.local — and each of those is a failed round trip to CoreDNS before the real lookup succeeds. So one external call becomes four DNS queries, which is both the latency and the CoreDNS load. I'd confirm with tcpdump port 53 in a pod and see the NXDOMAIN fan-out. The cleanest fix is to fully-qualify external names with a trailing dot so they're treated as absolute and skip the search list; beyond that, a per-workload dnsConfig to lower ndots for external-heavy services, and a NodeLocal DNS cache to absorb and speed up the rest. The reason ndots is 5 in the first place is to make internal short names resolve through the search domains, so it's a deliberate trade-off that helps internal names and hurts external ones.

Follow-upWhy might a service resolve fine on Ubuntu but fail on Alpine?

Because Alpine uses musl instead of glibc, and the two resolvers behave differently. The classic failure is that musl before version 1.2.4 didn't fall back to TCP when a DNS response came back truncated over UDP — DNS starts on UDP, and a response too large for the buffer returns with the truncation bit set as a signal to retry over TCP, which glibc does automatically and old musl did not, so large answers (a name with many records, big TXT/DKIM data, DNSSEC) could just fail on Alpine while working everywhere else. musl also queries all configured nameservers in parallel and takes the first response rather than trying them in order, so a fast-but-wrong resolver can win, and it has no /etc/nsswitch.conf, so anything depending on that doesn't apply. I'd prove it by capturing tcpdump port 53 on both and comparing — a truncated UDP response with no TCP retry on Alpine is the tell. The fixes are a glibc base image for DNS-heavy or large-response workloads, upgrading musl to 1.2.4 or later, or keeping responses small. It's the archetype of "works on my image, breaks on the tiny one."

RetainQuick revision

DNS is a distributed, delegated, cached database that maps names to addresses so machines can move without breaking clients. Your stub resolver sends one recursive query to a recursive resolver, which — on a cold cache — walks the tree iteratively: root refers it to the TLD, the TLD refers it to the authoritative server, and only the authoritative server returns the actual record. Every result is cached for its TTL, and even failures are cached (negative caching), which is why new records can take minutes to appear. /etc/resolv.conf controls everything through nameservers, search domains, and ndots; a high ndots plus search domains turns one lookup into several, which is the Kubernetes latency trap. glibc and musl resolve differently — musl queries nameservers in parallel and, before 1.2.4, didn't fall back to TCP — which breaks DNS-heavy services on Alpine. Debug with dig +trace (the whole path), dig @authoritative (bypass caches), and tcpdump port 53 (ground truth on the wire).

The 10 things to remember

  1. DNS is distributed, delegated, and cached — no server knows everything; each knows its zone and its delegations.
  2. Your stub asks for recursion; the recursive resolver does the iterative walk (root → TLD → authoritative).
  3. Root and TLD servers only ever return referrals; only the authoritative server returns the actual record.
  4. Every record has a TTL; lower it before a planned migration so clients follow the change quickly.
  5. Negative caching means NXDOMAIN/NODATA is cached too — a "fixed" record can stay broken until the negative TTL expires.
  6. resolv.conf = nameservers (tried in order), search domains, and ndots; those three decide every lookup.
  7. ndots:N applies the search list first when a name has fewer than N dots — the Kubernetes latency trap; fix with FQDNs.
  8. Record types to know: A/AAAA, CNAME, NS, SOA, MX, TXT, PTR, SRV, CAA.
  9. glibc vs musl differ: musl queries nameservers in parallel and (pre-1.2.4) had no TCP fallback — Alpine surprises.
  10. dig +trace shows the path, dig @authoritative bypasses caches, tcpdump port 53 is ground truth.
