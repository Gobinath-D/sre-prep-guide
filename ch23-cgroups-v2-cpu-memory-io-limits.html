cgroups v2: CPU, memory, I/O limits

Namespaces stop a container from seeing the rest of the machine. cgroups stop it from eating the whole machine. This is where "the pod got OOMKilled" and "the service is throttled to a crawl at 30% CPU" come from — and where you'll fix them.

Start: Beginner→→→End: Staff-level ~45 min read · Linux lab (Docker)

By the end of this chapter you can

  • Explain the cgroup v2 unified hierarchy and the "no internal processes" rule without hand-waving.
  • Set cpu.max and cpu.weight and say precisely how quota differs from proportional share.
  • Read cpu.stat and diagnose CPU throttling from nr_throttled and throttled_usec.
  • Trigger and explain a cgroup OOM kill via memory.max, and tell it apart from memory.high.
  • Map Kubernetes requests and limits onto the exact cgroup files the kubelet writes.

Why this exists · the problemThe problem cgroups solve

You've packed twelve containers onto one node. Namespaces gave each of them the illusion of a private machine, but they all still draw from one real pool of CPU, RAM, and disk bandwidth. Nothing you've built so far stops one of them from spinning up forty threads and pinning every core, or leaking memory until the kernel starts killing things, or hammering the disk so hard that everyone else's writes stall. Isolation of view doesn't give you isolation of consumption. One noisy neighbour can still take down the whole node.

You need a way to say: "this group of processes gets at most 1.5 CPUs, 512 MB of RAM, and 50 MB/s of write bandwidth, and if it exceeds memory, kill it, not someone else." That mechanism is control groups — cgroups. A cgroup is a labelled bucket of processes that the kernel accounts and limits as a unit. The scheduler and the memory and block subsystems all consult the cgroup a process belongs to before granting it CPU time, memory, or I/O.

There have been two generations. cgroups v1 gave each resource (CPU, memory, I/O) its own separate hierarchy, which let a process sit in inconsistent places across controllers and made coordinated decisions hard. cgroups v2 replaced that with a single unified hierarchy: one tree, and every controller reads the same tree. That's what modern kernels default to, what systemd manages, and what Kubernetes now assumes. This chapter is v2 only; if you ever meet cpu.cfs_quota_us or cpu.shares, you're looking at legacy v1, and the concepts map but the files differ.

Key point

Namespaces and cgroups are the two halves of a container and they answer different questions. Namespaces: "what can this process see?" cgroups: "how much can this process use, and what happens when it wants more?" A container missing its cgroup limits is a perfectly isolated process that can still starve the node. Both, always.

Mental model · analogyThe mental model: metered utilities in an apartment block

Namespaces gave each tenant a private suite. cgroups are the building's utility metering.

Real-world analogy

The building has one electrical supply, one water main, one internet uplink. Management gives each suite a metered allocation. Electricity works two ways: there's a hard breaker — draw more than your amperage and the breaker trips (that's a hard limit, cpu.max and memory.max) — and there's a fair-share rule for when everyone's busy at once: if the whole block is maxed, power is split in proportion to each suite's rating, so a "weight 200" suite gets twice the amps of a "weight 100" suite, but only while there's contention (that's cpu.weight).

Water (memory) is different: there's a soft warning line where management starts aggressively asking you to conserve and slows your taps (that's memory.high), and a hard cap where, if you still won't stop, they shut off your suite entirely rather than let the building flood (that's memory.max and the OOM kill). And the internet uplink (disk I/O) is rate-limited per suite in megabits (that's io.max).

The detail that matters: metering is per suite, and suites nest — a floor can have a budget that its suites share. Meters attach to the leaves where people actually live, never to the hallway. That last sentence is the "no internal processes" rule, and it trips up everyone the first time.

ArchitectureThe unified hierarchy and its controllers

cgroup v2 is a single filesystem tree mounted at /sys/fs/cgroup (filesystem type cgroup2). Each directory is a cgroup; make a subdirectory and you've made a child cgroup. A process's membership is written by echoing its PID into that cgroup's cgroup.procs file. The controllers — cpu, memory, io, pids, and a few others — are the per-resource logic that reads the tree and enforces limits.

Two files govern how controllers flow down the tree. cgroup.controllers lists which controllers are available in this cgroup. cgroup.subtree_control is where you enable them for your children, by writing +cpu +memory +io. A controller must be enabled in the parent's subtree_control before the child even has the corresponding interface files. That's how delegation works: the root enables controllers for kubepods.slice, which enables them for each pod, which enables them for each container.

The no internal processes rule is the one structural constraint to internalise: a cgroup may either contain processes or distribute controllers to children, but not both — the root cgroup is the sole exception. That's why the tree bottoms out in leaves: processes attach at the leaves, and every interior node is pure structure. When you build cgroups by hand in the lab, this is why your first move is always to migrate your shell into a fresh leaf before enabling controllers on the parent.

Think first: you mkdir a cgroup and immediately try echo "+cpu" > cgroup.subtree_control, but it returns EBUSY. Why?

Because the current cgroup still has processes in it (your shell, at least), and the no-internal-processes rule forbids a cgroup from both holding processes and delegating controllers to children — outside the true root. Move the processes down first: make a leaf, echo $$ > leaf/cgroup.procs to relocate your shell, and then the parent is process-free and will accept +cpu in subtree_control. The kernel isn't being difficult; it's keeping accounting unambiguous, because a controller can't sensibly split a resource between "processes right here" and "these child groups" at the same level.

Internal workingHow each limit is enforced

Each controller hooks a different kernel subsystem. Here's what actually happens, per resource.

  1. CPU ceiling — cpu.max. The file holds two numbers, $MAX $PERIOD, in microseconds; the default is max 100000 (no limit, 100 ms period). Write 50000 100000 and the cgroup may run for at most 50 ms of CPU time in every 100 ms window — half a CPU. This is the CFS bandwidth controller: once the cgroup burns its quota for the current period, every runnable task in it is throttled — taken off-CPU until the next period starts. The limit is enforced per period, not smoothed, which is the root of the throttling latency problem you'll meet below.
  2. CPU share — cpu.weight. A single number, default 100, range 1–10000. It only matters under contention: when runnable tasks want more CPU than exists, the scheduler divides CPU between sibling cgroups in proportion to their weights. Weight 200 beside weight 100 gets two-thirds of the contested CPU. When there's spare CPU, weight is irrelevant — a low-weight cgroup can use the whole machine if nobody else wants it. This is the opposite of cpu.max, which caps you even on an idle box.
  3. Memory soft/hard — memory.high and memory.max. Both default to max. Cross memory.high and the kernel puts the cgroup under heavy reclaim pressure and throttles its allocations to slow it down — but it never invokes the OOM killer; usage can even breach the line temporarily. Reach memory.max and, if reclaim can't bring usage back under, the kernel invokes the OOM killer inside that cgroup and kills a process there. So high is a rubber wall that slows you; max is a brick wall that kills you.
  4. The OOM event trail — memory.events. When a kill happens, the kernel increments counters in memory.events: max (times usage hit the ceiling), oom (times an OOM was entered), and oom_kill (processes actually killed). This file is your proof, after the fact, that a container died because of its own memory limit rather than something else.
  5. I/O rate — io.max. Keyed per block device by $MAJ:$MIN, with nested keys rbps, wbps, riops, wiops. Write 259:0 wbps=52428800 to cap writes on that device at 50 MB/s. The block layer delays I/O that would exceed the rate. One wrinkle: buffered writes are charged when the kernel flushes dirty pages (writeback), not when your program calls write(), so throttling of buffered I/O looks laggy — direct I/O throttles immediately.
  6. Accounting is hierarchical. A child's usage counts against every ancestor. A pod-level memory.max caps the sum of its containers; a container that stays under its own limit can still be killed if the pod's shared budget is blown. The tree isn't decoration — limits compose downward.
cpu.max vs cpu.weight — the distinction that gets asked

cpu.max is an absolute ceiling enforced every period, even on an otherwise idle machine: quota exhausted means throttled, full stop. cpu.weight is a relative share that only bites when the CPU is contended, and imposes no ceiling at all when it isn't. Kubernetes uses both: your CPU request becomes weight (fair share under load), your CPU limit becomes cpu.max (hard ceiling). Confusing the two is why people set a low CPU limit "to be safe" and then can't understand why a barely-busy service is slow.

ComponentsWho writes these files in production

You will rarely echo into cgroup.procs yourself outside a lab. In production three actors own the tree:

  • systemd owns the top of the tree on most distros. It carves the machine into system.slice (daemons), user.slice (logins), and slices for whatever it manages, and it's the cgroup manager the kubelet and containerd coordinate with (the systemd cgroup driver). Fighting systemd for control of the tree is a classic misconfiguration.
  • The kubelet builds and owns kubepods.slice (or kubepods path) on a Kubernetes node. It translates every pod's requests and limits into the cpu.max, cpu.weight, and memory.max files on the right leaves, and it groups pods into QoS sub-trees. When you write a pod spec, you are, at one remove, writing these files.
  • The runtime — runc via containerd (Chapter 26) does the actual mkdir-and-write for a container's leaf, from the cgroup section of the OCI runtime spec, at the moment it starts the container.

Your job is usually to read these files during an incident — cpu.stat, memory.current, memory.events — and to set the right numbers in the spec that eventually produces them.

Data flowFollowing a Kubernetes pod's limits down to the kernel

You write resources: {requests: {cpu: 250m, memory: 256Mi}, limits: {cpu: "1", memory: 512Mi}}. Here's where each number lands. The kubelet takes the CPU request, 250m, and turns it into a cpu.weight on the container's leaf — proportional to the millicores, so the pod gets its fair slice under contention. It takes the CPU limit, 1 core, and writes cpu.max = 100000 100000 (100 ms quota per 100 ms period = one CPU). It takes the memory limit, 512Mi, and writes memory.max = 536870912. The memory request, by default, is used only for scheduling and eviction ranking — it isn't a hard cgroup limit unless you've enabled the MemoryQoS feature, in which case it becomes memory.min (protected memory) and a memory.high throttle line is set below the max. The pod's QoS class — Guaranteed, Burstable, or BestEffort — decides which sub-slice it lands in and how aggressively it's reclaimed or evicted under node pressure. When the container later exceeds memory.max, the kernel OOM-kills a process in that leaf, the runtime sees the process exit with signal 9, and the pod status reads OOMKilled with exit code 137. Every step of that is visible; the labs walk it.

Think first: inside a container with a 512 Mi memory limit, free -h proudly reports the node's 64 GB of RAM. At what point does the app get OOM-killed?

At roughly 512 Mi, not 64 GB. The OOM decision is made against the cgroup's memory.max, which the kubelet set from the limit — the number free and /proc/meminfo print is the host's total, because those tools read host-wide files that aren't namespaced. So "the container thinks it has 64 GB" is a reporting artefact, and any runtime that sizes a heap or a cache off free will happily allocate its way straight into an OOM kill long before it reaches what it believed was available. The truth lives in memory.max and memory.current under the cgroup — read those, and make sure your language runtime is cgroup-aware.

Hands-onLabs — build, understand, break, troubleshoot

These labs write to the cgroup v2 filesystem, which needs a privileged container on a cgroup-v2 host (Docker Desktop is v2). Start one:

bash
# --privileged so /sys/fs/cgroup is writable and dmesg is readable.
docker run --rm -it --privileged ubuntu:24.04 bash

# Confirm you're on cgroup v2 — you want the type 'cgroup2':
mount | grep cgroup

Lab 1Build — create a cgroup and enable controllersBuild

Make a leaf for your shell (to satisfy the no-internal-processes rule), enable controllers, then make a demo cgroup you'll limit.

bash
cd /sys/fs/cgroup
mkdir -p init && echo $$ > init/cgroup.procs   # move THIS shell out of the root view
echo "+cpu +memory +io" > cgroup.subtree_control  # now allowed: root has no procs left
mkdir demo
cat demo/cpu.max demo/memory.max              # the fresh defaults
Outputmax 100000 max

A brand-new cgroup inherits no limits: cpu.max is max 100000 (unlimited, 100 ms period) and memory.max is max. Because you enabled +cpu +memory +io in the parent's subtree_control, the demo leaf now has all the interface files — cpu.max, cpu.weight, memory.max, memory.high, io.max, cpu.stat. If you'd skipped the subtree_control step, those files wouldn't exist. That's delegation in action.

Lab 2Understand — cap CPU and watch throttlingUnderstand

Limit demo to one-fifth of a CPU, drop a busy loop into it, and read the throttling counters.

bash
echo "20000 100000" > demo/cpu.max     # 20ms per 100ms = 0.2 CPU
bash -c 'while true; do :; done' &      # a pure CPU burner
BURN=$!
echo $BURN > demo/cgroup.procs          # place it under the limit
sleep 5
cat demo/cpu.stat
Outputusage_usec 1003217 user_usec 1001880 system_usec 1337 nr_periods 51 nr_throttled 50 throttled_usec 3987541

Read this like a story. The burner wants a whole CPU but is capped at 0.2. So in almost every 100 ms period (nr_periods 51) it burns its 20 ms quota fast and then gets throttled for the rest — nr_throttled 50, nearly every period, for a total throttled_usec of ~4 seconds of forced idle over those 5 seconds of wall time. Confirm the ceiling held: top -b -n1 -p $BURN | tail -1 shows it pinned near 20% CPU. This is cpu.max doing exactly its job. Now change the number to 50000 100000 and re-read cpu.stat — throttling drops as the quota loosens. Kill it: kill $BURN.

Lab 3Break — trigger a cgroup OOM killBreak

Give demo a small memory ceiling, then run a process inside it that keeps allocating. Watch what the kernel does — and work out which limit killed it before you look.

bash
echo 50M > demo/memory.max
# tail on /dev/zero never finds a 'last line' — it buffers forever, growing RAM:
bash -c 'echo $$ > /sys/fs/cgroup/demo/cgroup.procs; exec tail /dev/zero'
OutputKilled

The process died with a bare Killed. But who killed it, and why exactly at ~50 MB? It wasn't the host running out of RAM (the host has gigabytes free). Reason about what you set, then confirm.

Reveal the cause

The cgroup OOM killer fired, scoped to demo, because tail's memory reached memory.max = 50M and reclaim couldn't claw it back. This is a local OOM — only processes in that cgroup were candidates; the rest of the machine was never at risk. The proof is in the event counters and the kernel log:

bash
cat /sys/fs/cgroup/demo/memory.events    # oom and oom_kill counters
dmesg | grep -i 'oom\|killed process' | tail -3
Outputlow 0 high 0 max 141 oom 1 oom_kill 1 [ 8123.55 ] Memory cgroup out of memory: Killed process 812 (tail) ... anon-rss:51200kB ... oom_score_adj:0

oom_kill 1 is the smoking gun: one process killed by the cgroup OOM killer. In Kubernetes this exact event surfaces as a container that exits with code 137 (128 + signal 9) and a pod status of OOMKilled. Note what it is not: it's not memory.high (that throttles, never kills — its counter stayed 0), and it's not the host OOM killer. Getting this distinction right is the whole game when triaging an OOMKilled pod: was it the pod's own limit (fix the limit or the leak) or node-level memory pressure (fix scheduling/capacity)? We go deeper on the memory side in Chapter 3 and on the Kubernetes side in Chapter 36.

Lab 4Troubleshoot — "slow service, but CPU is only 30%"Troubleshoot

The most misdiagnosed cgroup problem in production. A latency-sensitive service reports p99 spikes, but your dashboard shows its CPU averaging a comfortable 30% of its limit. Everyone says "it's not CPU-bound, look elsewhere." They're wrong, and cpu.stat tells you why. Reproduce the shape: a workload that does short bursts of heavy compute, under a modest limit.

bash
echo "10000 100000" > demo/cpu.max     # 0.1 CPU limit
# Bursty worker: 30ms of hard work, then sleep 70ms, forever.
bash -c 'echo $$ > /sys/fs/cgroup/demo/cgroup.procs
while true; do end=$((SECONDS+0)); t=0
  s=$(date +%s%N); while [ $(( ($(date +%s%N)-s)/1000000 ) ) -lt 30 ]; do :; done
  sleep 0.07; done' &
W=$!
sleep 6
cat demo/cpu.stat
kill $W

Average CPU use is low — the worker sleeps most of every 100 ms. Yet each burst wants more than the 10 ms quota allows, so it gets throttled mid-burst and its "30ms of work" stretches into the next period. The user sees latency; the average CPU graph looks calm. What in cpu.stat proves throttling is the culprit, and what's the fix? Decide before revealing.

Reveal the diagnosis and fix

Diagnosis: nr_throttled is climbing and throttled_usec is large despite low average utilisation. That combination — "low mean CPU, high throttle count" — is the signature of a workload whose bursts exceed the per-period quota even though its average is well under the limit. The CFS bandwidth controller doesn't care about your average; it enforces the quota inside each 100 ms window, so a spiky workload gets punished for its peaks.

Fixes, in order of preference: raise the CPU limit so a normal burst fits within one period; or, for latency-critical services, consider removing the CPU limit entirely and relying on cpu.weight (the request) for fairness — a widely used pattern precisely because throttling hurts tail latency more than it protects neighbours. Tuning the CFS period is possible but rarely the right lever. The key SRE reflex: whenever someone says "it's slow but not CPU-bound," check nr_throttled before you believe them. This is one of the highest-value single metrics on a container platform, and it's the direct link to Chapter 36 and the runbooks in Chapter 104.

Lab 5Production challenge — I/O limits and the requests/limits mappingSenior challenge

First, throttle disk I/O to see the third resource in action. Find your backing device's major:minor, then cap writes.

bash
lsblk                                   # find the backing block device, e.g. vda
cat /sys/class/block/vda/dev            # its MAJ:MIN, e.g. 254:0
echo "254:0 wbps=10485760" > demo/io.max   # cap writes at 10 MB/s on that device
bash -c 'echo $$ > /sys/fs/cgroup/demo/cgroup.procs
         dd if=/dev/zero of=/tmp/blob bs=1M count=200 oflag=direct'
Output200+0 records in 200+0 records out 209715200 bytes (210 MB) copied, ~20 s, ~10.0 MB/s

Without the limit that dd finishes in a second or two; with io.max it's pinned to 10 MB/s. (Use oflag=direct — buffered writes are throttled only at writeback, so the cap looks laggy otherwise.) Now the senior question, no shell required: for a Guaranteed pod (requests equal limits) with cpu: "1" and memory: 512Mi, which cgroup files does the kubelet write, and should you set a CPU limit on a latency-sensitive service at all? Reason it through.

Reveal the reasoning

The mapping. The CPU request (1 core) becomes cpu.weight on the leaf — the fair share under contention. The CPU limit (also 1 core) becomes cpu.max = 100000 100000 — a hard one-CPU ceiling enforced every period. The memory limit becomes memory.max = 536870912. The memory request feeds scheduling and eviction ordering (or memory.min/memory.high under MemoryQoS). Because requests equal limits, this pod is Guaranteed: it lands in the protected QoS slice and is the last to be evicted under node pressure.

Should you set the CPU limit? This is a genuine staff-level judgement call, not a rule. A CPU limit caps you even when the node is idle, so a bursty latency-sensitive service can be throttled while cores sit free — the Lab 4 pathology. Many teams deliberately set CPU requests (for fair scheduling and weight) but omit CPU limits on latency-critical workloads, letting them burst into spare capacity, and rely on requests plus the scheduler to prevent starvation. The counter-argument is predictability and protecting noisy-neighbour blast radius in dense multi-tenant clusters. Memory limits are different — you almost always want memory.max set, because unbounded memory growth OOM-kills someone, and you'd rather it be the offender than a random victim. "Always limit memory; think hard before limiting CPU" is a defensible default you can articulate in an interview and defend on a cluster.

Failure & troubleshootingHow this shows up in real incidents

Incident pattern · OOMKilled with a healthy-looking node

Symptom: a pod restarts repeatedly, status OOMKilled, exit code 137, but the node has plenty of free memory. First cut: read the container's memory.events — a nonzero oom_kill confirms it was the cgroup limit, not the node. Confirm: compare memory.current against memory.max just before the kill (or from monitoring). Usual cause: the limit is genuinely too low for the workload's peak, or there's a memory leak. The fix is right-sizing the limit or fixing the leak — never silently raising it forever, which just moves the wall. Distinguish this from node-level OOM, where the kubelet evicts pods by QoS class under host pressure; that's a scheduling/capacity problem, not a per-pod limit problem.

Incident pattern · CPU throttling masquerading as a downstream problem

Symptom: tail latency spikes; CPU utilisation looks moderate; the team chases the database and the network for days. First cut: cat /sys/fs/cgroup/<path>/cpu.stat or the container_cpu_cfs_throttled_periods_total metric. Confirm: high nr_throttled relative to nr_periods. Usual cause: a CPU limit set too tight for the workload's bursts, so it's throttled at the peaks that matter for latency even though its average is low. This is common enough that "check throttling first" belongs at the top of any container latency runbook.

Incident pattern · a container with no limits eats the node

Symptom: one workload starts, and every other pod on the node slows down or gets evicted. First cut: check whether the offender has resource requests/limits at all (BestEffort pods have none). Usual cause: a workload deployed with no resources block, so it has no cpu.max and a wide-open memory.max, letting it consume whatever it wants and starve its neighbours. The fix is enforcing requests/limits via LimitRange and admission policy — visibility isolation (namespaces) was never going to save you here; only cgroups do.

Production · security · performanceWhat matters in production

Right-sizing beats guessing. The two numbers that cause the most pain are the memory limit (too low → OOMKilled, too high → poor bin-packing and wasted capacity) and the CPU limit (too low → throttling and latency). Set memory limits from observed peak plus headroom, and revisit them as the workload changes. For CPU, set requests from steady-state usage so the scheduler and cpu.weight behave, and be deliberate — as argued in Lab 5 — about whether a CPU limit helps or hurts a given service. Autoscaling and VPA (Chapter 37) exist largely to take this guesswork off your plate.

Security. cgroups are also a denial-of-service boundary: without a pids.max, a fork bomb in one container can exhaust the node's PID space and take down everything; without memory and CPU limits, one tenant can starve the rest. In multi-tenant clusters, enforced limits are a security control, not just a performance knob. The memory.max that OOM-kills the offender is what keeps a leak from becoming a node outage.

Performance and correctness of monitoring. Two traps. First, some runtimes and older JVMs read the host's CPU/RAM instead of the cgroup limit and size their thread pools or heaps for a 64-core box they can't actually use — modern JDKs and Go runtimes are cgroup-aware, but verify. Second, plenty of tools inside a container report host totals (free, /proc/meminfo) rather than the cgroup limit, so "the container thinks it has 128 GB" is a reporting artefact, not reality — read the cgroup files for the truth. The metric that actually predicts latency pain is throttling, so put nr_throttled on your dashboards, not just utilisation.

System-design connectionWhere this reaches into architecture

cgroups are what make dense multi-tenancy safe, and dense multi-tenancy is the entire economic argument for Kubernetes. Bin-packing many workloads onto few nodes only works if the platform can promise each one a floor (requests → weight) and enforce a ceiling (limits → cpu.max/memory.max) so a single misbehaving tenant can't take the shared substrate down. That promise is the foundation the scheduler reasons about (Chapter 36), that autoscalers act on (Chapter 37), and that capacity planning models (Chapter 89). When you design a platform, the requests/limits policy — who must set them, what the defaults are, whether CPU limits are mandatory — is one of the most consequential decisions you'll make, because it sets the trade-off between utilisation, predictability, and blast radius for every team on the cluster.

InterviewInterview questions & answers

Answer each in your head first, then open it.

30-secWhat do cgroups do, and how are they different from namespaces?

cgroups meter and limit how much of a resource — CPU, memory, I/O, PIDs — a group of processes may use, and decide what happens when they want more. Namespaces isolate what a process can see; cgroups limit what it can use. A container needs both: namespaces for isolation, cgroups so it can't starve the node. cgroup v2 is a single unified tree under /sys/fs/cgroup that every controller reads.

2-minExplain cpu.max versus cpu.weight.

cpu.max is a hard ceiling: two numbers, quota and period in microseconds, like 50000 100000 for half a CPU. Once the cgroup burns its quota in the current period, every task in it is throttled until the next period — and that's enforced even if the rest of the machine is idle. cpu.weight is a proportional share, default 100: it only matters under contention, dividing contested CPU between sibling cgroups in proportion to their weights, and imposes no ceiling when there's spare CPU. Kubernetes uses both — the CPU request becomes weight, the CPU limit becomes cpu.max. The practical consequence is that a tight cpu.max can throttle a bursty service even at low average utilisation, which is why I'm cautious about setting CPU limits on latency-sensitive workloads while always setting memory limits.

DeepA pod is OOMKilled but the node has free memory. Walk me through what happened.

That's a cgroup-local OOM, not a node OOM. The container's usage reached its memory.max, the kernel tried to reclaim within that cgroup, couldn't get under the limit, and invoked the OOM killer scoped to that cgroup — so only processes in that container were candidates, and the rest of the node was never at risk. I'd confirm by reading memory.events: a nonzero oom_kill proves the cgroup limit did it, and I can see it in dmesg as "Memory cgroup out of memory." The container exits with signal 9, so exit code 137 and status OOMKilled. Root cause is either a limit set below the workload's real peak or a genuine leak; the fix is right-sizing or fixing the leak, not reflexively raising the limit. This is distinct from node-level pressure, where the kubelet evicts whole pods by QoS class — that's a capacity problem, and memory.events on the individual container would not show the oom_kill.

ScenarioLatency is spiking but CPU sits at 30% of the limit. Where do you look?

cpu.stat, immediately — specifically nr_throttled and throttled_usec, or the equivalent throttling metric. Low average CPU with high throttle counts is the classic signature of a bursty workload whose peaks exceed the per-period quota even though its mean is well under the limit; the CFS bandwidth controller enforces the quota in each 100 ms window, so spikes get throttled and stretched into the next period, and the user sees latency while the average graph looks fine. The fix is to raise the CPU limit so normal bursts fit, or drop the CPU limit on that latency-critical service and lean on the request/weight for fairness. I would not go chasing the database or the network until I'd ruled throttling out, because this misdiagnosis routinely burns days.

Follow-upWhy do people say "always limit memory but be careful limiting CPU"?

Because the failure modes are asymmetric. Unbounded memory growth ends in an OOM kill of someone — better it be the offending container (its own memory.max) than a random neighbour or the node, so a memory limit contains the blast radius. CPU is different: exceeding a CPU limit doesn't crash anything, it just throttles, and throttling a bursty latency-sensitive service can hurt its tail latency even when cores are sitting idle, because cpu.max caps you regardless of spare capacity. So the memory limit is protective with little downside, while the CPU limit trades latency for predictability — a trade you should make consciously per workload, often preferring CPU requests (weight) without a hard CPU limit on latency-critical paths.

RetainQuick revision

cgroups v2 is one unified tree under /sys/fs/cgroup that every controller reads. A cgroup is a directory; processes join via cgroup.procs; controllers flow to children through cgroup.subtree_control; and processes live only in leaves (the no-internal-processes rule). CPU has two knobs: cpu.max is a hard quota-per-period ceiling enforced even on an idle box, and cpu.weight is a proportional share that bites only under contention. cpu.stat's nr_throttled/throttled_usec reveal throttling — the cause of "slow at 30% CPU." Memory has a soft memory.high (throttle + reclaim, never kills) and a hard memory.max (OOM-kill inside the cgroup when reclaim fails), with memory.events recording the kills. io.max rate-limits a device by bytes/s and IOPS. Kubernetes maps CPU request → weight, CPU limit → cpu.max, memory limit → memory.max; an OOM there is exit 137 / OOMKilled. Always limit memory; think before limiting CPU.

The 10 things to remember

  1. cgroups meter resource use; namespaces isolate visibility. A container needs both, and one without the other is a gap.
  2. v2 is one unified tree at /sys/fs/cgroup (type cgroup2); every controller reads the same hierarchy.
  3. Enable controllers for children via cgroup.subtree_control (+cpu +memory +io); processes attach only at leaves.
  4. cpu.max = quota period (µs): a hard ceiling enforced every period, even on an idle machine.
  5. cpu.weight (default 100) is a proportional share that only matters under contention — no ceiling when CPU is free.
  6. cpu.stat's nr_throttled + throttled_usec are how you prove throttling; low CPU + high throttling = tight limit.
  7. memory.high throttles and reclaims but never kills; memory.max OOM-kills inside the cgroup when reclaim fails.
  8. memory.events (oom_kill) proves a cgroup OOM; in Kubernetes that's exit 137 / OOMKilled.
  9. Kubernetes: CPU request → cpu.weight, CPU limit → cpu.max, memory limit → memory.max; requests also drive QoS and eviction.
  10. Default posture: always set a memory limit; be deliberate about CPU limits because they throttle even when cores are idle.
