Part IV · Kubernetes — Architecture & Internals · Chapter 33

Workloads I: Pod, ReplicaSet, Deployment

A Pod is the thing that actually runs your container — and it's mortal, disposable, and never restarted in place. Stack a ReplicaSet on it for "keep N alive," a Deployment on that for "roll me to a new version without downtime," and you have the object you'll deploy 99% of the time. This chapter is how that stack behaves when it works, and how it fails.

Start: Beginner→→→End: Staff-level ~40 min read · kind

By the end of this chapter you can

  • Say precisely what liveness, readiness, and startup probes each do, and which one to reach for.
  • Trace a rolling update from Deployment to ReplicaSet to Pods, and control it with maxSurge and maxUnavailable.
  • Roll a Deployment forward and back, and read why a rollout stalled.
  • Diagnose the two probe incidents that dominate on-call: a bad rollout that won't progress, and a probe misconfig that restarts healthy pods.

Why this exists · the problemThe problem: containers die, and you can't babysit them

You know what a container is (Chapter 21) and you know Kubernetes reconciles desired state (Chapter 31). Now put a real service on it. A bare container has three problems the moment it meets production. It dies — the process crashes, the node reboots, the kernel OOM-kills it — and nothing brings it back. You want more than one copy for capacity and redundancy, and keeping "exactly five running" by hand across a fleet is a full-time job nobody should have. And you need to ship new versions many times a day without dropping traffic, which means replacing running copies a few at a time while the rest keep serving.

Kubernetes solves these in three layers, each owning one job. A Pod is one or more containers that share a network namespace and a lifecycle — the smallest thing you can schedule. A ReplicaSet keeps a set number of identical Pods alive, replacing any that vanish. A Deployment manages ReplicaSets to give you versioned, controlled rollouts and one-command rollbacks. You almost never create a Pod or a ReplicaSet directly; you write a Deployment, and it drives the other two for you.

The one idea that trips everyone up first: Pods are never repaired, only replaced. Kubernetes doesn't fix a sick Pod in place — it deletes it and schedules a fresh one, often on a different node, with a new IP. Everything else in this chapter follows from treating Pods as cattle, not pets.

Key point

A Pod is mortal and immutable in its identity: it gets scheduled to a node once and stays there until it's deleted. "Restarting a Pod" almost always means replacing it with a new Pod (new name, new IP). The controllers above it — ReplicaSet and Deployment — exist so that replacement is automatic, counted, and version-aware.

Mental model · analogyThe mental model: a shift manager and a staffing agency

Real-world analogy

Picture a shop floor that must always have five workers on it. You don't hire and fire directly. You tell a staffing agency — "keep five workers here at all times" — and the agency watches the floor: someone goes home sick, the agency sends a replacement without you asking. That agency is a ReplicaSet. The workers are Pods: interchangeable, and when one leaves, a different person shows up, not the same one healed.

Above the agency sits a shift manager — the Deployment. When you want to switch the whole crew to a new trained team, the manager doesn't send everyone home at once. It brings on a couple of new-team workers, waits until they're ready to serve customers, then lets a couple of old-team workers go, and repeats until the floor is entirely the new team. That's a rolling update, and "a couple at a time" is exactly maxSurge (how many extra you'll allow) and maxUnavailable (how many you'll let drop below target).

Three health checks run constantly. A supervisor taps each worker on the shoulder to confirm they're conscious — if not, walk them out and the agency sends a replacement (that's liveness). Before routing a customer to a worker, the host checks they're actually ready — logged in, till open — and only then sends work their way (that's readiness). And a brand-new worker gets a grace period to finish setup before either check counts against them (that's the startup probe). Confuse these three and you either restart healthy workers or route customers to workers who aren't ready.

ArchitectureDeployment → ReplicaSet → Pods, during a rollout

Here's the ownership stack and what it looks like mid-rollout. A Deployment doesn't own Pods directly — it owns ReplicaSets, one per version, and each ReplicaSet owns the Pods for its version. During an update you have two ReplicaSets alive at once: the old one scaling down, the new one scaling up.

Why an SRE cares

The two ReplicaSets are why kubectl rollout undo is instant and safe: the old ReplicaSet is still there at zero replicas (Deployments keep a history), so rolling back is just "scale the old one up, the new one down" — the same rolling mechanism in reverse. It's also why a botched image tag doesn't take you down if readiness is set correctly: the new Pods never become Ready, so the rollout stalls with the old Pods still serving. Readiness is the safety interlock of the whole system.

Internal workingPod lifecycle, probes, and the rollout

1 · Phases and container states

A Pod has a coarse phase and, underneath it, per-container state. Keep them separate — the phase is a summary; the container state is where the truth lives. The phases:

  • Pending — accepted by the API server but not yet running: waiting to be scheduled, or pulling images.
  • Running — bound to a node with at least one container started.
  • Succeeded / Failed — all containers terminated (0 exit / non-zero). Terminal for that Pod.
  • Unknown — the node stopped reporting (usually the node is gone).

Each container is Waiting, Running, or Terminated, with a reason. The reason you'll see most is CrashLoopBackOff — a Waiting reason that means "this container keeps exiting, so the kubelet is backing off before restarting it again," with an exponential delay up to five minutes. CrashLoopBackOff is not the failure; it's the kubelet's response to a container that won't stay up. The real cause is in the container's logs and its exit code — a missing config file, a failed dependency at startup, a panic. Chasing the backoff instead of the exit code is the rookie move.

2 · restartPolicy and init containers

A Pod's restartPolicy (Always for Deployments, OnFailure/Never for Jobs) governs whether the kubelet restarts a container in the same Pod. Note that restart here means "run the container process again inside the existing Pod" — the Pod object itself is never resurrected; that's the controller's job.

Init containers run to completion, in order, before any app container starts — use them for one-shot setup: wait for a dependency, run a migration, fetch a secret to a shared volume. If an init container fails, the Pod restarts it (per policy) and no app container runs until it succeeds. Native sidecar containers are the modern fix for the long-standing "sidecar problem": an init container with restartPolicy: Always, which starts before the app containers, keeps running alongside them, and shuts down after them. That ordering is exactly what a mesh proxy or log shipper needs. Native sidecars are on by default since Kubernetes 1.29 — before them, people hacked ordering with lifecycle hooks and it was fragile. Use the native form.

3 · The three probes, precisely

The kubelet runs three independent health checks against each container. They look similar and do completely different things:

  1. Liveness probe. "Is this container wedged?" On failure, the kubelet kills and restarts the container. Use it only for deadlock/hang detection — a process that's alive but stuck. Make it depend on nothing external.
  2. Readiness probe. "Should this container receive traffic right now?" On failure, the Pod's IP is removed from the Service's endpoints (Chapter 43) — no restart, no kill. Use it to shed traffic during startup, warmup, or a temporary dependency blip.
  3. Startup probe. "Has this container finished booting?" While it's failing, the liveness and readiness probes are held off. Use it for slow-starting apps (a JVM warming up, a large cache load) so a long boot isn't mistaken for a hang and restarted.

The single most important distinction: liveness restarts, readiness routes. A failing readiness probe pulls a Pod out of rotation but leaves it running so it can recover; a failing liveness probe destroys and rebuilds the container. Mixing them up is the source of the two worst probe incidents, which you'll cause in the labs.

4 · The rollout, mechanically

When you change a Deployment's Pod template (a new image, an env var), the Deployment controller does this:

  1. It computes a hash of the new Pod template and looks for a ReplicaSet with that hash. None exists, so it creates a new ReplicaSet at 0 replicas.
  2. It scales the new RS up and the old RS down in steps, respecting maxSurge (how many Pods above desired are allowed) and maxUnavailable (how many below desired). With the defaults (25% each) and 4 replicas, it can run up to 5 and drop to 3 during the move.
  3. A new Pod counts as available only after its readiness probe passes and minReadySeconds elapses. Until then the controller won't scale the old RS down further — this is the interlock that makes "wait until ready" real.
  4. When the new RS is at full replicas and the old is at 0, the rollout is complete. The old RS is kept (at 0) up to revisionHistoryLimit, which is what makes rollback instant.
  5. If new Pods never become available, the rollout stalls and — after progressDeadlineSeconds (default 600) — the Deployment reports ProgressDeadlineExceeded. One detail matters more than the rest: a stalled rollout leaves the old Pods serving; it does not take you down, provided readiness is honest.
Think first: your app takes 90 seconds to warm up. You set a liveness probe with a 30-second initial delay and no startup probe. What happens on every deploy?

An endless restart loop. The liveness probe starts checking at 30 seconds, the app isn't up yet, so it fails, and the kubelet kills and restarts the container — right in the middle of its 90-second warmup. It never survives long enough to finish booting, so it never passes liveness, so it's killed again: CrashLoopBackOff caused entirely by the probe, not the app. The fix is a startup probe that allows up to, say, 120 seconds (a generous failureThreshold × periodSeconds); while it's still failing, liveness is suppressed, so the app gets its full warmup. Only once startup passes does liveness begin. This is the textbook reason startup probes exist, and forgetting one is a top cause of "the app works fine when I run it, but crashloops in the cluster."

ComponentsThe pieces, named

  • Pod — one or more containers sharing a network namespace, IP, and lifecycle; the smallest schedulable unit. Mortal and replaced, never repaired.
  • Init containers — run to completion in order before app containers; one-shot setup.
  • Native sidecar containers — init containers with restartPolicy: Always; start first, run alongside, stop last (default-on since 1.29).
  • Liveness / readiness / startup probes — the kubelet's three checks: restart, route, and gate-during-boot respectively.
  • ReplicaSet — keeps a fixed number of identical Pods alive by label selector; replaces any that disappear. One per Deployment version.
  • Deployment — apps/v1; owns ReplicaSets, drives rolling updates and rollbacks, keeps revision history.
  • maxSurge / maxUnavailable — the two knobs bounding how far above and below the target replica count a rollout may go.
  • minReadySeconds / progressDeadlineSeconds / revisionHistoryLimit — how long a Pod must be Ready to count, how long before a stalled rollout is flagged, and how many old ReplicaSets to keep for rollback.

Data flowFollowing one rolling update

You run kubectl set image deployment/web app=web:v2 on a healthy 4-replica Deployment:

  1. The API server admits the change to the Deployment's Pod template (Chapter 32) and stores it.
  2. The Deployment controller sees the template hash changed, creates ReplicaSet web-v2 at 0, and begins the move.
  3. It scales web-v2 to 1 (surge). The scheduler places the new Pod; the kubelet pulls web:v2 and starts it. The startup/readiness probes gate it.
  4. Once that Pod is Ready and minReadySeconds passes, the controller scales the old web-v1 from 4 to 3. Endpoints for the Service now include the new Pod and exclude the terminating one (Chapter 43).
  5. Repeat — up one new, down one old — until web-v2 is at 4 and web-v1 is at 0. Throughout, at least desired − maxUnavailable Pods are serving, so traffic never drops.
  6. The rollout is complete. web-v1 stays at 0 in history. kubectl rollout undo would scale it back up and web-v2 down — instant rollback.

If web:v2 is a bad image and its Pods never pass readiness, step 4 never advances: the old Pods keep serving, and after progressDeadlineSeconds the Deployment reports the stall. That is the system protecting you — a broken deploy that fails safe instead of failing closed.

Lab 3Break — a readiness probe that stops all trafficBreak

Add a readiness probe pointed at a path that doesn't exist, and watch the Pods stay Running but leave the Service. This is the difference between "restart" and "route" made concrete.

bash
kubectl expose deployment web --port=80
kubectl patch deployment web --type=json -p='[{"op":"add","path":"/spec/template/spec/containers/0/readinessProbe","value":{"httpGet":{"path":"/nope","port":80},"periodSeconds":3}}]'

Look closely: the Pods are Running with 0 restarts — the readiness probe did not kill them. But READY is 0/1, so every Pod was pulled from the Service's endpoints, and the endpoint list is empty. Any client hitting the Service now gets nothing. If this had been a liveness probe instead, you'd see the restart count climbing and CrashLoopBackOff. Fix it and confirm traffic returns:

Lab 4Troubleshoot — a rollout that won't progressTroubleshoot

Ship a broken image and investigate as if you didn't know it was broken. Notice that the old Pods keep serving throughout.

bash
kubectl set image deployment/web nginx=nginx:v-does-not-exist
kubectl rollout status deployment/web --timeout=30s
kubectl get pods -l app=web
OutputWaiting for deployment "web" rollout to finish: 1 out of 4 new replicas have been updated... error: deployment "web" exceeded its progress deadline NAME READY STATUS RESTARTS AGE web-59ab-... 1/1 Running 0 8m # old version, still serving web-6cd7-... 0/1 ImagePullBackOff 0 40s # new version, stuck

The rollout stalled but the service didn't go down — three old Pods are still Running. Now find why the new Pod is stuck before you reach for undo.

Reveal the diagnosis and the fix

The status ImagePullBackOff is the tell, but confirm the reason from the Pod's events rather than guessing:

bash
kubectl describe pod -l app=web | sed -n '/Events/,$p' | head
Output Warning Failed ... Failed to pull image "nginx:v-does-not-exist": ... not found Warning Failed ... Error: ErrImagePull Normal BackOff ... Back-off pulling image "nginx:v-does-not-exist"

The image tag doesn't exist, so the new Pod can never start, so the rollout can never advance past the surge Pod — which is exactly why the old Pods were never scaled down and you stayed up. This is the safety interlock working. The fix is one command, and it's instant because the old ReplicaSet is still in history:

bash
kubectl rollout undo deployment/web
kubectl rollout status deployment/web
Outputdeployment.apps/web rolled back deployment "web" successfully rolled out

Undo scaled the last-good ReplicaSet back to 4 and the broken one to 0 — the rolling mechanism in reverse. The general lesson: when a rollout stalls, read the new Pod's events for the real reason (bad image, failing probe, insufficient resources, CrashLoop), and lean on the fact that a well-configured Deployment keeps the old version serving while it's stuck.

Lab 5Production challenge — design probes that don't cause outagesSenior challenge

Your service calls a database on every request. A colleague proposes a liveness probe that returns 500 if the database is unreachable — "so Kubernetes restarts us and we reconnect." A whole fleet runs this. The database has a 30-second blip. What happens, and how should the probes actually be designed? Reason first.

Reveal the approach

The proposal causes a self-inflicted outage that outlasts the blip. When the database is briefly unreachable, every Pod's liveness probe fails at once, so the kubelet kills and restarts every Pod across the fleet simultaneously. Now, just as the database recovers, the entire service is cold-restarting — dropping in-flight requests, re-establishing connections in a thundering herd, and possibly failing liveness again during the slow reconnect. A 30-second dependency blip becomes minutes of full downtime. The rule this violates: a liveness probe must never depend on a downstream dependency. Liveness answers "is this process wedged," full stop — a trivial "am I looping / can I serve my own health endpoint" check with nothing external in it.

The correct design uses all three probes for their real jobs. Liveness: a cheap local check (process responds, event loop not deadlocked), generous thresholds, no dependencies — so it only ever fires on a genuine hang. Readiness: this is where a dependency check belongs — if the database is unreachable, fail readiness, which pulls the Pod from the Service and sheds traffic without restarting it, so it recovers the instant the dependency does. If all Pods fail readiness during the blip, the Service has no endpoints and requests fail fast, but nothing is destroyed, and recovery is automatic and immediate. Startup: for the warmup window, so a slow boot never trips liveness. The mental model to state in an interview: liveness restarts, readiness routes — put dependencies behind readiness, never behind liveness. Clean up: kind delete cluster --name wl1.

Failure & troubleshootingHow this shows up in real incidents

Incident pattern · CrashLoopBackOff after a deploy

Symptom: new Pods cycle through Running → Error/CrashLoopBackOff, restart count climbing. First cut: read the logs of the crashing container — kubectl logs <pod> --previous for the last crash — and its exit code and events. Usual causes: a missing config/secret or env var, a failed dependency at startup, a bad command, or a liveness/startup probe killing the app mid-warmup (Pod-caused vs probe-caused — check whether restarts correlate with probe failures in events). Fix: the app or its config; CrashLoopBackOff is the symptom, the exit code and logs are the cause.

Incident pattern · a rollout stalls at ProgressDeadlineExceeded

Symptom: kubectl rollout status hangs, then reports the progress deadline exceeded; new Pods are Pending, ImagePullBackOff, or Running-but-not-Ready. First cut: kubectl describe the stuck new Pod and read its events. Usual causes: bad image tag, a readiness probe that never passes, insufficient resources to schedule the surge Pod, or a crash on start. Reassurance: a correctly configured Deployment keeps the old Pods serving during the stall — so triage calmly, then rollout undo or fix forward.

Incident pattern · traffic drops but Pods look Running

Symptom: clients get connection failures, yet kubectl get pods shows everything Running with no restarts. Tell: the READY column is 0/1 and the Service's endpoints are empty. Cause: a readiness probe failing fleet-wide — often a bad probe path/port, or a shared dependency the readiness check depends on going down. Fix: check the readiness probe config and the dependency; remember readiness pulls Pods from the Service without restarting them, so recovery is immediate once the check passes.

Production · security · performanceWhat matters in production

Probe design matters more than anything else here, and it's the easiest thing to get wrong. Liveness must be a trivial, dependency-free "am I wedged" check with generous thresholds — its only job is to recover a genuinely hung process, and a jumpy liveness probe restarts healthy Pods and amplifies incidents. Readiness is where dependency and warmup logic belongs, because failing it sheds traffic without destroying anything. Startup probes protect slow-booting apps from liveness. If you take one rule from this chapter: never put a downstream dependency behind a liveness probe.

Set the rollout knobs deliberately. For a zero-downtime service, maxUnavailable: 0 guarantees you never drop below the target during a roll (at the cost of needing surge capacity); maxSurge controls how fast and how much extra capacity you'll spin up. Use minReadySeconds so a Pod must be stably Ready before it counts — this catches Pods that pass readiness then immediately fall over. Keep a sane revisionHistoryLimit (10 is the default) so rollback works but you don't hoard hundreds of dead ReplicaSets. Always set resource requests so the scheduler can place your Pods (Chapter 36) — a Deployment with no requests schedules unpredictably and is first to be evicted under pressure.

Pair Deployments with a PodDisruptionBudget and honest graceful shutdown. A PDB (Chapter 36) keeps a minimum number of Pods available during voluntary disruptions like node drains, so a cluster upgrade doesn't take all your replicas at once. And make sure your app handles SIGTERM and drains in-flight requests within terminationGracePeriodSeconds — Kubernetes removes a terminating Pod from endpoints and sends SIGTERM, but if your app ignores it and gets SIGKILLed, you drop connections on every rollout. A preStop hook with a short sleep is the common trick to let endpoint removal propagate before the process exits.

Common mistakes: liveness probes with dependencies (fleet-wide restart storms), no startup probe on a slow app (probe-induced CrashLoop), maxUnavailable too high for a latency-sensitive service, no resource requests, treating CrashLoopBackOff as the problem instead of reading the exit code, and no graceful-shutdown handling so every deploy drops in-flight requests.

System-design connectionWhere this reaches into architecture

The Deployment is the atom of stateless scaling and progressive delivery. Because Pods are disposable and replaced, you scale a service by changing one number — the base of horizontal scaling (Chapter 95) and of the HPA that changes it for you (Chapter 37). Because the two-ReplicaSet rollout gives you controlled, reversible version transitions, it's the substrate every advanced deployment strategy builds on: canary, blue-green, and automated progressive delivery with flagged rollbacks (Chapter 73) are all "manage which ReplicaSet gets traffic, and how fast." Readiness probes are the contract that makes load balancers and Services safe — they're how a mesh or an Ingress knows a backend is actually serving, so they connect straight to the reliability patterns of timeouts and health-based routing (Chapter 88). And the discipline that Pods are cattle, not pets, is precisely what lets you design for node failure as a routine event rather than an emergency: a node dying is just some Pods getting replaced elsewhere, which is only survivable if nothing depended on those specific Pods. When this layer is set up well, deploys are boring and node failures are invisible; when it's set up badly, every release is a gamble and every dependency blip is an outage.

InterviewInterview questions & answers

Answer each in your head before opening it.

30-secWhat's the difference between a liveness and a readiness probe?

A liveness probe answers "is this container wedged" — if it fails, the kubelet kills and restarts the container. A readiness probe answers "should this container get traffic right now" — if it fails, the Pod is removed from the Service's endpoints but keeps running, so no restart. Liveness restarts, readiness routes. That's why dependency checks belong behind readiness, never liveness: readiness sheds traffic and recovers on its own, while a liveness probe with a dependency triggers fleet-wide restarts when that dependency blips.

2-minWalk me through what happens when you change a Deployment's image.

The API server stores the new Pod template. The Deployment controller hashes it, sees no ReplicaSet matches, and creates a new ReplicaSet at zero replicas. Then it performs a rolling update: it scales the new ReplicaSet up and the old one down a few Pods at a time, bounded by maxSurge (how many above desired) and maxUnavailable (how many below). A new Pod only counts as available after its readiness probe passes and minReadySeconds elapses, and the controller won't scale the old ReplicaSet down further until enough new Pods are available — so if the new version can't become ready, the rollout stalls with the old Pods still serving, and after progressDeadlineSeconds it reports ProgressDeadlineExceeded. When the new ReplicaSet reaches full replicas and the old is at zero, the rollout is done; the old ReplicaSet stays at zero in history, which is what makes rollout undo instant. The important property is that readiness gates progress, so a broken deploy fails safe rather than dropping traffic.

DeepWhy are Pods replaced instead of repaired, and what follows from that?

A Pod is bound to a node at schedule time and its identity — name, UID, IP — is fixed for its life; there's no mechanism to "move" or "heal" a Pod, only to delete it and create a new one. That's a deliberate design choice that makes the system simpler and harder to break: the controller's job is just "is the count/version right," and the answer to any Pod-level problem is the same primitive — delete and recreate. Several things follow. You can never depend on a specific Pod's IP or name, so you address workloads through Services and DNS (Chapters 43, 45), which gives you a stable front to an unstable set of backends. Node failure becomes routine rather than catastrophic — a dead node just means its Pods get recreated elsewhere. Local Pod state is ephemeral, so anything that must survive a Pod needs a PersistentVolume or an external store (Chapter 38) — and that's exactly why stateful workloads need StatefulSets (Chapter 34), which add stable identity and storage on top of this cattle model. And rollouts work by creating new Pods and deleting old ones, never mutating existing ones, which is why they're reversible and why image changes are safe. The whole ecosystem of Services, health-based routing, and horizontal scaling assumes Pods are fungible and disposable.

ScenarioA deploy went out and now clients get errors, but every Pod shows Running with zero restarts. What's happening?

Running with zero restarts rules out crashes and liveness kills, so I'd look at readiness immediately. I'd check the READY column — I expect 0/1 — and then the Service's endpoints, which I expect to be empty. That pattern means the readiness probe is failing fleet-wide, so every Pod was pulled from the Service while staying alive. From there it's two possibilities: the deploy changed the readiness probe itself (wrong path, wrong port, too-tight threshold), or the probe depends on something that's now down. I'd read the Pod's events and the probe config, and if it's a bad probe I'd fix or roll back; if it's a real dependency, readiness is doing its job by shedding traffic and will recover on its own once the dependency returns. The key reasoning is that Running-but-not-Ready with empty endpoints is a readiness story, not a crash story — and readiness removes Pods from rotation without restarting them, which is both the cause of the outage and the reason recovery is automatic.

Follow-upWhat are native sidecar containers and what problem did they fix?

A native sidecar is an init container declared with restartPolicy: Always. That small change gives it sidecar lifecycle: it starts before the main app containers, keeps running alongside them, and shuts down after them — with the kubelet restarting it if it dies. It's been on by default since Kubernetes 1.29. The problem it fixed is real and old: a service mesh proxy or a log shipper needs to be up before the app so the app has network/logging from its first request, and needs to stay up until the app finishes draining. With ordinary sidecar containers (just extra containers in the Pod) there was no ordering guarantee — the app could start before the proxy was ready, or the proxy could exit first and drop the app's final requests, and Jobs would never complete because the sidecar kept running. People worked around it with startup hacks and lifecycle sleeps, which were fragile. Native sidecars give the ordering and shutdown semantics as a first-class feature, so you should use the init-container-with-restartPolicy: Always form rather than the old workarounds.

RetainQuick revision

A Pod is the smallest schedulable unit — one or more containers sharing a network namespace and lifecycle — and it's mortal: replaced, never repaired. Its phase (Pending/Running/Succeeded/Failed) summarises per-container states, and CrashLoopBackOff is the kubelet backing off a container that won't stay up, not the root cause — read the exit code and logs. Init containers run to completion first; native sidecars (init containers with restartPolicy: Always, default-on since 1.29) start before and stop after the app. The three probes do different jobs: liveness restarts a wedged container, readiness pulls a Pod from Service endpoints without restarting it, startup gates the other two during boot — and dependencies belong behind readiness, never liveness. A ReplicaSet keeps N identical Pods alive; a Deployment owns one ReplicaSet per version and rolls between them a few Pods at a time, bounded by maxSurge and maxUnavailable, with readiness gating progress so a broken deploy stalls safely while the old Pods keep serving. The old ReplicaSet stays at zero in history, so rollout undo is instant.

The 10 things to remember

  1. A Pod is mortal and immutable in identity — Kubernetes replaces Pods, it never repairs them, so never depend on a Pod's name or IP.
  2. Pod phase is a summary; the per-container state and exit code are where the truth is.
  3. CrashLoopBackOff is the kubelet's backoff on a container that keeps exiting — a symptom; the cause is in the logs and exit code.
  4. Liveness restarts a wedged container; readiness removes a Pod from Service endpoints without restarting it; startup gates both during boot.
  5. Never put a downstream dependency behind a liveness probe — it turns a dependency blip into a fleet-wide restart storm.
  6. A ReplicaSet keeps a fixed number of identical Pods alive by label selector, replacing any that disappear.
  7. A Deployment owns one ReplicaSet per version and performs rolling updates between them; the old RS stays at 0 for instant rollback.
  8. maxSurge and maxUnavailable bound how far above and below the target a rollout may go; maxUnavailable: 0 for strict zero-downtime.
  9. Readiness gates rollout progress, so a broken image or failing probe stalls the roll while the old Pods keep serving — it fails safe.
  10. Native sidecar containers = init containers with restartPolicy: Always (default-on since 1.29): start first, run alongside, stop last.
