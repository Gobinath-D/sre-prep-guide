Part IV · Kubernetes — Architecture & Internals · Chapter 34

Workloads II: StatefulSet, DaemonSet, Job, CronJob

A Deployment treats Pods as interchangeable, which is exactly wrong for a database that needs a stable name and its own disk, a node agent that needs one copy per machine, or a batch job that needs to finish and stop. Three more controllers cover those three shapes — and each has a failure mode a Deployment never had.

  • Wire a StatefulSet to a headless Service and know why pod DNS names work.
  • Use a DaemonSet to run one Pod per node, and a Job/CronJob for run-to-completion and scheduled work.
  • Read completions, parallelism, backoffLimit, and CronJob concurrencyPolicy correctly.

Why this exists · the problemThe problem: not every workload is a herd of clones

The Deployment model from Chapter 33 is built on one assumption: Pods are interchangeable. Any Pod can serve any request, they can start and stop in any order, and none of them owns anything that must survive. That assumption is perfect for a stateless web service and wrong for three common shapes of work.

A replicated database breaks all of it. The primary and the replicas are not interchangeable — a client that connected to db-0 as the primary needs to keep finding that member, each member owns a specific slice of data on a specific disk that must follow it across restarts, and the cluster must bootstrap in order (the first member forms the cluster, the rest join it). A node agent — a log shipper, a metrics exporter, a CNI daemon — needs exactly one Pod on every node, including nodes that join later, which "keep N replicas somewhere" can't express. And batch work — a nightly report, a data migration, a queue drain — needs to run to completion and stop, with retries on failure, not run forever like a service.

Kubernetes gives each shape its own controller. A StatefulSet adds stable identity, stable storage, and ordered lifecycle. A DaemonSet runs one Pod per matching node. A Job runs Pods to successful completion; a CronJob creates Jobs on a schedule. Same reconcile machinery underneath (Chapter 31) — different guarantees on top.

Key point

The three controllers here exist to relax the "Pods are interchangeable" assumption in three directions: give Pods durable identity and storage (StatefulSet), pin Pods to every node (DaemonSet), or make Pods finish and stop (Job/CronJob). Each guarantee brings a matching failure mode — ordered rollout can stall on one bad Pod, and run-to-completion can retry itself into a pile of failures.

Mental model · analogyThe mental model: temps, named staff, caretakers, and errands

Real-world analogy

Go back to the shop from the last chapter, where a Deployment hired interchangeable temps — any of them, any order, nothing personal. Now add the other kinds of worker a real business needs.

A StatefulSet is your named, permanent staff. Each has an assigned desk and a personal locker (db-0, db-1, db-2, each with its own storage), you can phone any of them by name (stable DNS), and you onboard them in order — the senior forms the team before the juniors join, and if you downsize you let the most junior go first. Their lockers stay locked and reserved even while they're on leave (the disk survives a restart), and you don't clear a locker just because someone's out sick.

A DaemonSet is the caretaker on every floor: exactly one per floor, and when the building adds a floor, a caretaker appears there automatically — no headcount to manage, the rule is "one per floor." A Job is an errand with a definition of done: "post these 100 letters," and when they're posted the errand is complete and the runner goes home; if a letter bounces, they retry, but only so many times before giving up. A CronJob is the calendar that sends someone on that errand every night at 2 a.m. — and you have to decide what it does if last night's errand is still running when tonight's is due.

The StatefulSet is the one with real machinery, so the figure shows it: a headless Service in front, ordinal Pods each with their own PersistentVolumeClaim, and the stable DNS names those two facts produce. A DaemonSet, by contrast, is just "one Pod per node," and a Job is "Pods until done" — simple enough to hold in your head without a diagram.

Why an SRE cares

The per-Pod PVC is the sharpest edge in production. When you scale a StatefulSet down, Kubernetes deletes the Pods but keeps the PVCs by default — deliberately, so you don't lose data by fat-fingering a replica count. That means scaling db from 5 to 3 leaves data-db-3 and data-db-4 (and their disks, and their cloud cost) sitting around until you clean them up or set a retention policy. Half of "why is our storage bill climbing?" traces back to orphaned StatefulSet PVCs.

Internal workingIdentity, DaemonSets, and run-to-completion

1 · StatefulSet: stable identity and storage

A StatefulSet gives each Pod a stable ordinal identity: names are <set>-0, <set>-1, … and a Pod keeps its name and its storage across restarts and reschedules. Three guarantees flow from that:

  • Stable network identity. Paired with a headless Service (clusterIP: None), each Pod gets a DNS record <pod>.<service>.<namespace>.svc.cluster.local that resolves straight to that Pod. A normal Service load-balances across a random Pod; a headless Service lets clients address a specific member — which is what clustered databases need to find the primary.
  • Stable storage. volumeClaimTemplates creates one PVC per Pod (data-db-0, data-db-1, …). When db-1 is rescheduled to another node, it re-attaches its PVC — same data. The PVC is not deleted when the Pod is (that's the orphaning cost above), and by default not when the set scales down.
  • Ordered lifecycle. Under the default podManagementPolicy: OrderedReady, Pods are created one at a time in ordinal order (0, then 1, then 2), each waiting until the previous is Running and Ready. Deletion and scale-down go in reverse (highest ordinal first). Rolling updates also go in reverse ordinal order, and you can pause them at an ordinal with the partition field for canary-style updates. podManagementPolicy: Parallel drops the ordering when your app doesn't need it.

The persistentVolumeClaimRetentionPolicy field (stable since 1.27) lets you opt into deleting PVCs whenScaled or whenDeleted — the modern, explicit way to avoid orphaned disks instead of cleaning up by hand.

2 · DaemonSet: one Pod per node

A DaemonSet's desired state isn't a count — it's "one Pod on every node that matches." The DaemonSet controller watches nodes, and when a node appears (a scale-up, a new node pool), it schedules the Pod there; when a node leaves, that Pod goes with it. To land on control-plane nodes or otherwise-tainted nodes, the DaemonSet Pod carries the matching tolerations (Chapter 36) — which is why the CNI and kube-proxy DaemonSets, which must run everywhere, tolerate all taints. DaemonSets roll updates node by node with their own updateStrategy (RollingUpdate with a maxUnavailable, or OnDelete). This is how every node-level agent runs: log shippers, metrics exporters, the CSI node plugin, the CNI.

3 · Job: run to completion

A Job creates Pods and tracks them until a target number succeed. The knobs:

  1. completions — how many successful Pod runs make the Job done (default 1). With completions: 5, the Job isn't finished until five Pods have exited 0.
  2. parallelism — how many Pods may run at once. parallelism: 3, completions: 5 runs three at a time until five succeed.
  3. backoffLimit — how many Pod failures the Job tolerates before it gives up and is marked Failed (default 6). Retries are delayed with exponential backoff (10s, 20s, 40s…).
  4. restartPolicy — must be OnFailure or Never for a Job (never Always). Never creates a fresh Pod per attempt; OnFailure restarts the container in place.
  5. ttlSecondsAfterFinished — auto-delete the Job (and its Pods) this long after it finishes, so completed Jobs don't accumulate.

For finer control there's activeDeadlineSeconds (a hard wall-clock timeout), an Indexed completion mode (each Pod gets a fixed index, for partitioned work), and a Pod failure policy to decide which exit codes count as retryable versus terminal — so a Job can fail fast on a bad config (exit 1) but retry a transient network error.

4 · CronJob: Jobs on a schedule

A CronJob wraps a Job template with a cron schedule and, critically, a concurrencyPolicy that decides what happens when a run is due while the previous one is still going:

  • Allow (default) — let them overlap. Fine for idempotent work, dangerous if two runs step on each other.
  • Forbid — skip the new run if the previous is still active. Use for jobs that must not overlap (a report that would double-count, a migration).
  • Replace — kill the running one and start the new. Use when only the latest run matters.

startingDeadlineSeconds bounds how late a missed run may still start (if the controller was down past that window, the run is skipped), and successfulJobsHistoryLimit/failedJobsHistoryLimit cap how many finished Jobs are kept. CronJobs interpret the schedule in the cluster's time zone unless you set spec.timeZone.

Think first: you scale a 5-replica StatefulSet down to 3, then back up to 5 an hour later. Do db-3 and db-4 come back with their old data?

Yes — and that's the design. Scaling down to 3 deletes Pods db-4 then db-3 (reverse order) but keeps their PVCs (data-db-3, data-db-4) by default. When you scale back to 5, the StatefulSet recreates db-3 and db-4 with the same names and re-binds them to those same PVCs, so each comes back to exactly the data it had. That's the whole point of stable identity plus stable storage: a StatefulSet member is a durable thing, not a fungible clone, so shrinking and growing the set is non-destructive. The flip side is the cost from the callout: if you scaled down permanently and never cleaned up, those PVCs (and their real disks) linger and bill forever. If you want the disks reclaimed on scale-down, set persistentVolumeClaimRetentionPolicy.whenScaled: Delete — but then scaling down really does destroy that member's data, so choose deliberately.

ComponentsThe pieces, named

  • StatefulSet — apps/v1; ordinal-named Pods with stable identity, per-Pod PVCs via volumeClaimTemplates, and ordered create/delete/update.
  • Headless Service — a Service with clusterIP: None; produces per-Pod DNS records so members are individually addressable.
  • volumeClaimTemplates & persistentVolumeClaimRetentionPolicy — the per-ordinal PVCs and the (opt-in) policy for deleting them on scale-down or set deletion.
  • DaemonSet — apps/v1; one Pod per matching node, driven by node events, using tolerations to reach tainted nodes.
  • Job — batch/v1; runs Pods until completions succeed, bounded by parallelism and backoffLimit.
  • CronJob — batch/v1; creates Jobs on a cron schedule with a concurrencyPolicy and history limits.
  • podManagementPolicy — OrderedReady (default, one-at-a-time) vs Parallel (all at once) for StatefulSet Pod creation/deletion.

Data flowFollowing a StatefulSet from zero to three

You apply a StatefulSet db with 3 replicas, a headless Service, and a volumeClaimTemplate:

  1. The controller creates PVC data-db-0 and Pod db-0. It waits for db-0 to be Running and Ready before doing anything else.
  2. db-0's PVC binds to a PersistentVolume (dynamically provisioned by the StorageClass — Chapter 38), the volume attaches to the node, and the Pod mounts it. DNS record db-0.db.ns.svc.cluster.local now resolves to it.
  3. Only once db-0 is Ready does the controller create data-db-1 and db-1, then wait again. Then db-2. Ordered, one at a time.
  4. A clustered app uses this: db-0 bootstraps the cluster, db-1 and db-2 join by resolving db-0's stable DNS name. The ordering guarantees the primary exists before replicas try to join.
  5. Reschedule db-1 (its node dies): the controller recreates a Pod named db-1, re-binds it to data-db-1, and it comes back with its data and its name. To the rest of the cluster, member 1 simply blinked.
  6. Scale down to 2: the controller deletes db-2 (highest ordinal) but keeps data-db-2 for a possible scale-up — unless a retention policy says otherwise.

Every guarantee — the DNS name that stays put, the disk that follows the Pod, the strict ordering — is what turns "a set of Pods" into "a database cluster's members." Remove any one and clustered software breaks.

Lab 1Build — a StatefulSet with per-Pod storage and stable DNSBuild

Create a headless Service and a 3-replica StatefulSet with a volumeClaimTemplate, then observe ordinal names, ordered creation, and per-Pod PVCs.

yaml
# sts.yaml
apiVersion: v1
kind: Service
metadata: { name: db }
spec:
  clusterIP: None                 # headless → per-pod DNS
  selector: { app: db }
  ports: [{ port: 80 }]
---
apiVersion: apps/v1
kind: StatefulSet
metadata: { name: db }
spec:
  serviceName: db
  replicas: 3
  selector: { matchLabels: { app: db } }
  template:
    metadata: { labels: { app: db } }
    spec:
      containers:
      - name: web
        image: nginx:1.27-alpine
        volumeMounts: [{ name: data, mountPath: /usr/share/nginx/html }]
  volumeClaimTemplates:
  - metadata: { name: data }
    spec:
      accessModes: ["ReadWriteOnce"]
      resources: { requests: { storage: 100Mi } }
bash
kubectl apply -f sts.yaml
kubectl get pods -l app=db --watch    # Ctrl-C once all 3 are Running
kubectl get pvc

Ordinal names, one PVC per Pod, created in order. Now prove stable DNS from another Pod:

kubectl run dns --rm -it --restart=Never --image=busybox:1.36 -- \

bash

kubectl delete pod db-0

kubectl apply -f - <<'EOF'

Lab 3Break — a Job that fails and exhausts its backoffLimitBreak

Run a Job whose Pod always exits non-zero, and watch it retry with backoff and then give up as Failed.

bash
kubectl apply -f - <<'EOF'
apiVersion: batch/v1
kind: Job
metadata: { name: flaky }
spec:
  backoffLimit: 3
  template:
    spec:
      restartPolicy: Never
      containers:
      - { name: work, image: busybox:1.36, command: ["sh","-c","echo trying; exit 1"] }
EOF
sleep 90
kubectl get job flaky
kubectl get pods -l job-name=flaky
OutputNAME STATUS COMPLETIONS DURATION AGE flaky Failed 0/1 80s 90s NAME READY STATUS RESTARTS AGE flaky-abc 0/1 Error 0 88s flaky-def 0/1 Error 0 70s flaky-ghi 0/1 Error 0 40s flaky-jkl 0/1 Error 0 8s # backoffLimit: 3 → 4 attempts, then give up

With restartPolicy: Never, each failure creates a new Pod (that's why you see four Error Pods, not one with rising restarts), and the delay between them grows — 10s, 20s, 40s — that's the exponential backoff. After backoffLimit failures the Job is marked Failed and stops trying. Check why it's failed, the way you would in production:

bash
kubectl describe job flaky | grep -A2 Conditions
kubectl logs -l job-name=flaky --tail=1
OutputConditions: Type Status Reason Failed True BackoffLimitExceeded trying

The condition BackoffLimitExceeded is the fingerprint of "the Job gave up," and the Pod logs and exit code tell you the real cause. Left with restartPolicy: Never and no ttlSecondsAfterFinished, those failed Pods stick around — which is how a flaky CronJob quietly buries a namespace in dead Pods.

Lab 4Troubleshoot — a stuck StatefulSet Pod halts the rolloutTroubleshoot

Break the middle ordinal and watch the ordered guarantee turn into a stall: nothing past the broken Pod updates. Trigger a rolling update with an image that won't start on db-1 onward by pointing the whole set at a bad image.

bash
kubectl set image statefulset/db web=nginx:v-nope
kubectl rollout status statefulset/db --timeout=30s
kubectl get pods -l app=db
OutputWaiting for partitioned roll out to finish: 0 out of 3 new pods have been updated... error: timed out waiting for the condition NAME READY STATUS RESTARTS AGE db-0 1/1 Running 0 20m # still old image db-1 1/1 Running 0 20m # still old image db-2 0/1 ImagePullBackOff 0 40s # highest ordinal, updated first, stuck

StatefulSet rolling updates go in reverse ordinal order, so db-2 was updated first — and it's stuck on the bad image. Now the important part: db-1 and db-0 are frozen on the old version and won't update. Why, and what's the safe recovery? Reason before revealing.

Reveal the diagnosis and the fix

The default podManagementPolicy: OrderedReady means the StatefulSet will not update the next ordinal until the current one is Running and Ready. db-2 can never become Ready (its image doesn't exist), so the controller refuses to touch db-1 — the rollout is blocked at db-2 forever. This is the guarantee working as designed: for a database you would not want the controller to keep rolling and take down a second member while the first is broken. But it means a single bad ordinal wedges the whole update. Confirm the cause on the stuck Pod, then roll back:

bash
kubectl describe pod db-2 | grep -A2 Events | head
kubectl rollout undo statefulset/db
kubectl rollout status statefulset/db --timeout=60s
Output Warning Failed Failed to pull image "nginx:v-nope": not found statefulset rolling update rolled back partitioned roll out complete: 3 new pods have been updated...

The distinguishing lesson from Chapter 33's Deployment stall: a Deployment stalls but the old Pods keep fully serving; a StatefulSet stall freezes the whole set at the failed ordinal, with the higher ordinals down and the lower ones un-updated. That's stricter and can be scarier during an incident, so for StatefulSets you lean even harder on testing images before rollout, and you use the partition field to canary one ordinal at a time. If a single member is genuinely stuck (bad node, corrupt disk) rather than a bad image, you delete that Pod (and, if needed, its PVC) so it's recreated cleanly — but only after confirming the data is safe or replicated.

Lab 5Production challenge — a CronJob that overlaps and piles upSenior challenge

A nightly CronJob runs a report that usually takes 5 minutes but occasionally takes 90 when the source database is slow. It's configured with the default settings and no history limits. Over a bad week it created dozens of overlapping Jobs, doubled some numbers in the report, and left hundreds of Pods behind. Diagnose what's wrong with the defaults and specify the correct configuration. Reason first.

Reveal the approach

Three defaults are biting. First, concurrencyPolicy defaults to Allow, so when a 90-minute run is still going and the next nightly trigger fires, a second report starts on the same data — that's the double-counting. A report that reads shared state and must not overlap needs concurrencyPolicy: Forbid (skip the new run while one is active) or Replace (kill the old, keep only the latest) if only the freshest result matters. Second, there are no history limits, and if the Job template uses restartPolicy: Never, every finished and failed Job keeps its Pods around — hence hundreds of dead Pods. Set successfulJobsHistoryLimit and failedJobsHistoryLimit to small numbers, and put ttlSecondsAfterFinished on the Job template so finished Jobs and their Pods are garbage-collected automatically. Third, there's no bound on a runaway run: add activeDeadlineSeconds so a report that hangs past a sane limit is killed rather than blocking the next window forever, and set a realistic backoffLimit so a hard failure doesn't retry endlessly.

The corrected CronJob: concurrencyPolicy: Forbid, a startingDeadlineSeconds so a run missed because the controller was briefly down is skipped rather than fired late in a burst, activeDeadlineSeconds to cap runtime, and history/TTL limits so Pods don't accumulate. The general principle: CronJobs fail in the seams between runs — overlap, pileup, missed schedules — so the settings that matter most are the ones governing what happens when a run is late, long, or still going. Clean up: kind delete cluster --name wl2.

Failure & troubleshootingHow this shows up in real incidents

Incident pattern · a StatefulSet rollout frozen at one ordinal

Symptom: a StatefulSet update stops partway — a high ordinal is down (ImagePullBackOff, CrashLoop, unschedulable) and the lower ordinals are stuck on the old version, not updating. Cause: OrderedReady won't proceed until the current ordinal is Ready, so one bad Pod wedges the whole set. First cut: kubectl describe the stuck ordinal for the real reason (image, resources, PVC not binding, node gone). Fix: roll back or fix the image; if a single member is genuinely stuck, delete that Pod (and its PVC only if the data is replicated/safe) to force a clean recreate — never blanket-delete PVCs on a StatefulSet.

Incident pattern · a Job or CronJob piling up failed Pods

Symptom: a namespace fills with Error/Completed Pods; a CronJob has dozens of active or leftover Jobs. Causes: restartPolicy: Never with no ttlSecondsAfterFinished (dead Pods never cleaned up), concurrencyPolicy: Allow with runs that outlast their interval (overlap pileup), or a Job retrying to its backoffLimit repeatedly. First cut: kubectl get jobs and the CronJob's lastScheduleTime/active count. Fix: set TTL and history limits, choose Forbid/Replace for non-overlapping work, and read the Pod logs for the underlying failure.

Incident pattern · orphaned StatefulSet PVCs and storage cost

Symptom: storage spend climbs; there are PVCs like data-db-7 for a set that's only 3 replicas now. Cause: scaling a StatefulSet down keeps the PVCs by default, so shrunk sets leave disks behind. Confirm: kubectl get pvc and match ordinals to current replicas. Fix: delete the orphaned PVCs deliberately, or set persistentVolumeClaimRetentionPolicy so scale-down reclaims them — understanding that then scale-down is destructive.

Production · security · performanceWhat matters in production

StatefulSets need PVC lifecycle discipline. The per-Pod PVC is the feature and the trap: it's why members keep their data, and why scaling down silently leaves disks and cost behind. Decide your persistentVolumeClaimRetentionPolicy up front — keep PVCs (the safe default) and clean up manually, or reclaim on scale-down and accept that shrinking destroys that member's data. Use the partition field to canary updates one ordinal at a time on critical data stores, and remember that OrderedReady makes a single bad ordinal freeze the whole rollout — so test images hard before you roll a database. A headless Service is mandatory for stable DNS; forgetting it is why "the replicas can't find the primary."

DaemonSets touch every node, so their resource footprint multiplies. A DaemonSet that requests 200Mi runs on 500 nodes and that's 100Gi cluster-wide — size requests and limits carefully, because a node agent that leaks memory takes down node after node. Give DaemonSets appropriate tolerations if they must run on control-plane or tainted nodes, and a sensible updateStrategy maxUnavailable so a rolling update of a critical agent (CNI, CSI) doesn't disrupt too many nodes at once. Because DaemonSet Pods are often infrastructure, they frequently need broader permissions than a normal workload — scope those tightly (a metrics exporter does not need host root).

Jobs and CronJobs fail in the gaps. Always set ttlSecondsAfterFinished so finished Jobs don't accumulate, a realistic backoffLimit so a hard failure doesn't retry forever, and activeDeadlineSeconds so a hung run is killed. For CronJobs, choose concurrencyPolicy based on whether overlap is safe — Forbid for anything touching shared state — and set startingDeadlineSeconds so a run missed during a controller outage is skipped rather than fired in a thundering catch-up burst. Set spec.timeZone explicitly if your schedule is business-time-sensitive. Make Job work idempotent: a Job can run its Pod more than once (retries, node failure mid-run), so "process these records" must tolerate being partially re-run.

Common mistakes: a StatefulSet without a headless Service; blanket-deleting StatefulSet PVCs and losing data; a DaemonSet with unbounded resources multiplying across the fleet; Jobs with no TTL burying namespaces in dead Pods; a CronJob left on Allow that overlaps and double-processes; and non-idempotent batch work that corrupts data when a Job retries.

System-design connectionWhere this reaches into architecture

These controllers are where "run it on Kubernetes" meets the hard parts of system design. StatefulSets are the on-cluster answer to running stateful systems — databases, message brokers, quorum-based coordination stores — and the choice to run those on Kubernetes at all is a real design decision (Chapter 86): you get stable identity, storage, and ordered lifecycle, but you inherit the operational weight of running a database, which is why many teams use a managed data service instead and keep only stateless workloads on the cluster. When you do run stateful workloads, the ordinal-plus-headless-Service pattern is exactly how distributed systems bootstrap and discover peers (Chapter 83), and the per-Pod PVC ties directly into the storage layer (Chapter 38). Jobs and CronJobs are the primitive under batch and asynchronous processing — the queue-worker and scheduled-pipeline patterns of Chapter 98 — where the design questions are all about idempotency, retries, and what happens when a worker dies mid-task, which are precisely the knobs this chapter names. DaemonSets are the deployment model for the platform's own nervous system: the observability agents, log shippers, CNI, and CSI node plugins that must exist on every node are all DaemonSets, so understanding "one Pod per node, follows node lifecycle" is understanding how the cluster instruments itself (Chapter 78). Pick the wrong controller — a Deployment for a database, a Job that isn't idempotent, a CronJob on Allow for non-overlapping work — and the failure isn't a crash, it's silent data corruption or runaway cost.

InterviewInterview questions & answers

Answer each in your head before opening it.

30-secWhen would you use a StatefulSet instead of a Deployment?

When Pods aren't interchangeable — when each needs a stable identity, its own durable storage, or an ordered lifecycle. Databases and other clustered stateful systems are the classic case: a member needs a stable DNS name so peers can find it, its own PVC that follows it across restarts so its data survives, and ordered startup so the cluster bootstraps correctly. A Deployment gives you none of that — its Pods are fungible with random names and shared or no storage — so it's right for stateless services and wrong for anything that owns data or has per-instance identity.

2-minExplain how a StatefulSet provides stable identity and storage.

Two mechanisms. For identity, Pods are named by ordinal — db-0, db-1, db-2 — and a Pod keeps its name across restarts and reschedules. Paired with a headless Service (clusterIP: None), each Pod also gets a DNS record pod.service.namespace.svc.cluster.local that resolves directly to that specific Pod, so peers can address individual members rather than a random one behind a load balancer. For storage, volumeClaimTemplates creates one PVC per ordinal — data-db-0, data-db-1 — and when a Pod is rescheduled, the StatefulSet re-binds it to its PVC, so the data follows the identity. The lifecycle is ordered too: under the default OrderedReady policy, Pods are created 0 through N-1 one at a time, each waiting until the previous is Ready, and deleted and updated in reverse. The one gotcha I'd flag: scaling down keeps the PVCs by default, so orphaned disks accumulate unless you clean them up or set a retention policy — and the ordered-Ready guarantee means one un-Ready ordinal freezes the whole rollout.

DeepExplain completions, parallelism, and backoffLimit, and why Job idempotency matters.

completions is how many Pods must exit successfully for the Job to be done; parallelism is how many may run at once. So completions: 10, parallelism: 3 runs three Pods at a time until ten have succeeded — a simple work-fan-out. backoffLimit is how many Pod failures the Job tolerates before it's marked Failed, with exponential backoff between retries; with restartPolicy: Never each retry is a fresh Pod, with OnFailure the container restarts in place. Idempotency matters because a Job can run the same unit of work more than once even when everything is "working": a Pod can fail after doing half its side effects and get retried, or a node can die mid-run and the Pod is rescheduled, or in indexed mode a completion can be re-attempted. If the work isn't idempotent — say it appends to a ledger or sends emails without a dedup key — those re-runs double-charge, double-send, or corrupt data. So batch work must be written so that running it twice on the same input produces the same result as running it once, typically with idempotency keys, upserts, or checkpointing. For finer failure handling there's a Pod failure policy to treat some exit codes as terminal (fail fast on a bad config) and others as retryable (transient network), plus activeDeadlineSeconds as a hard timeout and ttlSecondsAfterFinished for cleanup.

ScenarioA StatefulSet update is stuck: the top ordinal is in ImagePullBackOff and the others haven't updated. What's happening and what do you do?

The default OrderedReady policy updates ordinals in reverse and won't move to the next until the current one is Running and Ready. The top ordinal can't become Ready because its image doesn't exist, so the whole rollout is frozen there — and that's by design, because for a stateful cluster you don't want the controller to keep taking members down while one is already broken. My first move is to confirm the real cause on the stuck Pod with kubectl describe — image typo, registry auth, resources, PVC not binding — rather than assume. If it's a bad image or config, I roll back with kubectl rollout undo statefulset/..., which is safe because the update never progressed past the top ordinal, so the lower members are untouched. If instead a single member is stuck for a node or storage reason, I'd delete that specific Pod so it's recreated cleanly, and only delete its PVC if I've confirmed the data is safe or replicated elsewhere — never blanket-delete StatefulSet PVCs. The contrast I'd draw with a Deployment: a Deployment stall leaves the old Pods fully serving, but a StatefulSet stall freezes the set with the top member down, so it's stricter and warrants canarying with the partition field and testing images before rollout.

Follow-upHow does a DaemonSet decide how many Pods to run, and how do you make one run on control-plane nodes?

A DaemonSet has no replica count — its desired state is "one Pod per node that matches its node selector and tolerations." The controller watches the node list, and the count is derived: two schedulable nodes means two Pods, add a node and a third Pod appears there automatically, remove a node and its Pod goes away. That's exactly what node agents need — log shippers, metrics exporters, the CNI and CSI node plugins — because they must be present wherever workloads run, including nodes that join later. To run on control-plane nodes, which carry a taint like node-role.kubernetes.io/control-plane:NoSchedule, the DaemonSet Pod needs a matching toleration; the CNI and kube-proxy DaemonSets tolerate essentially all taints because they have to run everywhere for the cluster to function at all. You can also narrow a DaemonSet to a subset of nodes with a nodeSelector or node affinity — say, only GPU nodes for a GPU device plugin. Because DaemonSet Pods land on every node, their resource requests multiply across the fleet, so sizing them is a real capacity concern.

RetainQuick revision

Three controllers relax the "Pods are interchangeable" assumption. A StatefulSet gives Pods stable ordinal identity (db-0, db-1), stable per-Pod storage via volumeClaimTemplates, and ordered create/delete/update; paired with a headless Service it produces per-Pod DNS so clustered software can find specific members. Scale-down keeps PVCs by default (orphaned-disk cost) and OrderedReady means one un-Ready ordinal freezes the whole rollout. A DaemonSet runs one Pod per matching node, derived from the node list, using tolerations to reach tainted nodes — the model for every node agent. A Job runs Pods until completions succeed, bounded by parallelism and backoffLimit, and must be idempotent because Pods can re-run. A CronJob creates Jobs on a schedule, and its concurrencyPolicy (Allow/Forbid/Replace) decides what happens when a run is due while the last is still going — the usual source of overlap and pileup. Set TTLs and history limits or dead Pods accumulate.

The 10 things to remember

  1. StatefulSet Pods have stable ordinal names and keep their identity across restarts and reschedules.
  2. A headless Service (clusterIP: None) gives each StatefulSet Pod a stable DNS name so peers can address specific members.
  3. volumeClaimTemplates gives each ordinal its own PVC that follows the Pod — stable storage, not shared.
  4. Scaling a StatefulSet down keeps the PVCs by default; orphaned disks cost money until cleaned up or a retention policy reclaims them.
  5. OrderedReady creates/updates Pods one at a time in ordinal order, so a single un-Ready ordinal freezes the whole rollout.
  6. A DaemonSet runs one Pod per matching node, derived from the node list, and needs tolerations to run on tainted/control-plane nodes.
  7. A Job runs to completion: completions successes needed, parallelism at once, backoffLimit failures tolerated before Failed.
  8. Job work must be idempotent — Pods can re-run on retry or node failure, so re-running must not double-process.
  9. CronJob concurrencyPolicy Allow/Forbid/Replace controls overlap; use Forbid for anything touching shared state.
  10. Set ttlSecondsAfterFinished and history limits, or finished Jobs and their Pods pile up in the namespace.
