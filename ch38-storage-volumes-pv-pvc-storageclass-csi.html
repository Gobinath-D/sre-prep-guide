Part IV · Kubernetes — Architecture & Internals · Chapter 38

Storage: volumes, PV, PVC, StorageClass, CSI

A container's filesystem dies with the container. That's fine for a stateless web server and a disaster for a database. Kubernetes storage is the machinery that gives a pod a disk that outlives it — and understanding the request/supply split, the CSI attach-then-mount dance, and access modes is the difference between "the PVC is stuck Pending, why?" being a five-minute fix or a two-hour outage.

Start: Beginner→→→End: Staff-level ~45 min read · kind + kubectl

By the end of this chapter you can

  • Explain why a PVC and a PV are separate objects, and what "binding" actually does.
  • Set up dynamic provisioning with a StorageClass and read the whole PVC → PV → CSI attach → mount path.
  • Choose the right access mode (RWO / RWX / ROX / RWOP) and explain why RWO is not "one pod."
  • Use volumeClaimTemplates, reclaim policy, and volume expansion correctly.
  • Diagnose a Pending PVC with no provisioner, an RWO scheduling conflict, and a stuck attach.

Why this exists · the problemPods are cattle; some data is not

A container's writable layer is scratch space. Kill the container — or let the kubelet restart it, or reschedule the pod to another node — and everything written inside is gone. That's exactly what you want for a stateless service: no state to lose, any replica is interchangeable, and you met that model in the workloads chapters. But a Postgres pod, a Kafka broker, a Prometheus server, an upload service — these are their data. Lose the disk and you've lost the service.

So a pod needs a disk that isn't tied to the container's life. And here's the tension that shapes the entire design: the person who needs storage (an app developer writing a pod spec) is not the person who owns storage (a platform team, or a cloud provider with EBS volumes and quotas). The developer shouldn't have to know the AWS volume ID or the NFS export path. The platform shouldn't have to hand-create a disk every time someone deploys.

Kubernetes solves this by splitting the world into a request and a supply, exactly like a resource request and a node. The developer writes a PersistentVolumeClaim: "I want 10Gi, read-write-once, of the fast-ssd class." Somewhere, a PersistentVolume — a real disk — either already exists or gets created on demand to satisfy that claim. The two are matched and bound, and from then on the pod just references the claim by name. Neither side needs to know the other's details. The machinery that turns "I want 10Gi" into an actual attached, mounted, formatted filesystem is the subject of this chapter, and most of it is the CSI — the Container Storage Interface, the plugin contract that lets any storage vendor teach Kubernetes how to make disks appear.

Key point

Storage in Kubernetes is a claim (PVC, the request) matched to a volume (PV, the supply), decoupled by a StorageClass (the recipe for making supply on demand). The pod only ever names the claim. Everything hard — creating a cloud disk, attaching it to the right node, mounting it into the pod — happens below that line, driven by a CSI driver.

Mental model · analogyThe mental model: a self-storage facility

Carry this picture through the chapter.

Real-world analogy

You need somewhere to keep your stuff, so you go to a self-storage facility. You don't build a unit — you fill in a rental form: "I need a 10 m² climate-controlled unit." That form is the PVC. The actual concrete room with a roll-up door is the PV. The front desk matches your form to a free room and hands you the key — that matching is binding, and from then on your form and that specific room are married.

You picked "climate-controlled" from a menu of unit types — standard, climate-controlled, drive-up. That menu is the StorageClass. Pick a type and, if no matching room is free, the facility builds one on the spot to your spec — that's dynamic provisioning.

But renting the room isn't the same as being able to put things in it. First the facility has to assign the unit to your building (you can't use a unit that's in a different building across town) — that's the CSI attach. Then someone has to actually roll the door open so you can walk in — that's the CSI mount. Attach makes the volume reachable from your node; mount makes it usable inside your pod. Two distinct steps, and a failure at each looks completely different.

The rental agreement also says what happens when you leave: does the facility demolish the room (reclaim policy Delete) or keep it and your stuff for someone to collect (Retain)? And can two people share one unit at once? A standard unit is one tenant at a time (RWO); a shared workshop lets many in together (RWX).

Think first: a pod is deleted and rescheduled to a different node. Its PVC is ReadWriteOnce. Does its data survive the move?

Yes — the data survives, and that's the entire point of a PV. The PVC stays bound to the same PV across the pod's death and rebirth; the new pod references the same claim and sees the same files. What ReadWriteOnce controls is not persistence but concurrency: the volume can be mounted read-write by pods on one node at a time. So the old pod's volume is detached from the old node and the new pod's volume is attached to the new node — a detach-then-attach handoff. If the old pod is slow to terminate (a stuck finalizer, a hung kubelet), that handoff stalls and the new pod sits in ContainerCreating with a "Multi-Attach" or "volume node affinity" error until the old attachment clears. Persistence is guaranteed; a clean, fast handoff is the thing that bites you.

ArchitectureClaim, volume, and the CSI plumbing

The developer-facing part is two objects: a PVC and the PV it binds to. Below that line sits the CSI, split into a controller side (runs once per cluster, talks to the cloud API to create and attach volumes) and a node side (runs on every node as a DaemonSet, does the actual mounting). The diagram follows one claim from request to a mounted filesystem.

Why an SRE cares

Storage is where "the pod won't start" stops being a compute problem. A pod stuck in ContainerCreating for minutes is, four times out of five, a volume problem: the PVC never bound, the volume won't attach to this node, or the mount is hanging on a dead NFS server. Each has a different signature and a different fix, and they all look identical from kubectl get pods. The attach-vs-mount split you'll learn here is the map that turns that opaque wait into a diagnosis.

Internal workingThe CSI attach-then-mount flow, step by step

This is the sequence that turns "I want 10Gi" into a filesystem the container can write to. Watch which component does each step — controller (cluster-wide) versus node (per-machine) — because that's how you'll know where a failure lives.

  1. PVC created. A developer applies a PVC naming a StorageClass (or the cluster default). Its status is Pending — no PV yet.
  2. Provision (controller). The external-provisioner sidecar sees the unbound PVC and calls the driver's CreateVolume. For a cloud driver this makes a real disk (an EBS volume, a GCE PD). A PV object is created describing it, and the PVC and PV are bound — status flips to Bound.
  3. Schedule. The pod referencing the PVC is scheduled to a node. With volumeBindingMode: WaitForFirstConsumer, provisioning is deferred until now so the volume is created in the same zone as the node — otherwise you can create a disk in zone A and a pod in zone B that can never reach it.
  4. Attach (controller). The external-attacher creates a VolumeAttachment object; the driver's ControllerPublishVolume attaches the volume to the chosen node (the cloud "attach this EBS volume to this instance" call). Some drivers, like node-local ones, skip this — attachment isn't universal.
  5. Stage (node). On the node, the kubelet asks the CSI node plugin to NodeStageVolume: format the volume if it's blank, then mount it to a global staging directory on the host — once per node, shared if several pods use the same volume.
  6. Publish (node). The node plugin runs NodePublishVolume: a bind-mount from the staging dir into this specific pod's volume path under the kubelet directory. Now the container's mount namespace can see the filesystem, and the container starts.
  7. Teardown, in reverse. Pod dies → NodeUnpublish → NodeUnstage → ControllerUnpublish (detach). If the PVC is deleted and the reclaim policy is Delete, DeleteVolume destroys the cloud disk; Retain keeps it for manual recovery.

The single most useful thing in that list: attach is a controller/cloud operation; mount is a node operation. A pod stuck because the VolumeAttachment never succeeded is a cloud-API or IAM problem you debug from the controller. A pod stuck because NodePublishVolume hangs is a node problem — a dead NFS server, a filesystem that won't mount — you debug on the machine. Same symptom, opposite ends of the cluster.

Access modes are about nodes, not pods

ReadWriteOnce (RWO) means the volume is mounted read-write by pods on one node at a time — several pods on the same node can share it, which surprises people. ReadWriteMany (RWX) allows read-write from many nodes (needs a networked filesystem like NFS/CephFS; block volumes like EBS can't do it). ReadOnlyMany (ROX) is read-only from many nodes. ReadWriteOncePod (RWOP) is the strict one — exactly one pod, cluster-wide. The classic bug: assuming RWO means "one pod" and being baffled when two pods on the same node happily share it, or when a Deployment with RWO storage can't roll to a new node.

Think first: you deploy a 3-replica Deployment, each replica mounting the same RWO PVC. Two pods run; one is stuck. Why — and would a StatefulSet fix it?

An RWO volume attaches to one node. Two of the replicas landed on the same node, so they share the mount fine; the third landed on a different node and can't attach the already-attached volume — it hangs. Sharing one PVC across replicas is the mistake. A Deployment gives every replica the same pod template, including the same claimName, so they all fight over one volume. A StatefulSet fixes it precisely because of volumeClaimTemplates: it stamps out a separate PVC per replica (data-web-0, data-web-1, …), so each pod gets its own volume and there's no contention. That's why databases run as StatefulSets — not for the stable network name alone, but because per-replica storage is baked into the object.

ComponentsThe pieces, named

  • Volume. The generic "something mounted into a pod." Ephemeral kinds (emptyDir, configMap, secret, downwardAPI) live and die with the pod. Persistent ones are backed by a PV. This chapter is about the persistent kind.
  • PersistentVolume (PV). A cluster-scoped object representing a real piece of storage — capacity, access modes, the CSI driver and volume handle, reclaim policy. Either pre-created by an admin (static) or created by a provisioner (dynamic).
  • PersistentVolumeClaim (PVC). A namespaced request for storage — size, access modes, StorageClass. Pods reference the PVC, never the PV. One PVC binds to exactly one PV.
  • StorageClass. The recipe for dynamic provisioning: which provisioner (CSI driver), what parameters (disk type, IOPS, encryption), the reclaimPolicy, volumeBindingMode (Immediate vs WaitForFirstConsumer), and allowVolumeExpansion. A cluster has a default class marked with an annotation.
  • CSI driver. The vendor's implementation of the Container Storage Interface. A controller Deployment (with the external-provisioner/attacher/resizer/snapshotter sidecars) plus a node DaemonSet. Out-of-tree by design — the in-tree cloud volume plugins are gone, replaced by CSI.
  • VolumeAttachment. A cluster object tracking "is this volume attached to this node." Watch it to debug attach failures directly.
  • volumeClaimTemplates. A StatefulSet field that creates one PVC per replica, giving each pod its own durable, stably-named volume that follows it across reschedules.

Data flowFollowing one PVC from Pending to written

A developer applies a PVC (5Gi, RWO, class fast) and a pod that mounts it. Here's the whole life:

  1. PVC lands, status Pending. Because fast uses WaitForFirstConsumer, nothing provisions yet — the system waits to see where the pod will run.
  2. The scheduler picks a node for the pod, factoring in the volume's topology so it won't strand the disk in the wrong zone.
  3. The external-provisioner calls CreateVolume; a 5Gi cloud disk appears in the node's zone; a PV is created and bound to the PVC. Status → Bound.
  4. The external-attacher attaches the disk to the node via a VolumeAttachment.
  5. The node plugin stages the volume (formats to ext4/xfs if new, mounts to the global dir) and publishes it into the pod's path.
  6. The container starts with the filesystem present and writes data. Kill the pod and the data stays on the PV; a new pod bound to the same PVC picks it up.
  7. Delete the PVC: with reclaim Delete, the disk is destroyed; with Retain, the PV goes to Released and the disk waits for manual recovery.

Hands-onLabs — build, understand, break, troubleshoot

All local on kind, which ships the local-path provisioner and a default StorageClass named standard. It's a node-local CSI-style provisioner — perfect for seeing binding, mounting, and the access-mode gotchas without a cloud account. Use a two-node cluster so the RWO lab is real:

bash
cat > kind-store.yaml <<'EOF'
kind: Cluster

nodes:
- role: control-plane
- role: worker
EOF
kind create cluster --name store --config kind-store.yaml
kubectl get storageclass

Lab 1Build — a dynamic PVCBuild

Create a PVC against the default class and watch it stay Pending — on purpose.

bash
kubectl apply -f - <<'EOF'
apiVersion: v1
kind: PersistentVolumeClaim
metadata: { name: data }
spec:
  accessModes: [ReadWriteOnce]
  resources: { requests: { storage: 1Gi } }
EOF
kubectl get pvc data
OutputNAME STATUS VOLUME CAPACITY ACCESS MODES STORAGECLASS data Pending standard

Pending isn't broken here. The standard class uses WaitForFirstConsumer, so it won't provision a PV until a pod actually needs the claim. Confirm with the events:

bash
kubectl describe pvc data | tail -3
OutputNormal WaitForFirstConsumer ... waiting for first consumer to be created before binding

That message is the tell: the PVC is fine and deliberately waiting. Compare it in Lab 3 with a PVC that's Pending for a bad reason.

Lab 2Understand — mount it, and prove persistenceUnderstand

Attach the claim to a pod, write a file, then destroy and recreate the pod. If the file comes back, you've proven the volume outlives the container.

bash
kubectl apply -f - <<'EOF'
apiVersion: v1
kind: Pod
metadata: { name: writer }
spec:
  containers:
  - name: c
    image: busybox:1.36
    command: ["sh","-c","echo persisted-$(date +%s) > /data/marker; sleep 3600"]
    volumeMounts: [{ name: v, mountPath: /data }]
  volumes:
  - name: v
    persistentVolumeClaim: { claimName: data }
EOF
kubectl wait --for=condition=Ready pod/writer --timeout=60s
kubectl get pvc data          # now Bound — a PV was provisioned on demand
kubectl exec writer -- cat /data/marker
OutputNAME STATUS VOLUME CAPACITY ACCESS MODES data Bound pvc-6f2c... 1Gi RWO persisted-1727...

The PVC bound the moment the pod needed it — that's WaitForFirstConsumer doing its job. Now delete the pod and bring it back with a command that reads instead of writes:

bash
kubectl delete pod writer
kubectl run reader --image=busybox:1.36 --overrides='{"spec":{"containers":[{"name":"c","image":"busybox:1.36","command":["cat","/data/marker"],"volumeMounts":[{"name":"v","mountPath":"/data"}]}],"volumes":[{"name":"v","persistentVolumeClaim":{"claimName":"data"}}]}}' --restart=Never -it
Outputpersisted-1727...

Same marker, different pod. The container's filesystem died twice; the PV didn't. You can even see where the bytes live — docker exec store-worker ls /var/local-path-provisioner shows the host directory backing the PV.

Lab 3Break — a PVC that's Pending for a bad reasonBreak

Lab 1's Pending was benign. This one isn't. Ask for a StorageClass that doesn't exist:

bash
kubectl apply -f - <<'EOF'
apiVersion: v1
kind: PersistentVolumeClaim
metadata: { name: broken }
spec:
  storageClassName: fast-ssd            # no such class in this cluster
  accessModes: [ReadWriteOnce]
  resources: { requests: { storage: 1Gi } }
EOF
kubectl get pvc broken
OutputNAME STATUS VOLUME CAPACITY ACCESS MODES STORAGECLASS broken Pending fast-ssd

Same Pending as Lab 1 at a glance — but this one will wait forever, and no pod will fix it. Don't guess; read the events and figure out why before revealing.

Reveal the cause

There is no fast-ssd StorageClass, so no provisioner ever claims this PVC. The events say so directly:

bash
kubectl describe pvc broken | grep -A2 Events
OutputWarning ProvisioningFailed ... storageclass.storage.k8s.io "fast-ssd" not found

This is the number-one PVC failure in the wild, and it comes in three flavours: (1) the named StorageClass doesn't exist (typo, or a class that lives only in prod); (2) the class exists but its CSI driver isn't installed or its controller pod is crash-looping, so nobody answers CreateVolume; (3) no default class is set and the PVC named none, so nothing volunteers. The fix depends on which: create/rename the class, get the driver healthy, or set a default. The diagnostic reflex is always the same — describe pvc and read the event, because a Pending PVC never explains itself in get. Clean up: kubectl delete pvc broken.

Lab 4Troubleshoot — expand a volumeTroubleshoot

The data PVC is 1Gi and filling up. Growing it should be a one-line edit. Try it:

bash
kubectl patch pvc data -p '{"spec":{"resources":{"requests":{"storage":"2Gi"}}}}'
OutputError from server: persistentvolumeclaims "data" is forbidden: only dynamically provisioned pvc with allowVolumeExpansion=true can be resized

Rejected. The edit is legal syntax but forbidden. What property decides whether a volume may grow — and is it on the PVC or somewhere else? Work it out before revealing.

Reveal the cause and the mechanism

Expansion is gated by allowVolumeExpansion: true on the StorageClass, not the PVC — and kind's standard class ships with it false (you saw that in the setup output). The driver must also implement expansion (the external-resizer sidecar plus NodeExpandVolume). With a class that allows it, the flow is: edit the PVC's requested size upward, the resizer calls ControllerExpandVolume to grow the backing disk, and on next mount NodeExpandVolume grows the filesystem to fill it. Two hard rules: you can only grow, never shrink; and the class must have opted in before you need it. Prove the gate by making a class that allows expansion:

bash
kubectl apply -f - <<'EOF'
apiVersion: storage.k8s.io/v1
kind: StorageClass
metadata: { name: expandable }
provisioner: rancher.io/local-path
allowVolumeExpansion: true
volumeBindingMode: WaitForFirstConsumer
EOF
# A PVC on 'expandable' will accept the same patch that 'data' rejected.

(local-path itself doesn't implement resize, so the patch is accepted but the disk won't actually grow — which is its own lesson: the class opting in isn't enough, the driver has to do the work. On a real CSI driver like EBS, both are true and the volume grows online.)

Lab 5Production challenge — RWO across two nodesSenior challenge

A team reports: "We scaled our app and the new pod is stuck in ContainerCreating, but the old one is fine." Reproduce it. The data PVC's PV is pinned to whichever node ran the writer pod. Force a second pod onto the other node and have it mount the same claim:

bash
kubectl get pv -o custom-columns=NAME:.metadata.name,NODE:'.spec.nodeAffinity.required.nodeSelectorTerms[0].matchExpressions[0].values[0]'
# say the PV is pinned to 'store-worker'; pin a new pod to the control-plane node
OTHER=$(kubectl get nodes -o name | grep control-plane | cut -d/ -f2)
kubectl apply -f - <<EOF
apiVersion: v1
kind: Pod
metadata: { name: second }
spec:
  nodeName: ${OTHER}
  containers:
  - name: c
    image: busybox:1.36
    command: ["sh","-c","sleep 3600"]
    volumeMounts: [{ name: v, mountPath: /data }]
  volumes:
  - name: v
    persistentVolumeClaim: { claimName: data }
EOF
kubectl get pod second
OutputNAME READY STATUS RESTARTS AGE second 0/1 ContainerCreating 0 40s

It never goes Ready. From get pods this is indistinguishable from a slow image pull or a crashing app. The senior move: find out what the pod is actually waiting on, then explain to the team why "just add a replica" broke — and what they should have built instead.

Reveal the cause and the fix

The pod's events name it exactly:

bash
kubectl describe pod second | grep -A2 Events
OutputWarning FailedScheduling / FailedMount ... node(s) had volume node affinity conflict # on a cloud block driver the equivalent is: # Multi-Attach error for volume "pvc-..." Volume is already exclusively attached to one node

An RWO volume lives on one node. local-path's PV carries a node-affinity to the node that provisioned it, so a pod pinned elsewhere can't use it — "volume node affinity conflict." On a cloud block store (EBS) the same single-node truth shows up as a Multi-Attach error: the volume is already attached to node A and can't attach to node B while node A holds it. Different words, identical cause: RWO is one node at a time.

The fix depends on intent. If the pods genuinely need to share one filesystem, that's ReadWriteMany, which requires a networked filesystem (NFS, CephFS, EFS) — a block volume physically cannot do RWX. If instead each pod needs its own durable disk (the usual case for a scaled stateful app), you don't share a PVC at all — you run a StatefulSet with volumeClaimTemplates, which gives every replica its own PVC. Sharing one RWO PVC across replicas of a Deployment is the actual bug. Clean up with kind delete cluster --name store.

Failure & troubleshootingHow this shows up in real incidents

Incident pattern · the Pending PVC

Symptom: a pod is stuck Pending/ContainerCreating; its PVC is Pending. First cut: kubectl describe pvc <name> and read the events. Cause is usually: the StorageClass doesn't exist or was mistyped; the CSI driver isn't installed or its controller is crash-looping so nobody answers CreateVolume; or no default class is set and the PVC named none. A benign look-alike is WaitForFirstConsumer — that Pending is correct and clears when a pod is scheduled, so read the event before you panic.

Incident pattern · RWO won't move nodes

Symptom: a rolling update or a rescheduled pod hangs in ContainerCreating on a new node while the old pod's node still holds the volume. First cut: kubectl describe pod — look for "Multi-Attach error" or "volume node affinity conflict." Confirm: kubectl get volumeattachment to see the stale attachment. Cause is usually: RWO storage plus a workload that assumes multi-node access, or a slow detach because the old pod won't terminate. Fix by using RWX for genuine sharing, per-replica PVCs (StatefulSet) for per-pod state, or a Recreate rollout so the old pod releases before the new one attaches.

Incident pattern · stuck attach or hung mount

Symptom: ContainerCreating for many minutes with no obvious rejection. First cut: is it stuck at attach or mount? Check kubectl get volumeattachment (attach) and the CSI node plugin logs on that node (mount). Cause is usually: an attach that never completes because of a cloud-API throttle, an IAM permission the driver lacks, or hitting the per-node attached-volume limit — versus a mount that hangs because a networked filesystem (NFS) is unreachable, which will freeze NodePublishVolume indefinitely. Attach is a cloud/controller problem; mount is a node problem. Knowing which end to open the laptop on is the whole game.

Production · security · performanceWhat matters in production

Reclaim policy is a data-loss lever, so treat it like one. Dynamic PVs default to Delete: delete the PVC and the cloud disk — and everything on it — is destroyed. For anything you'd cry over, use Retain so a deleted PVC leaves the PV Released and the disk intact for recovery. The worst storage incidents aren't corruption; they're a kubectl delete on the wrong namespace wiping PVCs whose class was Delete. Back that up with real backups (volume snapshots via the CSI snapshotter), because reclaim policy is not a backup.

WaitForFirstConsumer is almost always what you want. With Immediate binding, the volume is provisioned the instant the PVC is created — before anything knows which node the pod will run on — so in a multi-zone cluster you can strand a disk in zone A and a pod that can only run in zone B, and the pod never schedules. Delayed binding provisions after the scheduler has chosen a node, so topology lines up. If you see zonal scheduling failures with healthy storage, check the binding mode first.

Security lives in three places. Filesystem ownership: a container running as non-root can't write a volume owned by root, so set fsGroup in the pod's securityContext to have the volume group-owned correctly. Encryption: enable it in the StorageClass parameters (most cloud drivers support encrypted-at-rest), because a detached, unencrypted volume is a data-exfil path. And access: a hostPath volume or an over-broad CSI mount can expose the node's filesystem to a pod — Pod Security Admission's restricted profile blocks hostPath for exactly this reason (Chapter 39).

Performance is set by the class, not the pod. IOPS and throughput are properties of the underlying disk type chosen in the StorageClass parameters — a pod can't ask for faster I/O than its volume's class provides. Under-provision the disk type and you get a database that's mysteriously slow under load with the CPU idle, because it's blocked on I/O the whole time. Match the class to the workload: gp3/io2 tiers for databases, cheaper tiers for logs and scratch. And remember node limits — each node can only attach so many volumes, which caps how many stateful pods land there.

System-design connectionWhere this reaches into architecture

The request/supply split you see in PVC-and-PV is the same decoupling pattern that runs through the whole book: the consumer declares intent, a controller reconciles supply, and neither is coupled to the other's implementation. It's why a developer can write one pod spec that runs unchanged on kind (local-path), on EKS (EBS), and on-prem (Ceph) — the StorageClass is swapped, the pod isn't. That portability is a design goal you'll defend in interviews.

Storage is also where the stateless/stateful boundary gets real. Everything you learned about scaling freely in Chapter 37 assumed disposable pods; the moment a pod owns a PV, it can't be freely multiplied or moved, and RWO turns "just add a replica" into a design decision. That pushes the industry pattern of keeping the compute tier stateless and concentrating durable state in a small number of carefully-run StatefulSets or, more often, in managed data services outside the cluster (Chapter 86). When you reach EKS you'll see this collide with the CSI driver, IAM, and multi-AZ topology (Chapter 57) — where a volume's zone dictates a pod's zone, and DR planning (Chapter 90) becomes a snapshot-and-restore story.

InterviewInterview questions & answers

Open each one only after you've answered it in your head.

30-secWhat's the difference between a PV, a PVC, and a StorageClass?

A PersistentVolume is the actual storage — a real disk, cluster-scoped. A PersistentVolumeClaim is a namespaced request for storage that a pod references by name. Binding marries one PVC to one PV. A StorageClass is the recipe for creating PVs on demand: it names the CSI provisioner and its parameters, so when a PVC asks for that class and no matching PV exists, one gets dynamically provisioned. The point of the split is decoupling — the developer writes a claim without knowing the disk's cloud ID or the driver, and the same pod spec is portable across clusters by swapping only the class.

2-minWalk me through what happens from applying a PVC to a container writing to the volume.

The PVC lands as Pending. With WaitForFirstConsumer nothing provisions until a pod that uses it is scheduled, so the volume ends up in the pod's zone. Once the pod is scheduled, the external-provisioner sidecar calls the CSI driver's CreateVolume, which makes the real disk; a PV object is created and bound to the PVC, so status flips to Bound. Then the external-attacher creates a VolumeAttachment and the driver's ControllerPublishVolume attaches the disk to the node — a cloud "attach volume to instance" call. On the node, the kubelet drives the node plugin: NodeStageVolume formats if needed and mounts the disk to a global staging directory once per node, and NodePublishVolume bind-mounts that into the specific pod's path. The container starts and writes. The key mental model I'd stress is that provision and attach are controller/cloud operations while stage and mount are node operations — so a stuck volume is diagnosed at opposite ends depending on which step failed.

DeepWhat exactly does ReadWriteOnce mean, and why does it trip people up?

RWO means the volume can be mounted read-write by pods on one node at a time — not one pod. Several pods co-located on the same node can share an RWO volume perfectly well, which surprises people who read "Once" as "one pod." The flip side bites harder: a Deployment with RWO storage can't do a normal rolling update onto a new node, because the new pod tries to attach a volume still held by the old pod's node, and you get a Multi-Attach error or a node-affinity conflict. The strict single-pod mode is a separate access mode, ReadWriteOncePod. RWO is also physically tied to block storage — an EBS volume attaches to one instance — so you cannot get RWX from it no matter what you write in the manifest; RWX needs a networked filesystem like NFS or CephFS. When someone wants "shared storage for all my replicas," the right questions are: do they need one shared filesystem (RWX) or one disk per replica (StatefulSet volumeClaimTemplates)? Those are different answers and confusing them is the classic mistake.

ScenarioA pod has been in ContainerCreating for eight minutes. How do you find out why?

ContainerCreating is a catch-all, so I isolate the layer. First kubectl describe pod and read the events — that usually names it: "FailedMount," "Multi-Attach," "volume node affinity conflict," or an image-pull error, which immediately tells me storage versus image. If it's storage, I check the chain in order. Is the PVC Bound? If it's Pending, the problem is provisioning — describe pvc for a missing StorageClass or a dead CSI controller. If the PVC is Bound, the volume exists but isn't reaching the pod, so I check kubectl get volumeattachment: a missing or stuck attachment points at the controller and the cloud API (throttling, IAM, per-node attach limits). If it's attached but not mounted, I go to the node and read the CSI node-plugin logs, because a hung NodePublishVolume is usually a networked filesystem that's unreachable. The framework is: bound → attached → mounted, and I find the first arrow that didn't complete. That turns an opaque eight-minute wait into a specific fix.

Follow-upWhy do databases run as StatefulSets instead of Deployments, from a storage angle?

Because a Deployment gives every replica the same pod template, including the same volume claim, so all replicas would fight over one PVC — and with RWO that means all but the co-located ones can't even mount it. A StatefulSet has volumeClaimTemplates, which stamps out a distinct PVC per replica: data-db-0, data-db-1, and so on. Each pod gets its own durable disk that stays bound to that ordinal and follows the pod across reschedules, which is exactly what a database replica needs — its own data directory, stable across restarts. That per-replica storage, together with stable network identity and ordered rollout, is why stateful systems use StatefulSets. If I only remembered one storage reason, it's that volumeClaimTemplates give per-pod persistent volumes automatically, and a Deployment cannot.

RetainQuick revision

A container's filesystem is scratch; durable data needs a PersistentVolume. Kubernetes splits storage into a request (PVC) and a supply (PV), decoupled by a StorageClass that provisions PVs on demand via a CSI driver — so the pod only names the claim and the same spec is portable across clusters. The CSI flow is provision (CreateVolume → PV, bound), then attach (ControllerPublishVolume → VolumeAttachment, a controller/cloud step), then stage and publish (NodeStage + NodePublish, node steps that format and mount) — attach and mount are different operations at opposite ends of the cluster, which is how you localize a stuck volume. Access modes are about nodes: RWO is one node at a time (not one pod), RWX needs a networked filesystem, RWOP is the strict single-pod mode. WaitForFirstConsumer avoids stranding a disk in the wrong zone; reclaim Delete destroys data while Retain keeps it; expansion needs allowVolumeExpansion on the class and a driver that implements it, and only grows. StatefulSet volumeClaimTemplates give each replica its own PVC — the reason databases aren't Deployments.

The 10 things to remember

  1. Container filesystems are ephemeral; a PV is a disk that outlives the pod.
  2. PVC = request (namespaced), PV = supply (cluster-scoped); binding is one-to-one and the pod names only the PVC.
  3. A StorageClass is the recipe for dynamic provisioning: provisioner, parameters, reclaim policy, binding mode, expansion.
  4. CSI has two halves: a controller (provision, attach — talks to the cloud) and a node DaemonSet (stage, mount).
  5. Attach ≠ mount: attach is a controller/cloud step, mount is a node step — debug them at opposite ends.
  6. RWO is one node at a time, not one pod; RWX needs a networked filesystem; RWOP is strict single-pod.
  7. WaitForFirstConsumer provisions after scheduling so the volume lands in the pod's zone.
  8. Reclaim Delete destroys the disk with the PVC; use Retain for anything precious, plus real snapshots.
  9. Volume expansion needs allowVolumeExpansion on the class and driver support, and can only grow.
  10. StatefulSet volumeClaimTemplates give each replica its own PVC — why databases aren't Deployments.
