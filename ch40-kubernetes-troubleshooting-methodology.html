Part IV · Kubernetes — Architecture & Internals · Chapter 40

Kubernetes troubleshooting methodology

CrashLoopBackOff, ImagePullBackOff, Pending, OOMKilled, NotReady — five words that cover most of what pages you, and every one of them is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Memorizing "I've seen this error before, here's the fix" runs out the first time the cause is new. A reasoning loop doesn't.

Start: Beginner→→→End: Staff-level ~50 min read · kind + kubectl lab

By the end of this chapter you can

  • State the five-step reasoning loop — symptom, hypotheses, evidence, isolate, root cause — and default to it instead of guessing.
  • Read a Pod's phase, conditions, container states, and events as a structured trail of evidence, not just a red/green signal.
  • Diagnose CrashLoopBackOff, ImagePullBackOff, Pending, OOMKilled, and NotReady from kubectl output alone, and explain why each is a status word, not a cause.
  • Tell a pod-level failure from a node-level one in the first thirty seconds — because they demand completely different search spaces.
  • Apply the same method to a failure mode you've never seen before, instead of freezing because it isn't in a lookup table.

Why this exists · the problemFive words, and none of them tell you what's wrong

Open kubectl get pods during an incident and Kubernetes hands you one of a short list of words: Pending, CrashLoopBackOff, ImagePullBackOff, OOMKilled, NotReady. Chapter 33 already made the point for one of these directly — CrashLoopBackOff is a symptom, not a cause, and the same is true of all five. Each one describes a shape of failure the platform observed, and each shape has a genuinely short list of mechanically possible causes behind it. The word alone never tells you which.

The tempting shortcut is pattern matching: "I've seen ImagePullBackOff before, it was always a bad tag, so it's a bad tag." That shortcut works until the day it isn't a bad tag — a private registry rotated its credentials, or a node's disk filled up and the pull itself failed for a reason that has nothing to do with the tag. Pattern matching against your own memory has no mechanism for the case you haven't seen yet, and production will eventually hand you that case at the worst possible time.

What scales instead is a small, boring loop you run every time, on purpose, regardless of how confident you feel: state the symptom precisely, list the hypotheses that could mechanically produce it, gather the specific evidence that would confirm or kill each one, isolate the one hypothesis the evidence actually supports, and only then commit to a root cause. It's slower than a lucky guess and faster than every wrong guess combined. This chapter applies it to the five words above, on purpose, so the loop itself — not the five answers — is what you walk away with. It's also the specific, Kubernetes-flavored instance of a general method that Part XI names outright: the universal troubleshooting framework of Chapter 103 is this same loop with the Kubernetes vocabulary stripped back out.

Key point

A status word tells you the shape of the failure — where in the pod's life it got stuck. It never tells you why. Treat every one of these five words as the start of an investigation, never the end of one.

Mental model · analogyThe mental model: differential diagnosis

Real-world analogy

A patient walks into an ER and says "chest pain." A bad doctor hears that and immediately treats the one condition they saw most recently. A good doctor treats "chest pain" as a symptom and opens a differential diagnosis — a short, specific list of conditions that could actually produce chest pain: a muscle strain, acid reflux, a cardiac event, a handful of others. Nobody orders every test in the hospital; they order the tests that would separate those specific possibilities — an ECG, bloodwork, a chest X-ray. That's evidence, chosen because of what it rules in or out, not because it's routine.

As results come back, the doctor isolates: the ECG is clean, bloodwork shows nothing cardiac, but the pain tracks exactly with meals — reflux is now the best-supported explanation, and the others are ruled out by specific evidence, not by feel. That's the root cause, and only now does treatment start. Notice what didn't happen: nobody treated "chest pain" directly. You cannot treat a symptom; you can only treat what's causing it — and kubectl gives you a stethoscope, not a diagnosis.

ArchitectureThe decision path from symptom to search space

Before you can pick hypotheses, you have to know which space you're searching. The single question that matters most in the whole framework is the very first branch: is this one workload misbehaving, or is it everything on one node? Those are different investigations with almost no overlap, and picking the wrong one burns the most valuable minutes of an incident on the wrong search space entirely.

Why an SRE cares

Paging systems alert on symptoms — a failed check, a status word, an error-rate threshold — because that's what's cheap to detect automatically. Nobody pages you with the root cause already attached; if they could, there'd be no incident. The gap between "here's the alert" and "here's the fix" is exactly this framework, run under time pressure. Skipping straight to a fix without running it is how you get an incident that looks resolved and pages again an hour later.

Internal workingThe five-step loop, in detail

  1. State the symptom precisely, in the tool's own words. "STATUS shows CrashLoopBackOff, RESTARTS is 14 and climbing, this started nine minutes ago" is a symptom. "The app is broken" is an interpretation wearing a symptom's clothes, and it already threw away information you'll want later — when it started, how fast it's recurring, whether it's one pod or many.
  2. List hypotheses — the short, specific set the platform can mechanically produce. Not "everything that could ever go wrong with software." For CrashLoopBackOff specifically, the container is exiting and restarting, which narrows things to: the process exits on its own (a bug, a missing config, a failed startup dependency check), or something outside the process kills it (the OOM killer, a liveness-probe-triggered kill, a node-level signal). That's a handful of concrete possibilities, not an open-ended search.
  3. For each hypothesis, name the one piece of evidence that would confirm or kill it — then go get exactly that. Exit code and Last State Reason from describe separate "the app exited itself" from "something killed it." The crashed container's own output, via logs --previous, separates "a bug" from "missing config" from "a failed dependency check" if it's the former. Resist the urge to run every command you know; run the one that actually discriminates between the hypotheses still standing.
  4. Isolate: read the evidence against each hypothesis specifically. This is the step most people skip under pressure — they see something alarming in the output and stop there, even if it doesn't actually explain the symptom. An old, unrelated warning in the events list is not your root cause just because it's the first scary-looking line. Ask, for each candidate: does this specific piece of evidence actually predict the symptom I started with?
  5. Commit to a root cause specific enough that you know the fix. "Networking issue" is not a root cause. "The image tag in the Deployment was bumped to a version that was never pushed to the registry" is — you know exactly what to change and exactly how to verify it. If your stated root cause doesn't imply an obvious next action, it's still a hypothesis, not a conclusion.
A symptom can be masked without being fixed

Restarting the pod, bumping the replica count, or deleting and recreating the object will often make the status word disappear for a while — and none of those touch a root cause you haven't found yet. If you skip straight from symptom to a generic remedy, the underlying condition (a leak, a bad config that only some replicas got, a node with the same fault) is still there, and it will page you again. The loop isn't bureaucracy; it's the difference between a fix and a snooze button.

Think first: two pods, both showing CrashLoopBackOff with RESTARTS climbing at roughly the same rate. Are they necessarily the same problem?

Not necessarily — the status word only tells you the shape (exiting and restarting on a backoff), not the cause, and two unrelated causes can produce visually identical backoff curves. One pod might be OOMKilled every time it hits a memory spike; the other might be crashing on a missing environment variable that's always missing. Both show the exact same word, a similar restart cadence, and even similar-looking backoff timing, because the backoff behavior comes from the kubelet's restart policy, not from the cause. Treating them as "probably the same issue" because they look alike at a glance is exactly the pattern-matching shortcut this chapter argues against — each one needs its own pass through the loop, starting from its own describe and its own logs --previous.

ComponentsWhere the evidence actually lives

  • kubectl get -o wide / custom-columns. The fast overview: STATUS, RESTARTS, AGE, NODE, in one line per pod. Good for triage — spotting whether a symptom is isolated to one pod or spread across one node — but too coarse to diagnose anything on its own.
  • kubectl describe. The richest single source. For a Pod: Conditions (the state-machine detail under the coarse phase), the per-container State/Last State with a Reason string and, for a terminated container, an Exit Code, and the Events list at the bottom — which is where most causes surface first, in plain English, and which most people read last or not at all.
  • kubectl logs and kubectl logs --previous. The crashed container's own stdout/stderr. The instant a container restarts, a normal logs call shows the new container's output, not the one that just died — --previous is the only way to see the crash itself, and it's only available for one generation back. This is why log shipping matters operationally (Chapter 78): fast-churning containers destroy their own evidence trail within minutes if nothing's collecting it off-box.
  • kubectl get events --sort-by=.lastTimestamp. A cluster- or namespace-wide event stream, useful when the symptom doesn't map cleanly to one object you already know about — a scheduling failure, an admission rejection, a volume problem, all land here too.
  • Pod phase versus conditions versus container state — three different layers, easy to conflate. Phase (Pending/Running/Succeeded/Failed/Unknown) is the coarsest possible summary. Conditions (PodScheduled, Initialized, ContainersReady, Ready) are booleans underneath it that tell you exactly which stage of startup a pod has and hasn't cleared. Container state (Waiting/Running/Terminated, each with its own Reason) is where ImagePullBackOff, CrashLoopBackOff, and OOMKilled actually live — most of the five words in this chapter's title are container-state reasons, not pod phases, which is why kubectl get pods's STATUS column is really reporting the most relevant of several layers, not one single field.
  • Node conditions. Ready, MemoryPressure, DiskPressure, PIDPressure, NetworkUnavailable — and a deliberate delay (the node-monitor-grace-period, 40 seconds by default) between a real heartbeat loss and the node flipping NotReady, so a single missed heartbeat doesn't flap the whole node's workloads.
Think first: kubectl get pods shows STATUS Running but READY 0/1. Is the pod's phase actually "not running"?

No — its phase genuinely is Running; the container started and hasn't exited. What's false is one specific condition underneath that phase: Ready. The STATUS column you're reading is actually a blend of several layers — phase, conditions, and container state — collapsed into one word for convenience, and here it's showing you the phase while READY is showing you the ContainersReady/Ready condition separately. Treating "Running" as "healthy" is the mistake: a pod can run indefinitely while correctly reporting itself unfit for traffic, which is exactly what a readiness probe is designed to let it do. The fix for this specific shape is never in the phase — it's always one layer down, in Conditions and Events.

Data flowFollowing one ambiguous page through the loop

The page says: "myapp is failing health checks." kubectl get pods -o wide shows myapp-7f9d: STATUS Running, RESTARTS 0, READY 0/1. Stop and actually read those three numbers before hypothesizing anything — Running with 0 restarts already rules out the entire crash-and-OOM branch of the tree; whatever this is, the container itself is alive and hasn't been killed once. That single line just cut the hypothesis space by more than half.

Hypotheses narrow to: the readiness probe itself is misconfigured (wrong path, wrong port), or the probe is correctly configured and the app is correctly reporting that it isn't ready. kubectl describe pod myapp-7f9d shows Conditions: Ready False and an Events line: Readiness probe failed: HTTP probe failed with statuscode: 503. That's evidence, and it discriminates cleanly: a misconfigured probe usually shows a connection error or a 404, not a clean 503 from the app's own handler. A 503 means the app is up, is answering, and is telling you itself that it isn't ready.

kubectl logs myapp-7f9d shows the app's own log line: WARN: database connection pool exhausted, marking unready. Root cause isolated — and notice where it actually is: not in this pod at all. The pod is behaving exactly as designed, correctly refusing traffic because a downstream dependency is unhealthy. Fixing this pod (restarting it, redeploying it) would do nothing, because it was never broken — the same "blocked, not crashed" reflex from Chapter 1 and the "check the endpoints, not the pod" reflex from Chapter 43, both showing up again here. The loop's job wasn't to fix myapp; it was to prove that myapp wasn't where the fix belonged.

Hands-onLabs — build, understand, break, troubleshoot

Five labs, five status words, all on one kind cluster:

bash
kind create cluster --name diag
kubectl create namespace demo
Lab 1Build — walk the loop explicitly on ImagePullBackOffBuild

Deploy a tag that doesn't exist, and this time, write out every step of the loop instead of jumping to the fix.

bash
kubectl -n demo create deployment web --image=nginx:1.99.99
kubectl -n demo get pods
OutputNAME READY STATUS RESTARTS AGE web-6b8f7c9d5-x2z4p 0/1 ImagePullBackOff 0 45s

Symptom: STATUS ImagePullBackOff, RESTARTS 0 — the container has never even started once. Hypotheses: a nonexistent tag, a private image needing credentials we didn't provide, or a network path to the registry that's down. Evidence: the Events list names the exact pull attempt and the exact response from the registry:

bash
kubectl -n demo describe pod -l app=web | grep -A4 Events
OutputWarning Failed ... Failed to pull image "nginx:1.99.99": rpc error: ... nginx:1.99.99: not found Warning BackOff ... Back-off pulling image "nginx:1.99.99"

Isolate: the registry answered — it didn't time out, it didn't reject credentials, it said the tag doesn't exist. That rules out "network down" and "bad credentials" with the same piece of evidence. Root cause: the tag 1.99.99 was never published for this image. Fix and confirm the word actually clears, not just that the pod exists:

bash
kubectl -n demo set image deployment/web web=nginx:1.27
kubectl -n demo rollout status deployment/web --timeout=60s

Five short steps, written down, took less time than the retry backoff you'd have waited through by just staring at the pod.

Lab 2Understand — Pending is not one thingUnderstand

Request more CPU than any node in the cluster has:

bash
kubectl -n demo apply -f - <<'EOF'
apiVersion: v1
kind: Pod
metadata: { name: hungry }
spec:
  containers:
  - name: c
    image: busybox:1.36
    command: ["sleep", "3600"]
    resources: { requests: { cpu: "100" } }
EOF
kubectl -n demo get pod hungry
OutputNAME READY STATUS RESTARTS AGE hungry 0/1 Pending 0 20s

Symptom: Pending — the same word Chapter 38 used for a PVC that hadn't bound yet, and a completely different mechanism. That's the whole point of treating the word as a symptom: two different root causes, one shared vocabulary. Hypotheses: insufficient resources, a node-affinity/taint mismatch, or (per Chapter 38) an unbound volume. Evidence::

bash
kubectl -n demo describe pod hungry | grep -A3 Events
OutputWarning FailedScheduling ... 0/1 nodes are available: 1 Insufficient cpu. preemption: 0/1 nodes are available: 1 No preemption victims found for incoming pod.

Isolate: the scheduler considered every node and rejected all of them for the same stated reason, Insufficient cpu — not a volume, not an affinity rule. Root cause: the requested 100 CPU is larger than any node's allocatable capacity, full stop; no amount of waiting fixes it. Confirm the ceiling and then stop asking for more than it:

bash
kubectl describe node -l node-role.kubernetes.io/control-plane!= 2>/dev/null | grep -A5 Allocatable
kubectl -n demo delete pod hungry

The event line did all the work here — it named the resource and the exact node count. Reading Events first, before forming a theory, would have saved a hypothesis or two.

Lab 3Break — CrashLoopBackOff with the cause hiddenBreak

This one you diagnose yourself before reading further.

bash
kubectl -n demo apply -f - <<'EOF'
apiVersion: v1
kind: Pod
metadata: { name: flaky }
spec:
  containers:
  - name: c
    image: busybox:1.36
    command: ["sh", "-c", "if [ -z \"$REQUIRED_CONFIG\" ]; then echo 'FATAL: REQUIRED_CONFIG not set' >&2; exit 1; fi; sleep 3600"]
EOF
kubectl -n demo get pod flaky -w
OutputNAME READY STATUS RESTARTS AGE flaky 0/1 CrashLoopBackOff 3 (25s ago) 84s

Run the loop yourself: state the symptom precisely, list the hypotheses a container that exits and restarts could actually have, and pick the one command that would separate them fastest. Then check.

Reveal the evidence and the cause

A plain kubectl logs flaky may come back empty or racy — by the time you run it, the container that logged the error may already have been replaced by the next restart. --previous is what actually catches it:

bash
kubectl -n demo logs flaky --previous
kubectl -n demo describe pod flaky | grep -A6 "Last State"
OutputFATAL: REQUIRED_CONFIG not set Last State: Terminated Reason: Error Exit Code: 1

Reason: Error with a non-zero exit code means the process exited on its own — nothing killed it. Combined with the log line, the root cause is specific and actionable: the container requires an environment variable it was never given. Not "the container crashes" — that's still the symptom restated. Fix it at the actual cause:

bash
kubectl -n demo delete pod flaky
kubectl -n demo run flaky --image=busybox:1.36 --env="REQUIRED_CONFIG=set" \
  --command -- sh -c 'sleep 3600'
kubectl -n demo get pod flaky

RESTARTS stops climbing and STATUS settles on Running — confirmation the loop closed on the right cause, not just that the symptom went quiet for a moment.

Lab 4Troubleshoot — the same word, a different causeTroubleshoot

Run this one without looking back at Lab 3 for the answer — that's exactly the trap it's testing.

bash
kubectl -n demo apply -f - <<'EOF'
apiVersion: v1
kind: Pod
metadata: { name: piggy }
spec:
  containers:
  - name: c
    image: busybox:1.36
    command: ["sh", "-c", "dd if=/dev/zero of=/dev/shm/fill bs=1M count=100; sleep 3600"]
    resources:
      limits: { memory: "20Mi" }
    volumeMounts: [{ name: shm, mountPath: /dev/shm }]
  volumes:
  - name: shm
    emptyDir: { medium: Memory }
EOF
kubectl -n demo get pod piggy -w
OutputNAME READY STATUS RESTARTS AGE piggy 0/1 CrashLoopBackOff 2 (30s ago) 50s

Read that STATUS column again. It is the identical word to Lab 3 — CrashLoopBackOff — with a similar restart cadence. If the word alone told the whole story, these would be the same incident. Go one command deeper before you decide what happened here.

Reveal the evidence and the cause
bash
kubectl -n demo describe pod piggy | grep -A6 "Last State"
OutputLast State: Terminated Reason: OOMKilled Exit Code: 137

Reason: OOMKilled, exit code 137 — 128 plus signal 9, SIGKILL. Nothing about the app decided to exit; the kernel's cgroup memory controller killed it the instant it crossed the 20Mi limit, exactly the mechanism from Chapter 3, just triggered by a container limit instead of whole-system memory pressure. You can see the kernel's own account of it on the node:

bash
docker exec diag-worker dmesg 2>/dev/null | grep -i "killed process" | tail -3
Output[...] Memory cgroup out of memory: Killed process ... (dd) total-vm:..., anon-rss:20308kB

Same STATUS word as Lab 3, opposite mechanism, and opposite fix. Lab 3's process chose to exit — the fix was giving it what it needed to keep running. This process was forcibly killed by the kernel for exceeding a limit — the fix is either raising the limit to match real usage, or fixing the workload to use less, never just "restart it and see." Confirming Reason and Exit Code before touching anything is the one habit that keeps these two labs from being misdiagnosed as the same incident. Clean up: kubectl -n demo delete pod piggy.

Lab 5Production challenge — half the fleet pages at onceSenior challenge

Stop the kubelet on one node directly, the way a real crash or a bad node-level change would:

bash
docker exec diag-worker systemctl stop kubelet
# wait for the node-monitor-grace-period (default 40s) to elapse
sleep 45
kubectl get nodes
OutputNAME STATUS ROLES AGE VERSION diag-control-plane Ready control-plane 4m v1.31.0 diag-worker NotReady <none> 4m v1.31.0

Now the senior question, framed exactly the way a page would frame it: several unrelated services' alerts fire within the same minute, all showing different symptoms — one CrashLooping, one failing readiness, one just not responding. What's the very first command you run, and why is it not kubectl describe pod on any of them? Reason it out before revealing.

Reveal the reasoning

Widen the aperture before you narrow it. Several unrelated services failing in different-looking ways, in the same minute, is far more likely to share one cause than to be several coincident, unrelated bugs. Chasing the first pod's describe output commits you to the pod-level search space before you've ruled out the node-level one — exactly the wrong-branch mistake the decision tree above warns about. The first command is the top of that tree, not the bottom of it:

bash
kubectl get nodes
kubectl get pods -A -o wide | awk '{print $8}' | sort | uniq -c   # pods per node, eyeball the skew

If the failing services all happen to have a pod scheduled on diag-worker, you've just explained every one of them with a single root cause instead of three separate investigations. Confirm what a NotReady node actually does to its existing pods — and notice it is deliberately not instant:

bash
kubectl describe node diag-worker | grep -A4 Conditions
kubectl get pods -A --field-selector spec.nodeName=diag-worker -o wide

Pods already running on the node keep running physically — the container runtime doesn't know the kubelet died — but the node stops reporting fresh status, and after the separate pod-eviction-timeout (five minutes by default) the control plane starts evicting them, which it can't fully confirm without a live kubelet to acknowledge it. That multi-minute gap is deliberate: a single missed heartbeat shouldn't trigger a mass reschedule. The practical read for an on-call engineer: a NotReady node explains degraded-but-not-instantly-dead pods on it, and the fix is restoring the kubelet (or replacing the node), not chasing each symptom pod individually.

bash
docker exec diag-worker systemctl start kubelet
sleep 15
kubectl get nodes   # diag-worker back to Ready

The lesson generalizes past this one lab: correlated, differently-shaped symptoms across unrelated services is itself evidence, and it points up a layer — node, control plane, or a shared dependency — before it points at any single pod's YAML.

Failure & troubleshootingHow investigations themselves go wrong

The five labs covered the failure modes. These three are about the investigation, not the workload — the mistakes that turn a five-minute diagnosis into a two-hour incident regardless of which status word started it.

Anti-pattern · treating the symptom as the cause

What it looks like: a pod is unhealthy, someone deletes it or bumps the replica count, the alert clears, and everyone moves on without ever running describe or logs. Why it bites later: nothing about the underlying condition changed — a slow leak, a bad config on a subset of replicas, a node with a fault — so the same alert fires again, often worse, often at a worse time. The fix is procedural: no incident closes without a stated root cause, even a one-line one, and "restarted it" is never that line by itself.

Anti-pattern · tunnel vision on the first hypothesis

What it looks like: someone has a pet theory ("it's always DNS," "it's always the last deploy") and keeps gathering evidence to confirm it while ignoring evidence that contradicts it. Why it bites later: the isolate step exists specifically to kill hypotheses the evidence doesn't support — skip it and you can talk yourself into the wrong root cause with real, if misread, evidence in hand, then "fix" something that was never broken while the actual cause keeps running. The fix: for every hypothesis still standing, ask what evidence would prove it wrong, not just what would confirm it.

Anti-pattern · pod-level thinking on a node-level problem

What it looks like: forty minutes spent re-reading one Deployment's YAML while three other, unrelated services degrade in the background on the same node. Why it bites later: every minute spent in the wrong search space is a minute the real cause — and its blast radius — kept growing. The fix: make "is this one workload or one node (or the whole control plane)" the very first check, every time, before opening a single pod's details, exactly as Lab 5 forces you to.

Production · security · performanceWhat matters in production

Encode the loop into runbooks, not just into memory. A good runbook for "pod stuck Pending" doesn't say "check resources" — it lists the specific hypotheses, the specific command that discriminates between them, and what each possible result means, so any on-call engineer reproduces a senior engineer's reasoning under pressure at 3 a.m., not just their own best guess. Chapter 107's Kubernetes runbooks are this chapter's method, pre-written for the cases you can predict in advance.

Evidence has a shelf life. A crashed container's logs survive exactly until the next restart evicts them from --previous, and a deleted pod's events survive only as long as the API server's event TTL. If your incident review keeps hitting "we don't actually know why it crashed" as the final answer, the fix isn't better memory — it's shipping logs and events off-box before they age out, which is Chapter 78's job, not this chapter's.

Which status word is trending is itself a signal. One OOMKilled incident is an app problem; a rising rate of OOMKilled incidents across many unrelated services is a capacity or right-sizing problem — the VPA and resource-request discipline of Chapter 37 is the systemic fix, not five individual app fixes. Common mistakes: reading RESTARTS as itself meaningful without ever checking the Reason behind it; assuming Pending always means "add capacity" when it's frequently a scheduling constraint that more capacity won't touch; treating a policy or admission rejection (Chapter 39) as if it were a runtime failure, when it never even reached a pod; and forgetting to check node health when several unrelated services misbehave at once.

System-design connectionWhere this reaches into architecture

This loop is the same one incident response runs at a larger scale, just with fewer Kubernetes-specific nouns: symptom, hypotheses, evidence, isolate, root cause is the skeleton underneath the postmortem process in Chapter 92, and it's exactly what Part XI generalizes past Kubernetes entirely, starting with Chapter 103. At a platform-engineering level, the design decision is where you spend the effort: alerting on symptoms is cheap and necessary, but a mature platform also invests in making the evidence-gathering step fast — structured logs, long event retention, dashboards that already show the node/pod correlation Lab 5 made you compute by hand. The method doesn't change; what a platform team builds is the tooling that makes step three (evidence) take seconds instead of minutes, every time, for everyone on call — not just the person who's internalized this chapter.

InterviewInterview questions & answers

Answer each in your head first, then open it.

30-secWhat's your framework for debugging an unfamiliar Kubernetes failure?

A five-step loop: state the symptom precisely using the tool's own words, list the small set of hypotheses that could mechanically produce that specific symptom, gather the one piece of evidence that would confirm or kill each hypothesis, isolate the one the evidence actually supports rather than the first thing that looks alarming, and only then commit to a root cause specific enough to imply the fix. It works on failure modes I've never seen before, because it never depended on recognizing the pattern in the first place — it depends on the platform's own vocabulary of status, conditions, and events, which is always there.

2-minWalk me through diagnosing a pod stuck in CrashLoopBackOff.

The symptom is precise: the container is starting and exiting repeatedly, on a growing backoff. That narrows hypotheses to two families — the process exits on its own, from a bug, missing config, or a failed startup dependency check, or something external kills it, most commonly the OOM killer or a liveness probe. The discriminating evidence is the per-container Last State: a Reason of Error with a small, deliberate-looking exit code points at the first family, and I'd pull logs with --previous immediately, before the next restart evicts them, to see what the process itself said on the way out. A Reason of OOMKilled with exit code 137 points at the second family entirely — that's the kernel's cgroup OOM killer, and no amount of reading application logs will explain it, because the process never got a chance to log anything; I'd check the container's memory limit against what it actually needs. I isolate on that Reason and exit code specifically, not on how alarming the pod looks, because CrashLoopBackOff itself looks identical either way, and confirm the fix by watching RESTARTS actually stop climbing, not just checking that the pod exists again.

DeepWhy is "just restart the pod" often the wrong first move, even when it makes the symptom go away?

Restarting resets the object Kubernetes is watching, not the condition that caused the failure. If the cause was a transient blip — a one-off network hiccup during startup — a restart genuinely fixes it, and that's fine. But if the cause is a leak that takes an hour to reappear, a config that's wrong for a subset of replicas, or a node with a hardware or disk fault, a restart clears the visible symptom while leaving the actual condition fully intact, and it will resurface, usually at a less convenient time and sometimes worse, because whatever was accumulating (a leak, disk fill, a queue backing up) kept accumulating on every other replica the whole time you thought it was resolved. The deeper cost is informational: every restart-without-diagnosis throws away the evidence — logs, exit codes, events — that would have let you find the real cause cheaply, so the next occurrence starts from zero again instead of building on what you learned. Restarting is a legitimate mitigation to buy time during an active incident; it's never a substitute for finishing the loop afterward.

ScenarioMultiple unrelated services start failing at the same time, in different-looking ways. How does your approach change from a single failing pod?

It changes at the very first step. A single failing pod puts me straight into the pod-level branch — describe, logs, events for that one object. Several unrelated services failing together, especially in different-looking ways, is a strong prior that the shared cause sits above any individual pod: one node, the control plane, a shared dependency like DNS or a Service with no endpoints, or a cluster-wide change like a new admission policy. So my first command is scoped wider, not narrower — kubectl get nodes, and a quick pass over which node each affected pod is scheduled on, before I open a single pod's details. If the affected services cluster on one node or share one recent change, I've explained all of them with one root cause instead of running the loop three separate times and probably missing the correlation. Jumping straight into the first pod's YAML is the classic version of this mistake — it commits you to the wrong search space before you've even confirmed which space you're in.

Follow-upHow does this framework generalize beyond Kubernetes?

The five steps never mention a pod, a status word, or kubectl — those are just where the evidence happens to live in this particular system. The same loop debugs a slow database query, a flaky CI pipeline, or a networking issue on a bare VM: state the symptom precisely, list the mechanically possible causes for that specific symptom, gather the evidence that discriminates between them, isolate the one the evidence actually supports, commit to a root cause specific enough to act on. What changes from system to system is only the vocabulary — Kubernetes gives you phases, conditions, container states, and events; a database gives you slow-query logs and lock waits; a network gives you packet captures. That's exactly why Part XI treats this as a universal framework and spends this chapter's worth of pages just teaching Kubernetes's particular vocabulary for it.

RetainQuick revision

Every Kubernetes status word — Pending, ImagePullBackOff, CrashLoopBackOff, OOMKilled, NotReady — describes the shape of a failure, never its cause. The reasoning loop that closes that gap has five steps: state the symptom precisely, list the mechanically possible hypotheses, gather the evidence that discriminates between them, isolate the one the evidence actually supports, and commit to a root cause specific enough to imply the fix. The first branch of any investigation is scope: one workload, or everything on one node — get the wrong branch and you burn the incident on the wrong search space. Pod phase, conditions, and container state are three different layers of the same object; most of the five words live in container-state Reasons, not the coarse phase. CrashLoopBackOff and OOMKilled can look identical at kubectl get pods and only diverge at describe's Last State and exit code — the status word alone cannot be trusted to distinguish them. Restarting a pod can mask a symptom without touching its cause, and correlated failures across unrelated services point up a layer, toward the node or the control plane, before they point at any single pod.

The 10 things to remember

  1. A status word is a symptom, not a diagnosis — every one of the five in this chapter hides more than one possible cause.
  2. The loop: symptom → hypotheses → evidence → isolate → root cause. Skip isolate and you'll fix the wrong thing.
  3. The first branch is scope: one pod, or everything on one node — decide that before opening any pod's details.
  4. Phase (coarse) → Conditions (state machine) → container State + Reason (where most of these five words actually live).
  5. kubectl describe's Events list is the single richest source and the one most people read last.
  6. kubectl logs --previous is the only way to see a crashed container's own output — it's evicted on the next restart.
  7. CrashLoopBackOff can mean Reason: Error (the process exited itself) or Reason: OOMKilled (the kernel killed it) — same word, opposite fix.
  8. Exit code 137 = SIGKILL, almost always the cgroup OOM killer; a small nonzero exit code is usually the app exiting on purpose.
  9. A NotReady node doesn't instantly kill its pods — there's a deliberate grace period, then a separate eviction timeout, by design.
  10. Correlated, differently-shaped failures across unrelated services are evidence of a shared cause one layer up — check node/control-plane health first.
