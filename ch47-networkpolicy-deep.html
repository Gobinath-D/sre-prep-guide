Part V · Kubernetes Networking at Packet Level · Chapter 47

NetworkPolicy deep

By default any pod can talk to any pod — a compromised frontend has a clear line to your database. NetworkPolicy is how you close that door. But it's a request, not a guarantee: the object is inert unless your CNI actually enforces it, and the day a policy silently breaks a service is the day you learn how these rules really compose.

Start: Beginner→→→End: Staff-level ~45 min read · kind + Calico

By the end of this chapter you can

  • Explain the default allow-all posture and exactly how one policy flips a pod to default-deny for a direction.
  • Write ingress and egress rules with podSelector, namespaceSelector, and ipBlock — and know when they AND versus OR.
  • Apply a default-deny baseline, then open exactly one path, and watch everything else drop.
  • Say why a NetworkPolicy can be accepted and then silently ignored, and verify whether yours is enforced.
  • Debug the classic incident: a "security" change that quietly took a working service offline — usually DNS.

Why this exists · the problemA flat network is a flat blast radius

The Kubernetes network model from Chapter 41 makes one strong promise: every pod gets a routable IP and any pod can reach any other pod with no NAT in between. That promise is what makes services, DNS, and the whole datapath simple. It's also, from a security standpoint, a wide-open floor plan. Your public-facing frontend can open a socket straight to the payments database. So can a debug pod someone left running. So can a workload that got popped through an unpatched dependency. Nothing in the base network stops any of it.

In a data centre you'd solve this with firewalls and VLANs keyed on IP addresses. That doesn't translate. Pod IPs are ephemeral — a pod restarts and gets a new address; a Deployment scales and ten new IPs appear; the address that was your database a minute ago belongs to a log shipper now. Writing firewall rules against IPs that churn every few minutes is hopeless. You need a firewall that keys on identity — "frontend pods may reach api pods" — and that re-computes itself automatically as pods come and go.

That's NetworkPolicy: a namespaced Kubernetes object that describes which connections are allowed to and from a set of pods, selected by labels rather than addresses. You declare intent — "these may talk to those, on these ports" — and something in the cluster turns it into real packet filtering that follows pods around. The catch, and the theme of this whole chapter, is in that word something.

Key point

A NetworkPolicy is a request for segmentation expressed against pod labels. The API server stores it whether or not anything can act on it. The actual dropping of packets is done by your CNI plugin — and if your CNI doesn't implement NetworkPolicy, the object is accepted and does absolutely nothing. A policy that looks applied but isn't enforced is worse than no policy, because it buys you false confidence.

Mental model · analogyKeycard readers on interior doors

Real-world analogy

Picture an office where, by default, every interior door is propped open. Anyone who's badged into the building can wander into any room — the mail room, the server closet, the CFO's office. That's the flat pod network: reachable is the default.

A NetworkPolicy is installing a keycard reader on a specific door. The instant you fit a reader to the server-closet door, that door stops being propped open — it defaults to locked, and now only the badges you explicitly list can open it. Every other door in the building is untouched and stays wide open. So the rule "select a room, and it becomes deny-by-default; list who may enter" is the whole model. You can fit readers that control entering a room (ingress), leaving it (egress), or both.

And here's the part people miss until it bites them: the readers only do anything if the building's security system is actually wired up. Fit shiny keycard readers in a building whose security contractor never connected them, and the doors look controlled on the floor plan while standing open in reality. The security system is your CNI. No enforcing CNI, no locks — just decorations.

ArchitectureWhere the packet is allowed or dropped

NetworkPolicy is not a proxy and not a controller that sits in the path. There's no NetworkPolicy pod. Instead, the CNI's agent on each node watches the API server for policies, pods, and their labels, and compiles that into kernel packet-filtering rules — iptables/ipset chains for Calico's iptables dataplane, eBPF programs for Cilium or Calico-eBPF. The decision happens inline, in the node's datapath, at the moment a packet is about to be delivered to (ingress) or sent from (egress) a selected pod. Follow one allowed connection and one dropped one:

Three facts fall straight out of that picture and govern everything else. First, selection is what arms the deny. Before any policy names a pod, that pod accepts everything; the moment a policy selects it for a direction, that direction flips to default-deny and only listed traffic passes. Second, policies are additive — a pure union of allows. The v1 API has no "deny" rule; you can't subtract. Two policies selecting the same pod combine into "allow anything either one allows." Third, the enforcement point is the CNI in the kernel datapath, not the API server. Store a policy on a cluster whose CNI ignores NetworkPolicy and you've written a memo nobody reads.

Internal workingIsolation, selectors, and the AND/OR that trips everyone

1 · Non-isolated by default; selection flips it

Every pod starts non-isolated in both directions: all ingress and all egress allowed. A pod becomes isolated for a direction only when at least one NetworkPolicy that includes that direction in its policyTypes selects it via spec.podSelector. Once isolated for ingress, the only ingress permitted is what the union of selecting policies' ingress rules allows; everything else is dropped. Egress is a completely separate switch — isolating a pod for ingress does nothing to its outbound traffic.

2 · The shape of a rule

A policy has a podSelector (which pods it governs, in its own namespace), a policyTypes list (which directions it controls), and lists of ingress and/or egress rules. Each ingress rule is a set of from peers plus optional ports; each egress rule is a set of to peers plus ports. Peers are one of three kinds:

  • podSelector — pods (in the policy's namespace, unless combined with a namespaceSelector) matching these labels.
  • namespaceSelector — all pods in namespaces matching these labels. Namespaces carry the automatic label kubernetes.io/metadata.name, which is how you target a namespace by name.
  • ipBlock — a raw CIDR (with an optional except), for traffic to/from IPs outside the cluster's pod network, like on-prem ranges or the internet.

3 · The AND/OR rule you must get right

This is the single most common source of policies that "don't work." Inside one from (or to) list element, a namespaceSelector and a podSelector written together combine with AND — "pods matching this podSelector that live in namespaces matching this namespaceSelector." Written as separate list elements, they combine with OR — "anything in those namespaces, plus those pods." The YAML difference is one dash:

yaml
# AND — pods labelled role=api ONLY in namespaces labelled team=payments
ingress:
  - from:
      - namespaceSelector: { matchLabels: { team: payments } }
        podSelector:       { matchLabels: { role: api } }

# OR — every pod in team=payments namespaces, AND (separately) any role=api pod in THIS namespace
ingress:
  - from:
      - namespaceSelector: { matchLabels: { team: payments } }
      - podSelector:       { matchLabels: { role: api } }

One is a tight cross-namespace allow; the other is far broader than most people intend. When a policy lets in more (or less) than you expected, check the dashes first.

4 · Default-deny — the baseline you actually want

Two policies express the recommended posture for a namespace. An empty podSelector: {} selects every pod in the namespace; empty rule lists allow nothing:

yaml
apiVersion: networking.k8s.io/v1
kind: NetworkPolicy
metadata: { name: default-deny-all, namespace: shop }
spec:
  podSelector: {}                 # selects all pods in the namespace
  policyTypes: [Ingress, Egress]  # isolate both directions
  # no ingress: and no egress: keys ⇒ nothing allowed either way

From that locked baseline you add narrow allow policies on top — and because policies are additive, each one only ever opens a path. That's the safe direction to work in: deny everything, then justify each hole.

5 · Enforcement is the CNI's job — and some CNIs don't do it

The API server validates and persists a NetworkPolicy and then walks away. Turning it into dropped packets is entirely the network plugin's responsibility, and the docs say so plainly: your cluster must use a plugin that supports NetworkPolicy enforcement. Calico and Cilium enforce it (and add their own richer CRDs on top); flannel on its own does not; and — the one that catches people in labs and CI — kind's default CNI, kindnet, does not enforce NetworkPolicy. On a non-enforcing cluster, kubectl apply succeeds, kubectl get netpol shows your policy, and traffic flows exactly as before. There is no error, no event, no warning. This is why the first question about any NetworkPolicy is never "is it correct?" but "is it enforced?"

6 · Where L3/4 ends, and what's coming

Core NetworkPolicy is layer 3/4 only: IPs, ports, protocols. It can't say "allow only HTTP GET" or "only to api.stripe.com" — those are L7 or DNS-aware features your CNI may add through its own CRDs, but they're not the portable API. The core API also can't express priorities or explicit denies across teams. The upstream answer to that gap is AdminNetworkPolicy and BaselineAdminNetworkPolicy — newer, cluster-scoped, ordered objects (with real Allow/Deny/Pass actions) meant for cluster admins to set guardrails above namespace-owned NetworkPolicies. Know they exist; they're how platform teams will enforce "no namespace may ever reach the metadata endpoint" without editing every app's policies.

Think first: you put an ingress policy on your api pods allowing only app=frontend. Ten minutes later the api pods stop being able to reach the database. Did your policy do that?

No — and this reflex saves real time. Your policy set policyTypes: [Ingress] and selected the api pods, so it isolated them for ingress only. Egress from the api pods is a separate switch you never touched, so they can still open connections to the database exactly as before. Ingress isolation controls who may start a connection to a pod; it says nothing about connections the pod itself initiates. If the api→database path broke right after your change, look for a different cause — a default-deny-egress policy someone else added, a database-side ingress policy, a DNS failure — not this ingress rule. Confusing "ingress to me" with "my egress" is one of the most common wrong turns in policy debugging.

ComponentsThe pieces and what each owns

  • The NetworkPolicy object (networking.k8s.io/v1) — namespaced. Owns the intent: which pods (podSelector), which directions (policyTypes), which peers and ports (ingress/egress). Inert on its own.
  • The CNI plugin — the enforcer. It watches policies, pods, and namespace labels and programs the node datapath. Its identity decides whether policy works at all and which extensions (L7, FQDN egress) you get.
  • The CNI's node agent — Calico's Felix, the Cilium agent, and so on. This is the process that actually writes and updates the iptables/ipset or eBPF rules as endpoints churn.
  • Pod and namespace labels — the identity the whole system keys on. A label typo silently narrows or widens a policy; treat labels as an interface, not decoration.
  • CoreDNS (Chapter 45) — not part of the API, but the dependency you must never forget: once you isolate egress, DNS to kube-dns is denied unless you explicitly allow it, and then everything that resolves a name breaks.
  • AdminNetworkPolicy / BaselineAdminNetworkPolicy — cluster-scoped, ordered guardrails layered above per-namespace NetworkPolicy for platform teams.

Data flowFollowing two connections into the api tier

Namespace shop has a default-deny-all policy plus one allow policy: api pods accept ingress on TCP 8080 from app=frontend. Trace a frontend request and a debug-pod probe:

  1. A frontend pod opens a TCP connection to an api pod's IP on 8080. The packet is routed by the CNI to the api pod's node (plain pod-to-pod, no NAT — Chapter 42).
  2. On that node, just before delivery to the api pod, the CNI's compiled rules run. The api pod is selected by an ingress policy, so it's isolated: the datapath checks the source against the allowed set.
  3. The source pod's labels resolve to app=frontend and the port is 8080 — a rule matches. Accept. The packet is delivered; the connection completes.
  4. Meanwhile a debug pod tries the same api pod on 8080. Same routing, same enforcement point. Its labels are app=debug; no ingress rule lists that. Drop. From the debug pod it looks like a timeout — the SYN vanishes with no RST.
  5. The api pod now calls the database. Nothing isolated api's egress, so the outbound connection is allowed and routed normally. (Had someone added default-deny-egress, this hop would fail — and DNS before it.)
Think first: a policy's from uses a namespaceSelector for namespace payments, but traffic from that namespace is still dropped. The namespace name is definitely "payments." What's missing?

A namespaceSelector matches on the namespace's labels, not its name — and the pod-side of the connection isn't the issue. Either you're selecting a label the namespace doesn't carry, or you assumed the name is automatically a selector. It is, but only through the built-in label kubernetes.io/metadata.name=payments that Kubernetes stamps on every namespace. So namespaceSelector: { matchLabels: { kubernetes.io/metadata.name: payments } } works out of the box, whereas { team: payments } only works if someone actually labelled the namespace team=payments. The fix is one of: use the metadata.name label, or kubectl label ns payments team=payments. This is a frequent "my cross-namespace allow does nothing" cause, and it's on the namespace, not the pods.

Hands-onLabs — build, understand, break, troubleshoot

NetworkPolicy is meaningless without an enforcing CNI, so we build a kind cluster with its default CNI disabled and install Calico. This is the exact shape the Kubernetes docs describe for using Calico for policy. Everything runs on your laptop; no cloud.

bash
# kind with NO default CNI, and Calico's default pod CIDR (192.168.0.0/16):
kind create cluster --name np --config - <<'EOF'
kind: Cluster
apiVersion: kind.x-k8s.io/v1alpha4
networking:
  disableDefaultCNI: true
  podSubnet: "192.168.0.0/16"
EOF
# Install Calico (any recent release). Nodes go Ready once it's up:
kubectl apply -f https://raw.githubusercontent.com/projectcalico/calico/v3.28.0/manifests/calico.yaml
kubectl -n kube-system rollout status ds/calico-node --timeout=180s
kubectl get nodes            # Ready = the CNI is installed and enforcing
Lab 1Build — an open namespace, everything reachableBuild

Before any policy, confirm the flat network: a client pod reaches a backend on its ClusterIP with no restriction.

bash
kubectl create ns shop
kubectl -n shop create deployment api --image=hashicorp/http-echo \
  -- /http-echo -listen=:8080 -text="api-ok"
kubectl -n shop expose deployment api --port=8080
kubectl -n shop run client --image=busybox:1.36 --labels app=debug -- sleep 3600
kubectl -n shop exec client -- wget -qO- --timeout=3 http://api:8080
Outputapi-ok

The debug-labelled client reaches the api with no policy in place — the door is propped open. That's the baseline every cluster ships with, and the thing we're about to take away.

Lab 2Understand — default-deny, then open one pathUnderstand

Lock the namespace to default-deny for ingress, watch the client fail, then allow exactly one source label and watch that source — and only that source — get back in.

bash
kubectl apply -f - <<'EOF'
apiVersion: networking.k8s.io/v1
kind: NetworkPolicy
metadata: { name: default-deny-ingress, namespace: shop }
spec:
  podSelector: {}
  policyTypes: [Ingress]
EOF
kubectl -n shop exec client -- wget -qO- --timeout=3 http://api:8080 || echo "DENIED"
Outputwget: download timed out DENIED

Every pod in shop is now isolated for ingress and nothing is allowed — the api included. Now open a single path: allow ingress to app=api from app=frontend on 8080. Relabel the client to prove the selector actually discriminates.

bash
kubectl apply -f - <<'EOF'
apiVersion: networking.k8s.io/v1
kind: NetworkPolicy
metadata: { name: allow-frontend-to-api, namespace: shop }
spec:
  podSelector: { matchLabels: { app: api } }
  policyTypes: [Ingress]
  ingress:
    - from:
        - podSelector: { matchLabels: { app: frontend } }
      ports:
        - { protocol: TCP, port: 8080 }
EOF
kubectl -n shop exec client -- wget -qO- --timeout=3 http://api:8080 || echo "debug STILL denied"
kubectl -n shop label pod client app=frontend --overwrite
kubectl -n shop exec client -- wget -qO- --timeout=3 http://api:8080   # now allowed
Outputdebug STILL denied api-ok

As app=debug the client is refused; relabel it app=frontend and the identical request succeeds. Nothing about the pod's IP or the api changed — only the label the policy keys on. That's identity-based firewalling: the rule follows the label, not the address.

Lab 3Break — a "security" change that kills DNSBreak

Now the incident that ends more Friday afternoons than any other policy mistake. Lock down egress too — the intuitive "nothing leaves unless I say so" — and watch a working app fall over in a way that looks nothing like a network-policy problem.

bash
kubectl apply -f - <<'EOF'
apiVersion: networking.k8s.io/v1
kind: NetworkPolicy
metadata: { name: default-deny-egress, namespace: shop }
spec:
  podSelector: {}
  policyTypes: [Egress]
EOF
# The client can reach api by IP fine, but by NAME it now fails:
kubectl -n shop exec client -- wget -qO- --timeout=3 http://api:8080 || echo "FAILS by name"
Outputwget: bad address 'api' FAILS by name

Look closely: it's not "connection refused" or "timed out" — it's bad address. The app didn't fail to connect; it failed to resolve. Everything that uses a hostname across the whole namespace is now broken, and the api-to-anything paths are dead too. Work out why a policy about egress produced a DNS error before revealing.

Reveal the cause

The default-deny-egress policy isolated every pod's outbound traffic — including the UDP/TCP port 53 packets pods send to CoreDNS in kube-system. Name resolution is the first thing every request does, so once DNS egress is denied, the symptom is "bad address," not "blocked" — and it hits everything, which makes it look like a cluster-wide outage rather than a policy you just applied. The fix is to allow DNS egress explicitly. Because CoreDNS lives in another namespace, this needs a namespaceSelector, and the port is 53 on both UDP and TCP:

yaml
apiVersion: networking.k8s.io/v1
kind: NetworkPolicy
metadata: { name: allow-dns-egress, namespace: shop }
spec:
  podSelector: {}
  policyTypes: [Egress]
  egress:
    - to:
        - namespaceSelector:
            matchLabels: { kubernetes.io/metadata.name: kube-system }
      ports:
        - { protocol: UDP, port: 53 }
        - { protocol: TCP, port: 53 }
EOF

Apply that and name resolution comes back. The permanent lesson: the first egress rule in any namespace is always DNS. A default-deny-egress without a DNS allow is the most common self-inflicted outage in this entire subject, precisely because the error message points at names, not at policy.

Lab 4Troubleshoot — the policy that does nothingTroubleshoot

A teammate swears they applied a strict ingress policy, but the pod is still reachable from everywhere. You have the enforcing Calico cluster from Lab 1. Reproduce the report with a policy that looks right but selects the wrong pods, and build the checklist you'll run every time "my policy isn't working."

bash
kubectl -n shop delete netpol --all          # clean slate
kubectl apply -f - <<'EOF'
apiVersion: networking.k8s.io/v1
kind: NetworkPolicy
metadata: { name: lock-api, namespace: shop }
spec:
  podSelector: { matchLabels: { app: API } }   # note the capital letters
  policyTypes: [Ingress]
EOF
kubectl -n shop exec client -- wget -qO- --timeout=3 http://api:8080   # still works?!
Outputapi-ok

The policy is applied, the cluster enforces policy (you proved that in Lab 2), yet the api is wide open. Don't guess — verify which pods the policy actually selects. What single command settles it?

Reveal the diagnosis

Ask the API which pods match the selector — labels are case-sensitive, and app=API matches nothing (the pods are app=api):

bash
kubectl -n shop get pods -l app=API        # the selector as written
kubectl -n shop get pods -l app=api        # the real label
kubectl -n shop describe netpol lock-api | sed -n '/PodSelector/,/Policy Types/p'
OutputNo resources found in shop namespace. NAME READY STATUS ... api-6d9... 1/1 Running PodSelector: app=API (0 pods selected)

The selector matches zero pods, so no pod was ever isolated — the policy is real but governs nothing. This is the checklist for every "policy isn't working," in order: (1) does the CNI enforce policy at all (are you on kindnet or flannel by accident?); (2) does the podSelector match the pods you think (kubectl get pods -l <selector> — watch case and typos); (3) is the direction right (ingress vs egress); (4) for cross-namespace, is the namespace labelled; and (5) did you forget DNS. Nine times in ten it's step 1 or step 2. Fix here: app: api. Then re-test — now denied.

Lab 5Production challenge — a correct default-deny for a 3-tier appSenior challenge

Design the real thing: a namespace running frontend → api → db that starts default-deny in both directions and allows only the paths the app needs — including the DNS rule everyone forgets. Before you read the answer, list every allow you'll need and in which direction each belongs. Miss one and a tier goes dark; add one too many and you've defeated the point.

Reveal the full policy set and the reasoning

Start from default-deny-all (Ingress + Egress, podSelector: {}), then add exactly these, thinking in terms of "who initiates the connection":

  • DNS egress for everyone — the Lab 3 rule. Without it nothing resolves.
  • frontend egress → api:8080 and api ingress ← frontend:8080 — both are needed. Egress isolation on the frontend and ingress isolation on the api are independent switches; the connection has to be allowed at both ends.
  • api egress → db:5432 and db ingress ← api:5432 — same pairing one tier down.
  • ingress to frontend from wherever real traffic enters — your ingress controller's namespace (an ipBlock or a namespaceSelector on the ingress-controller namespace, Chapter 46).
yaml
# The api tier, both directions expressed as one policy:
apiVersion: networking.k8s.io/v1
kind: NetworkPolicy
metadata: { name: api-tier, namespace: shop }
spec:
  podSelector: { matchLabels: { app: api } }
  policyTypes: [Ingress, Egress]
  ingress:
    - from: [{ podSelector: { matchLabels: { app: frontend } } }]
      ports: [{ protocol: TCP, port: 8080 }]
  egress:
    - to: [{ podSelector: { matchLabels: { app: db } } }]
      ports: [{ protocol: TCP, port: 5432 }]
    - to: [{ namespaceSelector: { matchLabels: { kubernetes.io/metadata.name: kube-system } } }]
      ports: [{ protocol: UDP, port: 53 }, { protocol: TCP, port: 53 }]
EOF

The senior habit this teaches: every allowed hop is two rules — an egress on the caller and an ingress on the callee — plus a DNS egress on anything that resolves names. Write the allowlist as a table of (from-tier → to-tier : port) before you touch YAML, translate each row into its two rules, and the policy set falls out mechanically. Verify by testing one blocked path per tier, not just the happy path — a policy you've only tested with traffic that should pass is a policy you haven't tested. Clean up: kind delete cluster --name np.

Failure & troubleshootingHow this shows up in real incidents

Incident pattern · default-deny egress broke DNS cluster-wide

Symptom: minutes after a "network hardening" change, apps across a namespace throw name-resolution errors — bad address, could not resolve host, Temporary failure in name resolution — not connection errors. First cut: kubectl get netpol -A for a recently added policy with policyTypes: [Egress] and no port-53 allow. Confirm: kubectl exec a pod and try nslookup kubernetes.default — if that fails but a hard-coded pod IP still connects, it's DNS egress. Cause: egress isolation with no rule permitting UDP/TCP 53 to kube-system. Fix: add the DNS egress allow; make it a mandatory template so no default-deny ships without it.

Incident pattern · a policy meant for one app took out the namespace

Symptom: several unrelated services in one namespace go unreachable at the same instant, right after someone added a policy to lock down a single app. Cause: the policy used podSelector: {} (or a label shared far more widely than intended), so it selected every pod in the namespace and isolated them all — but only added allow rules for the one app. Confirm: kubectl describe netpol and read how many pods the selector matches. Fix: tighten the podSelector to the intended app; treat {} as "the whole namespace" and only use it deliberately for the default-deny baseline.

Incident pattern · the policy that was never enforced

Symptom: a security review (or a pentest, or an audit) finds the database reachable from pods that policy clearly forbids. Everyone insists the policy is applied — and it is. Cause: the cluster's CNI doesn't enforce NetworkPolicy (flannel-only, kindnet in a test cluster, a misconfigured plugin), so every policy has been silently inert. Confirm: apply a known-deny between two test pods and check it actually blocks; check which CNI is installed. Fix: run an enforcing CNI, and add a CI test that asserts a deny really denies — the only way to catch a silent no-op is to test the negative.

Production · security · performanceWhat matters in production

Adopt default-deny per namespace, then allowlist. The safe posture is deny-both-directions as a baseline and narrow allows on top, because additive policy can only open holes — you can reason about "what did I permit," never worrying that a stray rule silently widened access. Ship the default-deny and the DNS-egress allow together as one bundle so nobody ever applies the first without the second. Roll out policy in audit mode first if your CNI supports it (Calico's staged policies, Cilium's audit mode), so you learn what real traffic exists before you start dropping it.

Your CNI choice is a security decision, not just a networking one. It determines whether policy is enforced at all and which capabilities you get beyond the core API — L7 rules, DNS/FQDN-based egress, WireGuard encryption, and observability of allowed/denied flows are all CNI extensions (Cilium, Calico Enterprise), expressed in vendor CRDs rather than the portable networking.k8s.io/v1 object. Decide up front how much you're willing to depend on one plugin's CRDs versus the portable core.

Labels are now a security interface. Because policy keys on labels, a careless relabel or a templating bug can quietly move a pod out of a policy's scope — the pod keeps running, but its firewall vanishes. Guard the labels that policies depend on the way you'd guard the policies themselves, and consider admission controls that reject pods missing required tier labels.

Watch the scale of the datapath. Thousands of pods times many policies can mean large iptables/ipset rule sets that the CNI must recompute as endpoints churn; eBPF-based dataplanes (Cilium, Calico-eBPF) scale this far better than legacy iptables. Common mistakes: forgetting DNS egress; assuming ingress isolation limits egress (or vice versa); the AND/OR selector slip; targeting a namespace by name without the label; using policy for L7 intent it can't express; and — above all — never verifying enforcement, so a broken assumption rides silently for months.

System-design connectionWhere this reaches into architecture

NetworkPolicy is micro-segmentation and the network half of zero-trust inside the cluster: instead of one trusted flat network, every tier only reaches the tiers it must, so a compromised pod can't pivot freely. That directly shrinks blast radius — the same discipline you'll apply with AWS security groups in Chapter 51, and the intra-VPC parallel is exact: identity/label-based allow-lists (NetworkPolicy, SG-referencing) versus address-based ranges (ipBlock, CIDR/NACL). It composes with, rather than replaces, the service-mesh identity layer: NetworkPolicy is L3/4 "which pods may connect," while mesh mTLS and authorization policy (Chapter 69) add L7 "which service, which method, proven by certificate." Defense in depth uses both — network policy as the coarse, cheap, always-on floor, and the mesh for fine-grained, cryptographically-verified intent. When you design a platform, this is where you decide the tenancy model (namespace-per-team with default-deny between them), how egress to the internet and to the cloud metadata endpoint is restricted (Chapter 48), and where cluster-wide guardrails (AdminNetworkPolicy) sit above app-team policies.

InterviewInterview questions & answers

Answer each in your head first, then open it.

30-secWhat does applying a NetworkPolicy actually do?

By default every pod accepts all traffic in and out. A NetworkPolicy selects a set of pods by label and, for the directions listed in its policyTypes, flips those pods to default-deny — after which only the traffic its ingress/egress rules allow is permitted. Policies are additive, a union of allows with no deny in the core API, and they're namespaced. The object only expresses intent, though — the CNI plugin enforces it, so on a CNI that doesn't support NetworkPolicy the object is accepted and does nothing.

2-minWalk me through locking down a namespace to default-deny and then allowing one path.

I start with a default-deny policy: podSelector: {} to select every pod in the namespace, and policyTypes: [Ingress, Egress] with no rule lists, so nothing is allowed either way. That immediately isolates everything — including DNS — so my very first allow is always egress on UDP and TCP 53 to the kube-system namespace where CoreDNS runs, or the whole namespace loses name resolution and everything looks broken. Then I open real paths, remembering each hop is two rules: an egress allow on the caller and an ingress allow on the callee, because those switches are independent. For example, to let frontend reach api I add egress-to-api on the frontend policy and ingress-from-frontend on the api policy, both scoped to the port. I verify by testing a path that should be blocked, not just one that should pass, and I confirm the CNI actually enforces policy before trusting any of it. Working deny-first means every rule can only open a hole, which is much easier to reason about than trying to subtract from allow-all.

DeepIn an ingress from block, when do namespaceSelector and podSelector AND versus OR?

It depends entirely on the list structure. If a namespaceSelector and a podSelector appear together as fields of the same from list element, they combine with AND — the peer is "pods matching the podSelector that live in namespaces matching the namespaceSelector," a tight cross-namespace allow. If they appear as separate elements of the from list (each with its own leading dash), they combine with OR — "any pod in those namespaces, plus, separately, pods matching that podSelector in this policy's own namespace." The YAML difference is a single dash, and getting it wrong is the classic bug: people write the OR form expecting AND and end up allowing an entire namespace instead of one app in it. A related gotcha is that namespaceSelector matches namespace labels, so targeting a namespace by name means matching the built-in kubernetes.io/metadata.name label or labelling the namespace yourself. When a policy allows more or less than intended, the list structure and the namespace labels are the first two things I check.

ScenarioA team applied a NetworkPolicy and a working service went down. How do you debug it?

First I characterise the failure precisely, because the error tells me the layer. If it's bad address or a DNS resolution failure across many pods, my prime suspect is a new default-deny-egress with no port-53 allow — I'd confirm with kubectl get netpol -A for a recent egress policy and an in-pod nslookup, then add the DNS egress rule. If it's connection timeouts on one path, I check both ends: does the callee have an ingress policy that now excludes this caller, and does the caller have an egress policy blocking it — both must allow. I verify the policy's podSelector actually matches the intended pods with kubectl get pods -l, watching for case and typos, and for cross-namespace rules I check the target namespace's labels. I also confirm the CNI enforces policy at all. The frequent root cause is a policy that used podSelector: {} and isolated the whole namespace while only allowing one app, so unrelated services went dark together — the tell is multiple services failing at the same instant after a single change. Throughout, I lean on the error message: resolution failures point at DNS egress, timeouts point at a missing ingress/egress pair.

Follow-upIs NetworkPolicy enough for zero trust? How does it relate to a service mesh?

It's necessary but not sufficient. NetworkPolicy operates at L3/4 — IPs, ports, pod and namespace identity by label — so it answers "which pods may open a connection to which," and it's cheap, always-on, and enforced in the kernel datapath. What it can't do is prove identity cryptographically or reason about L7: it can't say "only authenticated service X may call POST /refund." A service mesh adds that layer with mTLS, giving each workload a verifiable identity, and authorization policies at the request level. Real zero-trust in a cluster uses both: NetworkPolicy as the coarse network floor that shrinks blast radius even if the mesh is bypassed, and the mesh for fine-grained, verified, L7 intent. They're complementary layers, not substitutes — and because NetworkPolicy enforcement depends on the CNI while mesh enforcement depends on sidecars or eBPF, they also fail independently, which is part of why running both is defense in depth rather than redundancy.

RetainQuick revision

The pod network is open by default: any pod can reach any pod. A NetworkPolicy (networking.k8s.io/v1, namespaced) selects pods by label and, for the directions in its policyTypes, flips them to default-deny — then only its ingress/egress allows pass. Policies are additive (a union of allows, no deny in the core API). Ingress and egress isolation are independent switches. Selectors: podSelector, namespaceSelector (matches namespace labels, including built-in kubernetes.io/metadata.name), and ipBlock; namespace+pod selectors AND within one from element and OR across separate elements. Default-deny is podSelector: {} with empty rules; the first allow you add must be DNS egress (UDP/TCP 53 to kube-system) or names stop resolving cluster-wide. Enforcement is the CNI's job — Calico and Cilium do it, kindnet and flannel-alone do not — so a policy can be accepted and silently inert. Debug order: is it enforced, does the selector match, right direction, namespace labelled, DNS allowed. Every allowed hop is two rules (caller egress + callee ingress).

The 10 things to remember

  1. Pods are non-isolated by default — reachable is the baseline until a policy selects them.
  2. Selecting a pod for a direction flips that direction to default-deny; only listed traffic then passes.
  3. Ingress and egress are independent — isolating one does nothing to the other.
  4. Policies are additive: a pure union of allows, with no deny rule in the networking.k8s.io/v1 API.
  5. Enforcement is the CNI's job; kindnet and flannel-alone don't enforce, so policies there are silently inert.
  6. namespaceSelector + podSelector AND within one from/to element, OR across separate elements.
  7. namespaceSelector matches namespace labels; target by name via kubernetes.io/metadata.name.
  8. Default-deny is podSelector: {} with empty rules — and it isolates the whole namespace, so scope carefully.
  9. The first egress rule is always DNS (UDP/TCP 53 to kube-system), or every name lookup breaks.
  10. Debug order: enforced? selector matches (kubectl get pods -l)? direction? namespace labelled? DNS allowed?
