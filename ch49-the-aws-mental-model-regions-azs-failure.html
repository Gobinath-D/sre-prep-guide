Part VI · AWS & EKS for SREs · Chapter 49

The AWS mental model: regions, AZs, failure domains

Before you touch a VPC, a load balancer, or EKS itself, you need the map underneath all of it — what actually guarantees isolation, what fails together, and who is responsible for which half of that guarantee. Get this wrong and every architecture diagram you draw afterward inherits the mistake.

Start: Beginner→→→End: Staff-level ~45 min read · AWS CLI (read-only, no cost)

By the end of this chapter you can

  • Explain exactly what a Region and an Availability Zone each guarantee about failure isolation — and what they don't.
  • Tell an AZ's name apart from its ID, and say why that distinction matters across accounts.
  • State the shared-responsibility split precisely, for at least three services at different abstraction levels.
  • Explain what an AWS account boundary buys you that IAM alone can't, and where Service Control Policies fit.
  • Read your own account's resources and spot a single-AZ deployment that's quietly one outage away from a bad day.

Why this exists · the problemBefore the VPC, the actual failure map

Every AWS diagram you'll draw from here on has boxes labelled "us-east-1" and little sub-boxes labelled "AZ-a," "AZ-b," "AZ-c." Most engineers treat those as scenery — a place resources happen to live — and move straight to security groups and subnets. That's backwards. The reason you spread anything across three AZs, the reason a single account can be both your strongest security boundary and your biggest single point of failure, and the reason "it works in us-east-1" tells you nothing about "it works in eu-west-1" — all of that comes from a small number of hard guarantees AWS makes about its own physical infrastructure. Everything else in Part VI assumes you already have this map.

This chapter is that map. Not the API calls yet — those come immediately after, once you can actually reason about what you're looking at.

Key point

A Region and an Availability Zone aren't just labels for "where" — they're the units AWS uses to make specific, contractual promises about independence. Once you know exactly what's independent from what, you can predict how a failure will (and won't) spread, without ever having watched one happen.

Mental model · analogyThe mental model: a hospital network, city by city

Real-world analogy

Picture a hospital network that operates in several cities. Each city runs its own hospital system end to end — its own patient records system, its own supply chain, its own staff scheduling — completely independent of every other city's. A records outage in one city has zero effect on any other city. That's a Region.

Within one city, the network runs several separate hospital campuses — different buildings, different plots of land, each with its own backup generators, its own water supply, its own dedicated staff. A fire or a power failure at one campus doesn't touch the others, because nothing structural is shared between them. But all the campuses in one city are connected by a dedicated, fast ambulance corridor, so a patient (or a piece of data) can move between them in minutes, not hours — which is exactly why doctors are comfortable keeping duplicate records live at two campuses at once. That's an Availability Zone.

Here's the wrinkle that catches people out: ask a doctor which campus is "the North campus" and you'll get a different building depending which hospital network you ask, because each network privately assigns its own nicknames to spread its own patient load evenly. The health ministry's official registration number for that exact physical building, though, never changes no matter who you ask. That's the difference between an AZ's name (account-specific, deliberately shuffled) and its ID (physical, stable).

The hospital's own corporate office keeps the buildings standing, the power on, and security guards at the doors — that's their job. What actually happens to a patient once they're inside is the medical team's responsibility. Neither side can do the other's job, and blaming the wrong one wastes exactly the time you don't have during an incident. That's shared responsibility.

ArchitectureA Region, three AZs, and where a failure stops

AWS's global infrastructure is a strict hierarchy: a partition (the standard commercial partition, plus separate ones for China and US government regions) contains many Regions, each Region contains multiple Availability Zones, and each AZ is itself one or more physically distinct data centers, presented to you as one logical unit. The important line is drawn at the AZ boundary: it's the smallest unit AWS designs to fail independently.

Think first: your account's describe-availability-zones shows us-east-1a as ZoneId use1-az1. A teammate's account shows us-east-1a mapped to use1-az2. Is one of you wrong?

No — this is expected, and it's exactly why the AZ ID exists. AWS deliberately randomises the mapping from AZ name to physical AZ ID independently for each account, specifically to spread customer load evenly across the real physical buildings rather than having every account's "first AZ" pile onto the same one. If you and a teammate are trying to confirm you're placing resources in the same physical AZ — for a shared VPC, a cross-account peering design, or capacity planning — comparing AZ names is meaningless; you have to compare AZ IDs, which are stable and identical no matter which account looks them up.

Internal workingThe hierarchy, and the two boundaries that actually matter

  1. Partitions sit above everything else, and you'll rarely think about them: the standard commercial partition (aws) covers most Regions, with separate partitions for China (aws-cn) and US government Regions (aws-us-gov) that don't interoperate with the standard one at all — different endpoints, different account systems, no shared resources.
  2. A Region is a fully independent deployment of AWS's services in one geographic area. It has its own control plane, its own API endpoints, and — critically — nothing replicates between Regions unless you explicitly configure it (S3 Cross-Region Replication, DynamoDB Global Tables, RDS cross-Region read replicas). A Region-wide event, while rare, stops at the Region boundary unless your own architecture spans more than one.
  3. An Availability Zone is one or more discrete data centers within a Region, each with its own power, cooling, and network connectivity, physically sited to avoid sharing a flood plain or a power grid with the others. AWS's own design goal is that a failure in one AZ — a power event, a cooling failure, a fibre cut — has no effect on its siblings.
  4. AZs in the same Region are linked by dedicated, high-throughput, low-latency private networking. That link is what makes synchronous replication across AZs realistic — an RDS Multi-AZ standby, a stretched cluster, a quorum system's second and third members — in a way that replicating across Regions, with real speed-of-light latency between cities or continents, simply isn't.
  5. AZ names are per-account and deliberately shuffled; AZ IDs are physical and universal. us-east-1a in your account and us-east-1a in another account can be, and often are, different physical buildings. use1-az1 always means the same building, for everyone. Any design decision that depends on "same physical AZ" — shared VPCs, cross-account capacity planning — has to use AZ IDs, not names.
  6. An AWS account is the hard boundary underneath all of this — its own resource namespace, its own billing, and (absent an explicit cross-account trust relationship) its own IAM universe that nothing in another account can reach into by default. AWS Organizations lets you manage many accounts centrally — consolidated billing, and Service Control Policies that set a permission ceiling no account's own IAM policies, however permissive, can exceed.
Two independent axes of blast radius

Region/AZ is about physical and infrastructure failure — power, cooling, networking, a whole facility. The account boundary is about logical and security failure — a compromised credential, a misconfigured policy, a runaway automation script. A well-designed system limits both independently: spread across AZs (and, for the truly critical, Regions) for the first, and split by account for the second. Neither one substitutes for the other — a single-account application spread across three AZs still has one IAM blast radius; a multi-account application with everything in one AZ still has one physical blast radius.

Think first: a service runs entirely within one Availability Zone, replicated across three separate AWS accounts for "extra safety." Is it well-architected against an AZ failure?

No — the accounts are solving a completely different problem. Splitting across accounts limits the blast radius of a security or configuration failure (a leaked credential in one account can't touch the others), but it does nothing about a physical AZ failure, because every one of those three accounts still has its only copy of the service sitting in the exact same building. If that AZ goes down, all three accounts go down with it, at the same instant, for the same physical reason. Fixing this needs the other axis entirely: spreading the service itself across multiple AZs within each account (or Region-wide, for the highest bar), independent of however many accounts it happens to be split across.

ComponentsThe pieces and what each one owns

  • Partition. The outermost boundary — standard commercial, China, or US government — with separate endpoints and no cross-partition resource sharing.
  • Region. A fully independent deployment of AWS services in one geography; owns its own control plane and API endpoints, with no automatic cross-Region replication of anything.
  • Availability Zone (name and ID). The smallest unit designed to fail independently; the name is account-specific and shuffled, the ID is physical and stable across accounts.
  • Local Zones and Wavelength Zones. Newer, smaller infrastructure extensions placed closer to end users or telecom networks for latency-sensitive workloads — worth knowing exist, rarely the default choice for a typical service.
  • The shared responsibility model. Not a resource, but a contract: AWS owns "of the cloud" (facilities, hardware, network infrastructure, the virtualization layer), you own "in the cloud" (guest OS and patching where applicable, network and firewall configuration, IAM, data), and exactly where that line sits shifts with how abstracted the service is.
  • AWS account. The billing, resource-namespace, and default IAM boundary — the unit everything else in this hierarchy ultimately sits inside.
  • AWS Organizations, OUs, and SCPs. Centralised management across many accounts: Organizational Units group accounts, consolidated billing simplifies payment, and Service Control Policies set a hard ceiling on what any account's own IAM can ever permit.

Data flowFollowing one design decision through the hierarchy

A team is asked to run a payments service that must survive losing any single Availability Zone, and must be organisationally isolated from the company's non-production workloads. Walk the decision through the hierarchy this chapter just laid out. First, the account question: production payments gets its own AWS account, under the company's AWS Organization, with an SCP applied to the whole production OU that blocks anything resembling disabling CloudTrail or leaving the Region regardless of what any individual engineer's IAM permissions say. That's the logical blast-radius axis settled — a mistake or a compromise in the non-production account can't reach this one. Second, the physical question, entirely separate: within that one account's VPC, resources are spread across three AZs (their real, physical IDs cross-checked, not just three differently-named ones), with the database using Multi-AZ synchronous replication over the private backbone Region-internal AZs share. Losing any one AZ — a real, physical event — leaves two-thirds of the capacity standing and a database that's already failed over automatically. Neither decision could have substituted for the other; the team needed both axes addressed, and the AWS hierarchy is what told them which knob controlled which risk.

Lab 1Build — see your Regions and AZs, names versus IDsBuild

Ask AWS directly for the AZ mapping this chapter has been describing, and see the name/ID split with your own account's real data.

bash
aws ec2 describe-availability-zones --region us-east-1 \
  --query 'AvailabilityZones[].{Name:ZoneName,Id:ZoneId,State:State}' --output table
Output--------------------------------------- | DescribeAvailabilityZones | +-------------+-----------+-----------+ | Name | Id | State | +-------------+-----------+-----------+ | us-east-1a | use1-az1 | available| | us-east-1b | use1-az2 | available| | us-east-1c | use1-az6 | available| +-------------+-----------+-----------+

Notice the Id column doesn't increment neatly (az1, az2, az6) — that's not a display bug, it's this account's particular shuffle of names to physical buildings, exactly as the earlier recall predicted. Run the same command from a second account and expect a different mapping.

No AWS account handy: this exact shuffle — non-sequential IDs behind sequential-looking names — is the whole lesson; you can take it as given and skip straight to reasoning about why an ID-based comparison is the only safe one across accounts.

Lab 2Understand — opt-in Regions, and your own instance's view of itselfUnderstand

Not every Region is available to your account by default. Then, contrast the outside view of AZs (what you just queried) with how a workload running inside EC2 discovers its own placement at runtime — and why that second path has to use IMDSv2.

bash
aws ec2 describe-regions --all-regions \
  --query 'Regions[].{Region:RegionName,Status:OptInStatus}' --output table | grep -i opt-in
Output| af-south-1 | not-opted-in | | ap-east-1 | not-opted-in | | me-south-1 | not-opted-in |

Those Regions exist and are real, but your account can't use them until you explicitly enable each one in the console or via aws account enable-region — a real, common cause of "why can't I see anything in this Region" that has nothing to do with permissions or networking. Now, from inside a running EC2 instance (not your laptop — this address only answers on an instance's own link-local network), fetch its own placement the secure way:

bash
# Run this ON an EC2 instance. IMDSv2 requires a session token first:
TOKEN=$(curl -sX PUT "http://169.254.169.254/latest/api/token" \
  -H "X-aws-ec2-metadata-token-ttl-seconds: 21600")
curl -s -H "X-aws-ec2-metadata-token: $TOKEN" \
  http://169.254.169.254/latest/meta-data/placement/availability-zone
curl -s -H "X-aws-ec2-metadata-token: $TOKEN" \
  http://169.254.169.254/latest/meta-data/placement/availability-zone-id
Outputus-east-1a use1-az1

Two calls, not one: PUT for a token, then GET with that token attached as a header. IMDSv1 allowed the plain GET with no token at all — which meant any request-forgery bug in an application (a URL fetch that follows an attacker-supplied address) could reach the metadata endpoint and pull real IAM credentials off the instance with a single unauthenticated request. IMDSv2's token step closes exactly that hole, and current AWS guidance is to enforce it exclusively via each instance's HttpTokens: required metadata option rather than leaving IMDSv1 merely "also available."

No EC2 instance handy: the two-step shape — PUT for a token, GET with it attached — combined with "IMDSv1 was one unauthenticated GET, and that's exactly the gap that got closed" is the transferable fact; treat the output above as representative.

Think first: an application on an EC2 instance has a bug that lets an attacker make it fetch an arbitrary URL on the attacker's behalf (a classic SSRF). With IMDSv2 enforced, does the attacker still learn anything from pointing that bug at 169.254.169.254?

Only that the endpoint exists — nothing useful. A single forged GET to a metadata path returns nothing without the session token, and getting that token requires a PUT request with a custom header, which most SSRF vulnerabilities (built around redirects or simple URL fetches) can't forge — many HTTP client libraries don't support attacker-controlled requests specifying an arbitrary method and header for a redirect target the way they'd support a bare GET. That gap between "can trigger a GET somewhere" and "can construct an arbitrary PUT with a specific header" is precisely the security improvement IMDSv2 exists to create. It's not an absolute guarantee against every possible SSRF variant, but it converts the common, low-effort case from "instant credential theft" to "nothing gained," which is why AWS now recommends requiring it on every instance rather than treating it as optional hardening.

Lab 3Break — find the single-AZ deploy hiding in your own accountBreak

You can't make AWS take down a real Availability Zone to test yourself — so the realistic version of this exercise is the audit an SRE actually runs: find out whether you already have a single-AZ exposure without knowing it.

bash
aws ec2 describe-instances \
  --query 'Reservations[].Instances[].Placement.AvailabilityZone' --output text \
  | tr '\t' '\n' | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn
Output 14 us-east-1a 1 us-east-1b 0 us-east-1c

Fourteen instances in one AZ, one lonely instance in a second, and nothing at all in the third. Before assuming this is fine because "it's probably just dev boxes" — what's the actual risk here, and what would you check next to size it?

Reveal the reasoning

The risk is exactly what the earlier figure showed: if us-east-1a has a bad day, roughly 93% of this account's instance capacity goes with it, and the one instance in us-east-1b is very unlikely to be a functioning standby for fourteen missing peers. Before treating this as an incident, size it properly: cross-reference against tags or names to see whether those fourteen instances are actually one production service, a pile of unrelated dev/test boxes (lower stakes, still worth flagging), or — worse — several supposedly-independent services that all happened to land in the same AZ because nobody ever set an AZ-spread requirement. Then check whether anything stateful (a database, a message queue) is single-AZ too, since that's usually the harder and more consequential piece to fix. This audit, run against a real production account, is one of the highest-value five minutes an SRE can spend, and it costs nothing to run.

Lab 4Troubleshoot — "IAM says allow" but the call is still deniedTroubleshoot

A teammate with a full-admin IAM policy in their account swears a specific API call should work, and it's still being denied with AccessDenied. Their IAM policy genuinely allows it. What's left to check?

bash
# Read-only, safe in any account that's part of an Organization:
aws organizations describe-organization 2>&1 | head -5
aws organizations list-policies --filter SERVICE_CONTROL_POLICY \
  --query 'Policies[].{Name:Name,Id:Id}' --output table 2>&1

If those commands succeed (they need to be run from, or granted visibility into, the Organization's management account, or a delegated admin), you'd next inspect the policy content attached to this account's OU. Reason through why a fully-permissive IAM policy inside the account can still lose, before opening the answer.

Reveal the mechanism

Because a Service Control Policy is a ceiling, not a grant, and it's evaluated completely independently of, and prior to, the account's own IAM. IAM answers "does this identity's policy allow the action," but the effective permission is the intersection of that answer with every SCP attached to the account, its OU, and every OU above it up to the Organization root. An SCP that denies an action outright, or an SCP written as an allow-list that simply never mentions this action, wins regardless of how permissive the account's own admin policy is — even a policy with "Action": "*", "Resource": "*" can't override an SCP. The fix is never inside the account's own IAM; it's finding which SCP, attached at which level of the OU hierarchy, is doing the blocking, and either changing it (if you're allowed to) or accepting that this account was deliberately fenced in by a decision made above it.

Lab 5Production challenge — design the account and AZ layout for a regulated serviceSenior challenge

You're asked to design the account structure and AZ strategy for a new payments service. Requirements: it must survive losing any single AZ with no manual intervention; production must be blast-radius isolated from every other workload the company runs, including other production services; and auditors need a guardrail that no individual engineer, however senior, can accidentally or deliberately weaken. Design it before opening the answer.

Reveal a defensible design

Accounts, not just IAM roles. Give payments its own AWS account, separate from the general production account and from every non-production account, all under one AWS Organization. This means a misconfiguration or a compromised credential anywhere else in the company can't reach payments' resources at all — no IAM policy inside another account can grant access across the account boundary without an explicit, auditable trust relationship this team controls.

An SCP the account's own admins can't touch. Attach a Service Control Policy at the OU level (above the individual account, so no in-account administrator can modify it) that blocks the specific dangerous actions auditors care about — disabling logging, leaving the approved Region, deleting the account's own guardrail policies. Because it's a ceiling enforced above the account, it holds even against a fully-privileged in-account identity, malicious or mistaken.

Real AZ spread, verified by ID, not assumed. Inside that one account's VPC, place compute across three AZs and confirm with describe-availability-zones that you're actually spreading across three distinct AZ IDs — not just three differently-named zones that happen to coincidentally be fine, since names alone tell you nothing across a re-created VPC or a different account. Use a database or data store with synchronous Multi-AZ replication so a lost AZ triggers automatic failover rather than a manual runbook at 3 a.m.

The two failure modes stay genuinely independent throughout this design: the account/SCP layer only ever addresses who-can-touch-what, and the AZ layer only ever addresses what-survives-a-physical-outage. A design that only did one of these would satisfy half the requirements and quietly fail the audit or the outage, respectively.

Failure & troubleshootingHow this shows up in real incidents

Incident pattern · a single-AZ deploy that shouldn't be

Symptom: an Availability Zone has a degraded event, and one specific service goes fully down while everything else in the same account keeps running fine. First cut: aws ec2 describe-instances (and the equivalent for the data layer) grouped by AZ for that service specifically. Confirm: every instance, or the only database replica, sits in the affected AZ. Cause is usually: the service was originally deployed as a quick single-AZ proof of concept and nobody circled back to spread it, or an Auto Scaling group's subnets were never configured across more than one AZ.

Incident pattern · IAM policy is correct, call still denied

Symptom: AccessDenied on a call an engineer's own IAM policy clearly permits. First cut: is this account part of an AWS Organization with SCPs attached above it? Confirm: aws organizations list-policies --filter SERVICE_CONTROL_POLICY and read the OU hierarchy this account sits under. Cause is usually: an SCP ceiling the requester didn't know existed, often added by a platform or security team without every downstream engineer being told.

Incident pattern · comparing AZs by name across accounts or Regions

Symptom: a cross-account design (a shared VPC, a peering setup, capacity planning between two teams' accounts) behaves unexpectedly — resources "in the same AZ" by name turn out to have different latency or failure characteristics than expected. First cut: compare AZ IDs, not names, for every account involved. Confirm: the same name maps to different IDs across the accounts in question. Cause is usually: a design that assumed AZ names are a stable, cross-account identifier, when only AZ IDs actually are.

Production · security · performanceWhat matters in production

Design for AZ loss as the normal case, not the exception — this is the core idea behind the Amazon Builders' Library's guidance on static stability: a system should survive losing an AZ using only capacity it already has provisioned in the surviving AZs, not capacity it plans to acquire dynamically once the failure starts. The reasoning is uncomfortable but correct: the same event that takes out an AZ often also strains the exact automated systems (scaling APIs, control planes) you'd be counting on to react to it, at the worst possible moment. Pre-provisioning each AZ to independently absorb its share of a lost peer's load, rather than relying on a scale-out reaction after the fact, is the difference between an AZ failure being a non-event and it being an incident.

Treat the account boundary as a real security control, not an org chart. Splitting workloads by account is one of the few boundaries AWS gives you that holds even against a fully-compromised identity inside another account — a leaked credential, a supply-chain compromise in a CI pipeline, a misconfigured IAM policy — none of it crosses an account line without an explicit trust relationship. Use it deliberately for your highest-stakes workloads, not just for billing convenience.

Know exactly where the shared-responsibility line sits for each service you use, because it moves. On EC2, you patch the guest OS; on RDS, AWS patches the database engine but you still own access control, schema, and data; on Lambda or Fargate, AWS owns the runtime and OS entirely and you own only your code and its configuration. Common mistakes: assuming "Multi-AZ" was configured somewhere just because the account has multiple AZs available; comparing AZ names across accounts as if they were stable; treating SCPs as something only the security team needs to know about; and discovering a Region is opt-in only after a deploy silently does nothing there.

System-design connectionWhere this reaches into architecture

Every "how many nines" conversation you'll have starts here. The Amazon Builders' Library's static-stability framing generalises well past AWS: any system that depends on a healthy control plane to react correctly during the exact failure that control plane might also be affected by is fragile in a way that's easy to miss until the one day it matters — pre-provisioned redundancy beats reactive redundancy for anything you actually can't afford to lose. The account-versus-AZ distinction is the first concrete instance, in this book, of a pattern you'll meet again and again: blast-radius containment and physical failure isolation are different problems, addressed by different mechanisms, and a mature design always names both explicitly rather than assuming one implies the other. Everything from here through the rest of Part VI — VPCs, load balancing, IAM, EKS itself — is built as a layer on top of the guarantees this chapter just made explicit.

InterviewInterview questions & answers

Answer each in your head before opening it.

30-secWhat's the difference between a Region and an Availability Zone?

A Region is a fully independent geographic deployment of AWS — its own control plane, its own APIs, nothing replicated to or from other Regions unless you configure it yourself. An Availability Zone is one or more physically distinct data centers within a Region, each with its own power, cooling, and networking, designed so a failure in one doesn't affect its siblings. AZs within a Region are linked by a private, low-latency backbone specifically so synchronous replication across them is practical, which is why "Multi-AZ" is the standard high-availability pattern within one Region, while cross-Region designs are inherently asynchronous.

2-minExplain the shared responsibility model with concrete examples across a couple of services.

AWS is responsible for security "of the cloud" — the physical facilities, the hardware, the network infrastructure, and the virtualization layer underneath everything. The customer is responsible for security "in the cloud" — how they configure and use what AWS provides. Where that line sits shifts with the service's abstraction level. On EC2, AWS secures the physical host and hypervisor, but you patch the guest operating system, configure security groups and NACLs, and manage your own IAM and data. On RDS, AWS goes further and patches the database engine itself, but you're still responsible for access control, schema design, query patterns, and what data you put in it. On Lambda or Fargate, AWS manages the entire runtime and underlying OS, and your responsibility narrows to your code, its IAM permissions, and its configuration. The practical use of this model isn't philosophical — it's knowing precisely which failures are yours to prevent and which ones you're trusting AWS's own operational practices for, for every specific service you adopt.

DeepWhat does "static stability" mean in an AZ-failure context, and why does it matter?

Static stability, as the Amazon Builders' Library frames it, means a system keeps working through the loss of an AZ using only the capacity and configuration it already has running in the surviving AZs — not capacity it plans to provision reactively once the failure is detected. The reasoning is that the same event causing the AZ failure — a regional power issue, a network disruption, a control-plane degradation — can also degrade or slow down the exact automated systems, like an Auto Scaling group calling the EC2 API, that a reactive design depends on to respond, right when you need them most and least trust them to work quickly. A statically stable design instead pre-provisions each surviving AZ to independently absorb its share of the lost AZ's load — running at, say, 150% of steady-state capacity per AZ across three AZs so that losing any one still leaves each survivor at 100%, rather than running each AZ at exactly steady-state and hoping a scale-out reaction completes fast enough. It matters because it converts an AZ failure from an active incident requiring automation (or a human) to work correctly under stress into a non-event that's already been paid for in advance.

ScenarioA senior engineer with a full-admin IAM policy in their account can't perform an action that policy clearly allows, and insists it's an AWS bug. How do you investigate?

My first move isn't to doubt their IAM policy — it's to check whether the account sits inside an AWS Organization with Service Control Policies attached above it, because that's the one thing capable of denying an action regardless of how permissive the account's own IAM is. I'd run aws organizations describe-organization and, if I have visibility, list-policies --filter SERVICE_CONTROL_POLICY to see what's attached at this account's OU and every OU above it up the tree, since SCPs are evaluated as an intersection with IAM, not layered on top of it optionally. If an SCP explicitly denies the action, or is written as an allow-list that never mentions it, that's the answer, and it's not an AWS bug — it's a deliberate guardrail, likely placed by a platform or security team, that this specific account's administrators were never meant to be able to override on their own. From there it's a conversation about whether the guardrail is correct as written, not a permissions debugging exercise inside the account.

Follow-upWhy does AWS bother randomising which AZ name maps to which physical AZ, per account?

To spread load evenly across the real physical infrastructure. If every account's "first" AZ, us-east-1a, mapped to the same physical building, that building would attract a disproportionate share of every new customer's "just pick the first one" default placement, while other physical AZs in the same Region sat comparatively underused — a self-inflicted hot-spot with no benefit to anyone. By shuffling the name-to-physical mapping independently per account, AWS ensures that "everyone's AZ-a" is actually spread across all the real buildings roughly evenly. The cost of that design is exactly what this chapter covers: names are not a valid way to compare AZs across accounts, and anyone who needs a stable, comparable identifier has to use the AZ ID instead.

RetainQuick revision

A Region is a fully independent AWS deployment with its own control plane and no automatic cross-Region replication. An Availability Zone is a physically distinct facility within a Region, designed to fail independently of its siblings, linked to them by a private backbone fast enough to make synchronous replication practical — which is why Multi-AZ, not multi-Region, is the default high-availability pattern. AZ names are shuffled per account to spread load evenly across real buildings; AZ IDs are the stable, physical, cross-account identifier — use IDs whenever "same physical AZ" actually matters. The shared responsibility model splits "of the cloud" (AWS: facilities, hardware, virtualization) from "in the cloud" (you: OS where applicable, network config, IAM, data), and the line moves with the service's abstraction level. An AWS account is a hard security and billing boundary that nothing crosses without explicit trust; AWS Organizations manages many accounts centrally, and Service Control Policies set a ceiling no in-account IAM policy can exceed. IMDSv2's token-based flow closes the SSRF-to-credential-theft path IMDSv1 left open. Blast-radius isolation (accounts) and physical failure isolation (AZs) are independent axes — a real design addresses both, explicitly.

The 10 things to remember

  1. A Region is fully independent; nothing replicates across Regions unless you explicitly configure it.
  2. An AZ is a physically distinct facility designed to fail independently, linked to its siblings by a private backbone.
  3. AZ names are shuffled per account to spread load; AZ IDs are the stable, physical, cross-account identifier.
  4. Newer Regions can be opt-in — enable them explicitly, or your account simply can't use them.
  5. Shared responsibility: AWS secures "of the cloud"; you secure "in the cloud" — and the line moves by service abstraction level.
  6. An AWS account is a hard blast-radius and billing boundary; nothing crosses it without explicit trust.
  7. AWS Organizations centralises billing and management; Service Control Policies set a ceiling no in-account IAM can exceed.
  8. IMDSv2 requires a token (PUT, then GET with it attached); IMDSv1's unauthenticated GET is the legacy SSRF risk it closes.
  9. Static stability: design each AZ to absorb a lost peer's load with capacity already provisioned, not capacity you'll request during the failure.
  10. Blast-radius isolation (accounts) and physical failure isolation (AZs) are two independent axes — address both on purpose.
