Incident management, postmortems, and the SRE operating model

Every system in this book will eventually break in a way nobody predicted. The difference between a team that gets steadily more reliable and one that relives the same outage every quarter isn't luck — it's whether they run incidents with a structure, write down what actually happened without flinching, and let the error budget decide what happens next.

Start: Beginner→→→End: Staff-level ~48 min read · Local lab (Docker/kind, Python, Redis, k6)

By the end of this chapter you can

  • Run an incident using named roles instead of everyone freelancing on the same broken system.
  • Write a blameless postmortem that finds the systemic cause, not a person to blame.
  • Tell a root cause from a contributing factor, and use a timeline to get there.
  • Define toil, explain why SRE caps it, and check whether an on-call rotation is sustainable using Google's own math.
  • Explain the error-budget model well enough to say what should happen to next week's release after a bad outage.

Why this exists · the problemThe problem incident management solves

Here's an uncomfortable fact this whole part of the book has been building toward: distributed systems fail, chaos experiments prove they fail in specific and repeatable ways, and reliability engineering exists to survive that. None of the previous eleven chapters make outages go away. At Google's scale, SRE's own numbers say roughly 70% of outages trace back to a change in a live system — a deploy, a config push, a feature flag. You cannot review your way out of that; you can only get faster and calmer at responding to it, and better at making sure the same change doesn't burn you twice.

So the real question isn't "how do we prevent incidents." It's "what happens in the fifteen minutes after the page fires, and in the week after the incident closes." Get the first part wrong and a routine blip turns into a multi-hour outage because five engineers are all SSH'd into the same box making uncoordinated changes. Get the second part wrong and you fix the symptom, skip the postmortem, and watch the identical failure page someone else in four months. Incident management is the discipline for the first problem. Postmortems and the error-budget model are the discipline for the second. Together they're what "SRE operating model" means — not a title, a set of habits.

Key point

An incident with no structure doesn't fail because the engineers are bad. It fails because everyone is simultaneously doing real, useful-looking work — reading logs, trying fixes, calling people — with nobody holding the whole picture. Structure isn't bureaucracy bolted onto an emergency; it's the thing that lets technically strong people avoid stepping on each other.

Mental model · analogyThe mental model: the fire department shows up

Google didn't invent incident response from scratch. They borrowed it, by their own account, from wildland firefighting.

Real-world analogy

A structure fire doesn't get fought by whoever arrives first improvising. The first truck's officer becomes incident commander on the spot — not the most senior firefighter necessarily, just whoever is holding command at that moment — and everyone else plugs into defined roles: a crew actually fighting the fire, someone managing the staging area and supplies, someone talking to dispatch and the public. Nobody free-lances. If a firefighter has a clever idea, it goes through command, not straight into action, because uncoordinated "help" is how firefighters die.

This is the Incident Command System (ICS), and it's built to scale: a two-person kitchen fire and a hundred-person wildfire use the same roles, just with more people per role. Google's own incident management framework is explicitly modeled on it. Swap "burning building" for "five datacenters losing traffic" and the roles map almost one for one.

In the analogyIn an SRE incident
The first officer on scene, who takes commandThe Incident Commander (IC) — usually the engineer who first responded, not necessarily the most senior person available
The crew actually pointing hoses at the fireOps lead — the only role allowed to make changes to the system during the incident
The officer managing supplies, relief crews, and the clockPlanning lead — bugs, handoffs, tracking what's diverged from normal so it can be reverted
The officer talking to dispatch and bystandersCommunications lead — status updates to stakeholders, keeps the incident doc current
The command post / staging areaThe incident channel and a live, shared incident document
Calling in a second engine companyEscalating to another team or the developer on-call
The after-action review, once the fire is outThe blameless postmortem

ArchitectureThe shape of a well-run incident, start to loop-back

Lay the roles out over time and you get a lifecycle, not a one-time checklist. It has a beginning, a middle, and — this is the part teams skip — a loop back into prevention. An incident that ends at "resolved" without closing that loop is only half handled.

Why an SRE cares

Notice where "find the root cause" sits in that loop: after mitigation, not before. Google's own guidance is blunt about priority order — stop the bleeding, restore service, then preserve evidence and dig for causes. An IC who insists on understanding everything before acting is optimizing for the postmortem instead of the users.

Internal workingRunning the roles, step by step

"Assign roles" is the model. Here's what each one actually does, in the order a real incident unfolds:

  1. Someone declares. Google's own rule of thumb: declare an incident if you need a second team, if it's customer-visible, or if it's still unsolved after an hour of focused work by one person. Declare early — a five-minute non-event that gets the framework spun up costs almost nothing; a real outage handled ad hoc for two hours before anyone declares costs you the two hours.
  2. The first responder becomes Incident Commander — not necessarily the most senior person on the call, just whoever is holding it. The IC's job is not to fix the system. It's to hold the high-level state, assign the other roles, and remove roadblocks. By default the IC holds every role nobody has been given yet.
  3. The IC delegates Ops, Comms, and Planning. Ops applies the actual fixes and is the only group touching production during the incident — this single rule is what stops the "everyone SSHs in and tries their own idea" failure mode. Comms drafts stakeholder updates on a cadence, so leadership stops pinging engineers directly for status. Planning tracks what's diverged from normal (feature flags flipped, scaling turned up, alerts silenced) so it can all be reverted later, and handles the boring load-bearing logistics — ordering the next shift, filing the bugs.
  4. A live incident document opens — editable by everyone involved simultaneously, with the most important facts at the top: current status, impact, and the next update time. This document is the single source of truth for the timeline you'll need later, and it's the thing a new person reads instead of asking "what's going on" over voice for the fifth time.
  5. Ops mitigates before anyone roots-causes. Rollback, reroute traffic, fail over, shed load — whatever stops user impact fastest, even if nobody yet knows exactly why it worked. Understanding comes after the bleeding stops.
  6. Command hands off cleanly across time zones or shifts. The outgoing IC states explicitly "you're the incident commander now, okay?" and waits for acknowledgment before leaving. An implied handoff is not a handoff — it's how incidents get quietly dropped.
  7. The incident is declared resolved once user impact stops, not once the underlying bug is fixed. Those are different milestones, and conflating them keeps people from stepping down when they should.
  8. A postmortem starts if it meets the team's pre-agreed triggers: user-visible downtime past a threshold, any data loss, an on-call engineer having to intervene by hand, a resolution time over some bar, or a monitoring gap that meant a human found the problem before the alert did.
Think first: three engineers are on the call. One is investigating logs, one is trying a config rollback, and a third — without telling anyone — decides to restart a suspicious-looking service. The restart makes things worse. What actually went wrong here?

Not the restart itself — plenty of good ideas make things worse sometimes, and that's tolerable. What went wrong is that nobody held the role of deciding which changes get made. This is exactly the "freelancing" failure Google's own incident retrospectives call out by name: well-intentioned engineers making uncoordinated changes to a live, already-degraded system. The fix isn't "be more careful." It's assigning an Ops lead as the only person allowed to change the system, so a bad idea gets caught in conversation before it reaches production, and a good idea doesn't collide with someone else's simultaneous good idea.

ComponentsThe pieces: roles, RCA technique, and the postmortem itself

Four roles, one document, and — once the incident closes — a second document that has to survive contact with people's egos.

  • Incident Commander. Holds state, assigns roles, unblocks Ops. Doesn't touch the keyboard unless nobody else can.
  • Ops lead. The only hands allowed on production. Reports findings and proposed actions up to the IC rather than acting solo.
  • Communications lead. Owns the outside world: periodic updates to stakeholders and, where relevant, customers. Keeps engineers from being interrupted by the fifth "any update?" message.
  • Planning lead. Tracks divergence from normal operation, arranges handoffs, files the bugs that would otherwise get forgotten by morning.
  • Root-cause analysis technique. Google's own postmortem guidance deliberately doesn't mandate one — teams pick what fits. Two of the most common, and worth knowing by name: 5 Whys, where you ask "why" repeatedly until you stop hitting technical causes and start hitting process or design causes ("why did the bad config ship" eventually becomes "why didn't CI catch it," which is fixable); and a timeline of contributing factors, where you lay out every event in order and look for the two or three that, removed individually, would have prevented the outage — usually more than one, which is the point.
  • The postmortem document. A written record of the incident, its impact, what was done, the contributing causes, and the follow-up actions — reviewed by other engineers before it's considered done, and shared as widely as the organization allows so the lesson doesn't stay local to one team.

Data flowFollowing one real incident

A deploy at 14:02 quietly starts serving 500s from a subset of pods. A latency alert fires at 14:06. The on-call engineer, Priya, spends six minutes confirming it's real and not a monitoring blip, then declares — it's customer-visible and she can't isolate it alone. She becomes IC by default, immediately pulls in a teammate as Ops, and opens the incident doc: impact: ~8% of checkout requests failing; status: investigating; next update: 14:25. Ops rolls back the 14:02 deploy at 14:14. Errors stop at 14:16. Priya updates the doc, tells Comms to send the "resolved, monitoring" note, and declares the incident closed at 14:30 — not because the bug is understood, only because impact has stopped, which is the correct bar.

The postmortem gets written over the next two days. The timeline shows the deploy went out without a canary stage that would normally have caught it — the canary step had been temporarily skipped three weeks earlier to hit a deadline and never restored. That's the real, systemic cause: not "the engineer who wrote the bug," but "a safety step got skipped under deadline pressure and nothing forced it back." The action item is to make the canary stage non-skippable in CI, not "review code more carefully." That distinction — a system fix versus a behavioral plea — is the entire difference between a postmortem that prevents recurrence and one that's just a diary entry.

Lab 1Build — a tiny service worth having an incident aboutBuild

A Flask "orders" service backed by Redis. It's deliberately small — the point of this lab is the incident process, not the app.

python
# app.py
from flask import Flask
import redis, time
app = Flask(__name__)
r = redis.Redis(host="redis", port=6379, socket_timeout=1)

@app.get("/health")
def health():
    r.ping()
    return {"status": "ok"}, 200

@app.get("/order/<id>")
def order(id):
    r.incr("orders_served")
    return {"order": id, "served_by": time.time()}, 200

Deploy it (any way you like — a ConfigMap-mounted script is fine for a lab), confirm /health returns 200, and treat that as your steady state. Everything below is you deliberately leaving that state and practicing how to come back.

Lab 2Understand — the living incident documentUnderstand

Before breaking anything, write the document you'll actually use. Keep it this bare — the value is in the habit, not the template's cleverness.

text
INCIDENT: orders service
Status: INVESTIGATING | MITIGATING | RESOLVED
Impact: <what users see, in one line>
IC: <name>   Ops: <name>   Comms: <name>   Planning: <name>
Next update: <time>
--- timeline (newest last) ---
14:06  alert fired: /health latency > 2s
14:08  declared. Priya = IC.
What to noticeEvery line is timestamped and short. Nobody has to interpret prose to find the current status — it's the first field. This is the same document Google's own guidance describes: messy is fine, unreadable is not.

Assign the four roles to yourself and up to three teammates (or play all four if you're solo — the point is knowing which hat you're wearing at each moment). Now go break something.

Think first: why does the incident doc track "current status" as a single field at the top, instead of just letting the timeline speak for itself?

Because the timeline answers "what happened," and a newly-arriving responder, a manager, or Comms drafting an update all need "what's true right now" without reading the whole history first. Separating current state from history is the same reason a status dashboard doesn't make you read the logs — it's a deliberate design choice for a document that gets read by stressed people under time pressure, not a formatting preference.

Lab 3Break — take out the dependency mid-flightBreak

Generate steady traffic, then pull the rug out from under it.

bash
k6 run - <<'EOF'
import http from 'k6/http';
export const options = { vus: 20, duration: '3m' };
export default function () { http.get('http://localhost:8080/order/42'); }
EOF
# 60 seconds in, from another terminal:
kubectl scale deployment redis --replicas=0
Output (k6, after the scale-down)http_req_failed.....: 74.11% ✗ 1483 ✓ 517 http_req_duration...: avg=1.02s p(95)=1.00s

Errors climb immediately. Before you look at logs: declare, using the document from Lab 2. What's your impact line? Who's IC? Investigate before reading on.

Reveal what actually happened

You scaled Redis to zero — a stand-in for "the dependency vanished." The app's r.ping() and r.incr() calls now time out against the 1-second socket timeout, so every request eventually fails, but slowly — that's why p(95) latency pins near exactly your timeout value rather than failing instantly. That number is a diagnostic in itself: a fleet of requests all failing at almost exactly the same duration is the signature of a timeout, not a crash. Mitigation here is kubectl scale deployment redis --replicas=1 — restore the dependency first, understand why it disappeared second.

Lab 4Troubleshoot — reconstruct the timeline after the factTroubleshoot

Mitigate first — scale Redis back to 1 — then do the part most teams skip under pressure: build the actual timeline from evidence, not memory.

bash
kubectl scale deployment redis --replicas=1
kubectl get events --sort-by=.lastTimestamp | tail -15
kubectl logs deploy/orders --since=10m | grep -i error | head -5

You have k6's error timestamps, kubectl get events, and the app logs. Before opening the reveal: write three timeline lines — first symptom, the triggering change, and mitigation — with real timestamps from your own terminal output.

Reveal the timeline-reconstruction habit

A correct timeline in this lab looks like: T+0:00 traffic steady, 0% errors → T+1:00 redis scaled to 0 (the change) → T+1:02 first failed /order request (the symptom, ~2s later because of the timeout) → T+1:45 redis scaled back to 1 (mitigation) → T+1:47 errors return to 0. The habit that matters: the triggering change and the first symptom are not the same timestamp, and the gap between them is diagnostic — here it's exactly your socket timeout. In a real incident, that gap is often minutes, and confusing "when we noticed" with "when it started" is how postmortems get the timeline, and therefore the cause, wrong.

Lab 5Production challenge — write it up blamelesslySenior challenge

Turn Lab 3 and 4 into an actual postmortem paragraph. Try it yourself first — one paragraph, contributing causes, one action item — before reading either example below.

Version A"Someone scaled Redis down during a live test, which is a careless thing to do without checking active traffic first. We need people to be more careful before touching shared infrastructure."
Version B"The orders service has no fallback when Redis is unreachable — every request blocks for the full socket timeout and then fails, instead of degrading. A routine scaling change on a shared dependency turned into full checkout failure because nothing isolated the app from that dependency's availability. Action item: add a circuit breaker so repeated Redis timeouts fail fast after N attempts instead of queueing behind a 1-second timeout per request, and mark Redis as a traffic-sensitive resource requiring a change-freeze check."
Reveal why B is the postmortem and A isn't

Version A blames a person and proposes "be more careful," which is not an action item — it's a wish, and it will not survive contact with the next deadline. Version B assumes good intent (someone scaled a deployment, which is an entirely normal action) and asks the systemic question: why did a normal action have an outsized blast radius? The answer — no circuit breaker, no timeout tuned for graceful degradation — is fixable in code, which is exactly what Chapter 88's bulkheads and circuit breakers are for. This is the real test of "blameless": not the tone of the writing, but whether the action item changes the system or just asks humans to be better.

Failure & troubleshootingHow this shows up in real incidents

Incident pattern · the freelancing spiral

Symptom: an outage that keeps getting worse despite (or because of) multiple engineers actively working it. First cut: ask who is currently allowed to change production — if the honest answer is "everyone on the call," that's the problem. Confirm: two or more unrelated changes landed within minutes of each other with no coordination. Cause is usually: no Incident Commander was named, so there's no single point deciding what gets tried. Fix: name an IC and an Ops lead immediately, even mid-fire — it's never too late to add structure.

Incident pattern · the postmortem nobody acts on

Symptom: the exact same failure recurs months later. First cut: pull the old postmortem — it usually already names the cause. Confirm: the action items were filed as low-priority bugs and never scheduled. Cause is usually: postmortems treated as a compliance artifact instead of backlog input with real priority. Fix: action items get the same tracking rigor as customer-reported bugs, with an owner and a date, reviewed in the same meeting cadence as the postmortem itself.

Incident pattern · alert fatigue burying the real page

Symptom: a serious incident runs 40 minutes before anyone notices, despite monitoring "covering" it. First cut: check the on-call engineer's page volume for the prior weeks. Confirm: multiple low-value, non-actionable alerts per shift trained the responder to triage slowly. Cause is usually: paging on causes instead of user-visible symptoms, or duplicate alerts for one condition. Fix: every page should be actionable and rare — Google's own target is a flat, low rate with a median near zero incidents per shift, not "acceptable because it's always been noisy."

Production · security · performanceWhat matters in production

Toil and its ceiling. Toil is manual, repetitive, automatable work tied to running a service that produces no lasting improvement and scales linearly with the system — not "work you dislike." SRE caps it at roughly half of an engineer's time on principle: past that ceiling, a team stops doing the engineering that would have prevented the next incident and starts purely reacting to them, which is a one-way ratchet toward burnout and a growing backlog of the exact fixes that would reduce toil. If your team's honest toil number is climbing, that's a leading indicator worth acting on before it shows up as a bad quarter of incidents.

On-call sustainability, with actual numbers. Google budgets on-call at up to 25% of an SRE's time, which sets a floor of about 8 engineers for a single-site 24/7 rotation with primary and secondary coverage. They also target no more than roughly two paging events per 12-hour shift, because each incident — investigation, mitigation, and the postmortem that follows — costs about 6 hours of real work on average. A rotation that blows past either number isn't a toughness problem; it's understaffed or the system is too unreliable for the team size, and both are fixable facts, not complaints to push through.

The error-budget operating model. 100% availability is the wrong target for almost everything, because users can't tell 99.999% apart from 100% — the rest of the path (their Wi-Fi, their ISP) is already less reliable than that. So the business picks an availability target, and 1 − target becomes the error budget: the unreliability you're allowed to spend. Spend it on shipping fast — canaries, phased rollouts, experiments. Overspend it, and the previously abstract conflict between "ship features" and "keep it up" gets a mechanical answer: slow down releases until the budget recovers. This is what turns a bad incident from a source of blame into a source of a concrete, non-negotiable policy decision.

Common mistakes: letting the most senior person in the room become IC by title instead of by who's actually holding context; treating "resolved" and "root-caused" as the same milestone; writing a postmortem's action items as pleas for more attention instead of changes to the system; and running on-call at a rate everyone privately knows is unsustainable because nobody's put a number on it. Every one of those is now a number or a rule you can point to instead of an argument.

System-design connectionWhere this reaches into architecture

This chapter closes Part IX by turning theory into an operating model, and it's the hinge into Part X. A system you can't mitigate quickly during an incident is a system that was designed without kill switches — feature flags, circuit breakers, a fast rollback path — and that gap only becomes visible at 2 a.m., which is the worst possible time to discover it. When you design systems in the coming chapters, "how does an IC stop the bleeding on this in under five minutes" is a real design requirement, not an afterthought: it's why cell-based architectures exist (contain the blast radius before an IC even gets involved), why the system-design method has an explicit failure-modes step, and why the error budget is the mechanism that actually enforces the trade-off between velocity and reliability you'll be asked to defend in every design review from here on.

InterviewInterview questions & answers

Answer each in your head before opening it.

30-secWhat does an Incident Commander actually do?

Holds the high-level state of the incident and structures the response — assigning Ops, Communications, and Planning roles rather than fixing the system personally. By default the IC holds any role not yet delegated. The point of the role is a single source of truth for decisions during a chaotic event, borrowed directly from the Incident Command System used in firefighting, so technically strong people don't collide by all acting on their own read of the situation.

2-minWalk me through Google's incident management roles and when you'd declare an incident.

Google declares an incident when any of three things is true: you need a second team, the outage is customer-visible, or it's gone unsolved for an hour of focused work. The first responder becomes Incident Commander by default and stays there until they explicitly hand off — "you're IC now, okay?" with acknowledgment, never an implied handoff. The IC delegates three roles: Ops, who is the only group allowed to make production changes, so fixes don't collide; Communications, who owns stakeholder updates on a cadence so engineers aren't interrupted for status; and Planning, who tracks what's diverged from normal so it can be reverted and handles logistics like handoffs and filing bugs. Everything gets recorded in a live, shared incident document with current status at the top and a timestamped timeline underneath. The priority order throughout is mitigate first — stop user impact — then understand root cause, and the incident is marked resolved when impact stops, not when the bug is fixed. A postmortem follows if it meets pre-agreed triggers like user-visible downtime, data loss, or manual intervention.

DeepWhy must postmortems be blameless, and how do you stop that from becoming an excuse to skip real accountability?

Blameless doesn't mean consequence-free — it means the document assumes everyone involved had good intentions and acted on the information they had, and it targets the systemic reasons that information was incomplete or the guardrail was missing, rather than the person. The reason is practical, not just kind: if postmortems assign blame, the next engineer who spots a near-miss will quietly fix it and say nothing, because surfacing it invites punishment. You lose all the free information that comes from people reporting their own mistakes. The way you keep it from becoming toothless is to judge the postmortem by its action items, not its tone: a good one changes the system — a canary stage that can't be skipped, a circuit breaker, a timeout — while a bad one asks humans to "be more careful," which is not an engineering fix and won't survive the next deadline. Accountability shows up as "did the action item land and get verified," tracked with the same rigor as a customer bug, not as who gets named in paragraph two.

ScenarioYou're IC on a Sev1 that's still unresolved after an hour, and a VP keeps calling engineers directly for updates. What do you do?

First, that's exactly Google's own second trigger for declaring an incident formally if it somehow hasn't been — unresolved after an hour is the threshold, so the structure should already be running. Concretely: I make sure a Communications lead is assigned and is the one talking to the VP, on a stated cadence — say, every 15 minutes — so engineers actively working the problem aren't interrupted mid-investigation. I don't personally drop into firefighting; my job as IC is to hold state and unblock Ops, not to also become the point of contact for leadership. If I'm short-staffed, I ask Planning to help pull in another responder rather than absorbing the comms role myself. And I periodically step back and ask whether the current approach is working or whether we should try something else — Google's own guidance calls this out explicitly, because tunnel vision under pressure is a real failure mode, not a hypothetical one.

Follow-upHow does the error-budget model change what happens after a bad incident?

The error budget is one minus your availability target, and it converts a values argument — "should we ship fast or be careful" — into a number both sides already agreed to in advance. If an incident burns a meaningful chunk of the quarter's budget, the pre-agreed policy kicks in: slow down releases, add more testing or staged rollout, until the budget recovers. Nobody has to argue about it in the moment, because the rule was set before anyone was emotionally invested in shipping the next feature. It also reframes the incident itself — it's not purely bad news, it's an expected cost of moving fast that you've now spent, the same way a budget line item gets spent. That's a very different conversation than "whose fault was this," and it's the mechanism that actually makes blameless postmortems sustainable at an organizational level, not just a nice tone in a document.

RetainQuick revision

Incidents fail when nobody is holding the whole picture — the fix is named roles borrowed from the Incident Command System: an IC who holds state, an Ops lead who is the only one touching production, Communications who shields responders from status requests, and Planning who tracks the mess so it can be reverted. Mitigate before you root-cause; resolved means impact stopped, not that the bug is understood. A postmortem is judged by whether its action items change the system, not by its tone — blameless means targeting systemic causes, not skipping accountability. Toil is capped near 50% and on-call has real sustainability math behind it: roughly 25% of time, an 8-person floor for a 24/7 rotation, about two pages per shift as the ceiling. The error budget — one minus your availability target — is the mechanism that turns "should we slow down" from an argument into a pre-agreed policy.

The 10 things to remember

  1. Most outages trace back to a change, not a mystery — expect to spend more time responding well than preventing perfectly.
  2. Structure isn't bureaucracy: it's what stops technically strong people from colliding during a real incident.
  3. Roles, borrowed from firefighting's Incident Command System: IC (holds state), Ops (only one touching prod), Comms, Planning.
  4. Declare early — a second team needed, customer-visible impact, or unsolved after an hour are Google's own triggers.
  5. Mitigate first, root-cause second. "Resolved" means user impact stopped, not that the bug is fixed.
  6. Handoffs must be explicit and acknowledged — an implied handoff is how incidents get dropped.
  7. A postmortem's worth is its action items changing the system, not its tone — "be more careful" isn't a fix.
  8. Toil is manual, repetitive, automatable, no-lasting-value work, capped near 50% of an SRE's time by design.
  9. On-call sustainability has real numbers: ~25% of time, ~8-person floor for 24/7, ~2 pages per 12-hour shift.
  10. The error budget (1 − availability target) turns "should we slow down after this" into a pre-agreed policy, not a fight.
